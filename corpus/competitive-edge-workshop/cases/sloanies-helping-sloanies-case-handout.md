### SLOANIES HELPING SLOANIES

What do we owe one another in a high-performance community?

### The Evaluation

It was 11:07 p.m. on a Thursday night in December. Peer Evaluations for the first-semester Strategy course were due in less than an hour. Michael stared at his laptop. Three names appeared on the screen, each accompanied by a rating scale (see Appendix A) and a box for qualitative comments. The instructions were simple:

Peer Evaluations should reflect each team member’s contribution to the team’s success and completion of assignments in this course. Thoughtful and honest evaluations are an important component of leadership development and accountability in this course and at MIT Sloan. Peer Evaluations will be read by the instructor only.

Everyone knew that the Peer Evaluations would directly influence their final grade in the Strategy course. Course grades, in turn, were used in the selection process for student awards given at the end of the year.  Michael’s cursor hovered over Daniel’s name—he wasn’t sure what to do about his evaluation.

#### The Team

Michael, Elena, Priya, and Daniel had been assigned to the same MBA team, working together on assignments, projects, and presentations across their courses. Their first Strategy assignment earned them one of the highest grades in their section—they were off to a good start.

Michael, a former US military officer, was highly dependable and calm under pressure. He often found himself mediating disagreements among classmates.

Elena, a former management consultant from Mexico, was known for her organization, direct communication style, and high standards.

Priya, a former product manager from Singapore was thoughtful, analytical, and respected by her peers for the quality of her work.

Daniel, a former investment banking analyst from Chicago, was energetic and efficient. He also held leadership roles in two prominent career-related student organizations at Sloan.

#### Plummeting Participation

As recruiting intensified during the semester, Daniel’s involvement with the team began to decline—he missed a required team meeting and was late in submitting his portion of an assignment. At the next team meeting, Daniel apologized: “Investment banking recruiting is getting pretty intense right now. I may need some help over the next few weeks.” Elena responded immediately: “Don’t worry about it. We’ve got you.” The others agreed. Everyone knew how demanding investment banking recruiting could be.

Over the following weeks, however, Daniel’s involvement became more problematic—not showing up to meetings and failing to submit his work on time. His teammates quietly reassigned portions of his work to avoid missing deadlines. Nobody brought up the issue directly; nobody suggested consulting their Strategy Teaching Assistant or Professor to get some guidance on how to deal with the unbalanced workload on their team.

#### Evaluating the Options

After one particularly frustrating week, Elena suggested that someone should speak directly with Daniel. Priya hesitated: “I’m not sure that’s the best approach,” she said. “Maybe he’s dealing with something we don’t know about.” Elena looked surprised: “But if we don’t tell him, how will he know this is a problem?” Priya paused: “I don’t feel comfortable confronting him like this.” Michael understood both perspectives. The group ultimately decided to wait and see if the situation improved on its own. It did not.

Checking In

#### The team’s final Strategy project accounted for nearly half of the course grade and was due in two weeks. Elena decided to talk with Daniel, so she asked him to meet for coffee. She explained that the workload was unbalanced. “We’re all under pressure,” she said. “But we’re carrying a lot of your work.” Daniel listened carefully. “I know,” he replied. “I’m sorry. Recruiting has been brutal. I’ll do better.” Elena left feeling optimistic that Daniel would step up, but little changed.

#### The Final Strategy Project

Michael, Elena, and Priya spent long evenings conducting analyses, preparing slides, and rehearsing their presentation. The night before it was due, Daniel sent several slides and a list of suggested edits. Some of the recommendations improved the presentation. Others were never used. After the in-class presentation, Daniel congratulated the team on a job well done. Everyone smiled. No one mentioned the imbalance that had characterized much of the semester. The team received an A- on the final project. The only thing left to do in the Strategy course was to submit the Peer Evaluations.

#### New Information

The evening before Peer Evaluations were due, Michael received a phone call from Daniel. The conversation lasted nearly thirty minutes. During the call, Daniel revealed several things he had not previously shared with his teammates. First, his father had experienced a serious medical emergency earlier in the semester. Daniel had made several unplanned trips home while trying to keep up with recruiting and coursework. Second, after weeks of interviews, he had recently learned that he did not receive the offer he had been pursuing. Finally, he acknowledged the strain his absence had placed on the team. “I know I wasn’t the teammate I wanted to be,” he said. “Honestly, I’m disappointed in myself. I’ve learned a lot from this semester.” Before ending the call, Daniel added: “I hope you and the others will consider the whole picture when you fill out the Peer Evaluations.”

#### The Discussion

The next morning, the Michael, Elena, and Priya met to discuss what Daniel had shared with Michael. Elena spoke first: “The evaluation is supposed to reflect contribution. Daniel didn’t contribute equally. If we ignore that, then the system means nothing.” Priya nodded: “It’s true: we did the majority of the work.” Michael hesitated: “Yes, but we also kept saying we’d cover for him. We never told him there would be consequences.” Elena shook her head: “Are you saying that his behavior is our fault?” “No,” Michael replied. “I’m asking whether we share some responsibility for how this happened.” The room fell silent.

Priya spoke up: “To be honest, I think I do.” Elena looked at her: “What do you mean?” Priya thought for a moment: “I’ve been frustrated about this for months. But confronting someone directly doesn’t come naturally to me. It makes me very uncomfortable.” “Well,” Elena said, “I did say something to him once—and nothing changed!” “Yes, but I wonder whether we failed him by not giving him clearer feedback sooner,” said Daniel. “Also, there was that other stuff going on in his life.” “Sure,” said Elena, “but waiting until now to share what was going on feels a little suspect—he should have spoken up sooner.”

Michael could see all the angles. Sloan’s values emphasized excellence, openness, respect, belonging, and community. Holding Daniel accountable felt important to maintaining high standards at Sloan and so did recognizing circumstances that they had not fully understood until last night. Michael also knew that part of developing his leadership skills was to have a frank conversation with his peers when necessary—and he failed to do this. He certainly would want to be given feedback if he was falling short of his teammates’ expectations. The Peer Evaluations were due at midnight. No one outside the team would ever know how any individual had voted. The three teammates had less than an hour remaining to make their individual decisions.

Case Discussion Questions

- What do you see as the main issues in this scenario?

- What could the team have done prior to this point in this semester to avoid the situation they were in?

- How would you evaluate Daniel’s contribution?

- How might cultural norms and communication styles impact team dynamics?

- What do members of a high-performance community owe one another?
