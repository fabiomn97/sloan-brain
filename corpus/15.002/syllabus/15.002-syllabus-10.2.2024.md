15.002 Leadership Challenges for an Inclusive World

1 unit, P/D/F

At MIT Sloan, our mission is to nurture principled innovative leaders dedicated to making a positive impact on the world. Recent global events have underscored the pressing need for transformative leadership capable of addressing the challenges we face as a society.

In the course "15.002 LEAD Week: Leadership Challenges for an Inclusive World," we provide firstsemester MBA students with a core educational experience focused on fostering effective leadership. The course centers around the understanding that being a successful leader necessitates a profound comprehension of human complexities in our interconnected world.

Throughout this three-day program, students will have the unique opportunity to select from over 30 diverse workshops. These workshops are grouped under three themes that are integral to the MIT Sloan mission of developing principled innovative leaders: ethics, leadership, and DEI (Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion).

To accommodate varied schedules, many workshops will be offered twice daily, in both morning and afternoon sessions. Each day, students will attend two workshops from each theme, culminating in a total of six workshops during the course. This approach ensures a comprehensive and well-rounded learning experience, empowering students to develop the necessary skills and perspectives to become innovative leaders who drive positive change for the betterment of all.

LGO students participate in one ethics workshop on Day 3 of this course.

Components of 15.002

You will complete 15.002 by participating in six different 3-hour modules, two each day in the areas of Ethics, Leadership and Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (DEI). These modules are offered on the following days: 1. Monday, October 21 – Leadership

2. Tuesday, October 22 – DEI

3. Wednesday, October 23 – Ethics

Talking It Out: Five Conversations That Can Change a Life

Lou Bergholz, Senior Lecturer, MIT Leadership Center; Founder Edgework Consulting

In the course of our personal and professional lives, there are certain conversations that carry particular weight, because of their potential for impact. This workshop will explore five vital conversations and equip you with an expanded toolkit to navigate each of them.

In this workshop you will:

-  Define a “vital conversation”

-  Identify the elements of conversation that have the most impact on outcomes

-  Examine five vital conversations, how to identify when they are happening, and how to support the conversation towards a meaningful and powerful outcome

-  Apply this conversation framework to important conversations in your work and personal life

Leadership and the Inner Critic: An Experiential Leadership Lab

Daena Giardella, Senior Lecturer, MIT Sloan, Faculty Affiliate MIT Leadership Center, Organizational Leadership, Teams, and Communication Consultant, and Executive Leadership Coach

Our ability to be effective in our “outer” negotiations or conversations with other stakeholders depends greatly on our ability to adeptly manage and navigate our own “Internal Negotiation” with our Inner Critic. This capability is crucial for leaders who wish to be skillful influencers —especially during difficult conversations. Managing one’s internal negotiation is one of the key aspects of emotional self-regulation in leadership, which is the ability to regulate one’s emotions and reactions as we respond to situations throughout the day when we are faced with stress, conflict, difficult conversations, disappointments, etc. Emotional self- regulation is the ability to respond spontaneously with ease and relational awareness when needed as well as the ability to avoid counterproductive, impulsive, or reflexive reactions.

Internally and externally conflictive situations that take us out of our comfort zone can often stir up our Inner Critic, which is defined as “Negative Self-Talk”— toxic, self-condemning thoughts, insecurities, beliefs, and emotions, which cause self-doubt and diminish our confidence, freedom to take risks, and trust in our choices. It sabotages our ability to be spontaneous in the moment, creative, and natural.

Sometimes our Inner Critic messages arise from internalized unconscious bias, negative stereotypes, conditioned family or peer group messages, or social prejudices that affect how we feel about ourselves. Many times, one’s Inner Critic messages translate into a feeling of “Imposter Syndrome,” which can cause us to doubt our achievements, question our intelligence in comparison to others, or secretly worry that we could be exposed as a “fraud” who doesn’t belong where we are. The skillful leader is aware of this universal inner obstacle and has reliable methods for successfully managing it in oneself and helping others manage it as well. This awareness is a crucial dimension of our “emotional intelligence” skill set as a leader that helps cultivate psychological safety, individual development, creativity, and optimal execution in a team.

The concept of the Inner Critic as well as its impact on leadership and team communication will be explored. Students will learn how to identify this experience and manage it successfully with a specific set of skills and self-reflection practices. We will also discuss how understanding the universality of the Inner Critic can help leaders and team members be more effective and strategic as they collaborate, communicate, negotiate, mentor, and influence others.

Included in this workshop:

-  How does the Inner Critic impact our Leadership capabilities?

-  How can managers help team members who might be experiencing Impostor Syndrome?

Listening to Lead: Making a Difference Through Transformative Listening

Andy Cahill, Founder and Lead Coach of The Mindful Creative; Associate Partner at KONU; MIT Leadership Center Executive Coach

Have you ever felt that moment when someone really “gets it?” When what you’re communicating and what they’re receiving are totally in sync? It is an energizing and inspiring experience that deepens trust, heightens collaboration, and unlocks latent creative potential. On the flip side, we’ve all had those moments where it seems like no matter what we say, we can’t find alignment. Somehow, as the volume of communication increases, the quality of outcomes only gets worse.

Successful, sustainable teams in every arena - sports, theater, military, corporate, startup, etc – all share certain qualities of trust, collaboration, and a seemingly effortless exchange of power, creativity, and complimentary skills. Perhaps the single difference that makes the biggest difference in their success is their capacity to be “in tune” with each other. A kind of collective “flow state” that minimizes friction and short-circuits interpersonal drama. Great leaders recognize how valuable this cohesion is. They work to unlock it by modeling a listening practice that simultaneously empowers each unique individual while also encouraging everyone to understand and contribute to the bigger picture.

In this 3-hour workshop, we’ll unpack the fundamentals of deep listening, exploring what it looks and feels like to move from a “transactional” to a “transformational” listening stance. We’ll also spend some time exploring how these listening fundamentals can translate into the practice of leading teams and organizations. This workshop is for you if:

-  You are uninspired by “command and control” approaches to leadership – or at least ready to sample different approaches.

-  You have an intuition that people have more potential than they realize, but you’re not sure how to “tap into it.”

-  You’ve had a prior experience of deep listening – for instance with a great mentor, manager, or professional coach or therapist – and you want to practice that more intentionally.

-  You are willing to adopt a learning mindset and try unconventional experiences to support your learning & growth.

Unlock the secrets to achieving extraordinary performance by mastering the mental skills used by the world's top athletes, executives, and performers. As a leading mental performance consultant for professional athletes in the NFL, NBA, MLB, and MLS and mo st recently the Chief of Staff for the Washington Commanders, join me for an interactive and insightful workshop that will provide you with practical tools and strategies to elevate your performance during your time at Sloan or any field you might

In this workshop, we will explore six foundational mental skills that consistently make a difference at the highest levels of performance. Through engaging discussions, hands-on activities, and real-world examples, you will learn how to apply these skills to your own pursuits, ensuring you are prepared to excel in both your professional and personal life.

Motivation: Imagine a marathon runner at the 20-mile mark, feeling exhausted and ready to give up. It's their internal motivation that pushes them to continue, envisioning the finish line and a sense of accomplishment. Achieving fairness and equity via analytics

Prof. Ray Reagans and Prof. Jordan Levine Location: E51-325

Our students are tasked with leading in a world where people are increasingly aware of the implications of systemic processes that favor certain groups while disadvantaging others. The goal is clear: to create a more just and equitable organization. However, there are challenges in both defining this objective and how to achieve this objective. This session aims to address the "how" by equipping learners with a set of analytical methods that can uncover hidden effects of interdependent choices and decisions. We will explore how to leverage this understanding to generate more equitable outcomes.

The session will be case-based, with cases selected to represent a variety of equity challenges. Through these cases, students will gain a deeper understanding of how to detect issues and transparently create more equitable outcomes. Ultimately, the goal is to equip our students with the tools and knowledge needed to lead organizations toward greater justice and equity.

Core Ethics: Business Decisions and Ethical Dilemmas Robert Pindyck, Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi Ltd Professor in Finance and Economics, Professor, Applied Economics

What determines if a particular business decision or practice is ethical? We will examine several decisions and practices that raise questions of ethics but have no clear answers and discuss how these questions might be resolved. We will first examine the very notion of “ethical behavior.” Can we agree on a set of criteria, or at least rough guidelines, which can be used to decide whether a particular action is ethical?

We will then turn to several business-related “case studies.” First, we will discuss the development and marketing of “light cigarettes” by the tobacco industry during the late 1970s and 1980s. These cigarettes were promoted as being safer than regular cigarettes because (according to industry claims) they were lower in tar. By 2004, at which point the market share of “light” cigarettes reached 83%, studies showed that in fact they delivered the same amount of tar. In 2009 anti-smoking legislation made it illegal to use descriptors such as “light” or “low- tar,” but tobacco companies got around this by color-coding their products. Was it ethical to work on the promotion of “light” cigarettes? Would it be ethical today to take a job in the marketing department of a tobacco company?

Second, we will turn to the issuance of credit cards. Research has shown that card issuers target less educated customers with more steeply back-loaded fees (e.g., lower introductory APRs but higher default APRs, late fees, and over-limit fees) compared to offers made to better- educated customers. Why? Because less educated customers are less able to anticipate onerous or hidden fees. Is the issuer taking advantage of less-educated customers’ lack of financial sophistication, and if so, is this practice ethical? Time permitting, we will examine some of the practices of financial advisors, such as claiming that by choosing stocks for your portfolio, they can out-perform broad index funds. (Considerable research has shown that such claims are usually unwarranted.)
