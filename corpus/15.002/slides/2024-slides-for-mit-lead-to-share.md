What are microaggressions?

-  Microaggressions are seemingly innocuous, social-identity-threatening acts or statements predominantly communicated by a dominant group member and directed toward a marginalized group member (Smith & Griffiths, 2022; Sue et al., 2007)
- Subtly convey negative sentiments about a marginalized target’s social identity (e.g., race, gender, and sexual orientation) (Ashforth & Mael, 1989; Edelman, Fuller, & Mara-Drita, 2001; Tajfel & Turner, 1986), regardless of perpetrator intent (Smith & Griffiths, 2022; Williams, 2020)
- Microaggressions at work are a form of relational threat
- Result in a relational fracture, or an injury to the relationship characterized by negative relational feelings and a sense that one’s expectations around how one should be treated in the relationship have been violated (Olekalns et al., 2020: 4)

Jackson, S.R., & B. Tewfik. (Forthcoming). “From Deterioration to Adaptation: Illuminating the Range of Relational Outcomes Following a


Workplace Microaggression.” Academy of Management Review

Decision-Making Biases – What can we do about them?

Bias Definition Solution

Self-serving Bias Desire to make decisions that maintains selfesteem

Analyze who gains or loses from your decision

Affect Heuristic Tendency to make decisions based on emotional reaction

Check for strong emotions, lay out evidence for decision

Saliency Bias Weighting prominent or striking items heavily when making decisions

Ask for a broader set of data; make sure sides are representative

Availability Bias Heavily weighting easily recalled examples when making a decision

Collect broader set of data behind examples; structure diverse panel

Confirmation Bias Tendency to interpret new evidence as confirmation of belief

Seek outside opinions; structure diverse panel

Groupthink Desire to conform results in irrational decisionmaking

With unanimity, seek out dissent 1 on 1 or assign a devil's advocate; structure diverse panel

Anchoring Depending too much on an initial piece of info when making a decision

Re-anchor; know where the numbers came from

Bias Definition Solution

Sunk Cost Fallacy Taking into account costs that are already spent Look forward when making new decisions

Defining inclusion & diversity Initiatives

-  Inclusion initiatives are programs designed to change employee perceptions of the importance of diversity within the organization

-  Diversity initiatives are approaches to managing diversity. They promote fair treatment of employees with different attributes, and typically involve policies addressing:

-  Non-discrimination practices: ensure that decision-making (e.g. hiring, promotions, allocating desirable assignments) is based on qualifications and abilities
- Resource practices: provide under-represented candidates with additional support and opportunities
- Accountability practices: increase responsibility for and monitoring of diversity outcomes

## Page 32

Research Implications

-  For Academia
- I identify a new source of employee resistance to DEI initiatives – repugnant market concerns – for further research
- I identify a new source of differential treatment – different candidate recruitment models, depending on the race of the candidate (i.e., transactional for White employees and developmental for racial minorities) – for further research
- For Employers
- The business case for diversity has received a lot of attention and support from employers and academics alike

-  However, my research theorizes that such a transactional approach to DEI may raise repugnant market concerns from managers and can lead them to resist implementing the DEI initiative
- While a developmental approach to DEI may overcome these concerns, it is not a panacea as it may not yield candidates with the appropriate tenure levels

-  For DEI Vendors
- Employers are increasingly interested in partnering with DEI vendors to solve their workforce diversity problems. However, how the product and approach is framed has important implications for whether or not the DEI solution will be adopted

-  A developmental approach (emphasizing individuality, equity, community, and commitment) will be more amenable to employers who espouse a moral case for diversity
- A transactional approach (emphasizing speed, quantity, efficiency, and compensation) will be more amenable to employers who espouse a business case for diversity
- However, vendors should ensure their products address inequality at all levels of the organizational hierarchy or they risk reinforcing inequality by concentrating diversity at the lower levels
