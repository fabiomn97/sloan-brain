Overview of Tax Incentives for Renewable Energy Projects

Renewable energy projects in the US qualify for several tax benefits available from the federal government

> Section #45 Production Tax Credits (PTC)

> Section #48 Investment Tax Credit (ITC)

> Bonus Depreciation

Other incentive mechanisms

> Several state governments provide additional tax or cash benefits

> State mandated Renewable Portfolio Standards (“RPS”)

## Page 5

Production Tax Credit (PTC)

Generation-based incentive set as a part of the 1992 Energy Policy Act (EPA)

> Qualifying projects are eligible for a tax credit for 10-year period from the “Placed-in-Service” date

> The EPA set the rate at $0.015/KWh with inflation adjustment. The PTC rate for year 2022 was $0.026 per KWh

> The credit for certain other renewable energy technologies (e.g. open-loop biomass, small irrigation power, landfill gas, trash,

qualified hydropower, and marine and hydrokinetic facilities) is 50% of the rate applicable for wind projects

Extended through the Protecting the Americans from Tax Hike Act of 2015 (PATH Act)

> Also envisages phasing out the PTCs over the course of four years:

-  100% PTCs for the projects that begin construction on or before December 31, 2016

-  80% PTCs for the projects that begin construction on or before December 31, 2017

-  60% PTCs for the projects that begin construction on or before December 31, 2018

-  40% PTCs for the projects that begin construction on or before December 31, 2019

-  0% for projects that begin construction on or after January 1, 2020

Additional extensions through Extenders Bills

> On December 20, 2019, the Tax Extender Bill extended the beginning of construction deadline for wind projects claiming PTCs by

one year to December 31, 2020. The Act also increased the PTC rate to 60% for projects beginning construction in year 2020

> A similar extension was enacted on December 27, 2020 extending the beginning construction deadline by additional one year and

PTC rate was increased to 60% for the projects that began construction in 2021

## Page 7

PTC Qualification Criteria

PTC qualification was previously based on the “placed-in-service” criteria. However, the American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012 changed the eligibility criterion to “beginning of construction” (by the termination date) rather than “placed in service”.

The eligibility for the PTCs in the PATH Act is based on the beginning of construction. There have been several notices to clarify the definition of beginning of construction.

> The developer must have incurred at least 5% of the project cost by the end of the applicable year of qualification

> The other alternative is to have construction of significant nature undertaken by the end of the applicable year of

qualification

The IRS notice in 2017 provides the developers four years to complete the eligible project.

> However, if it takes longer than four years to complete an eligible project, it may still qualify for the PTC rate applicable

for the year in which it began construction, as long as the developer can demonstrate continuous construction or development until the project is placed in service

During the COVID pandemic, the IRS issued two separate notices essentially providing a twoyear extension to the continuity safe harbor

## Page 8

Investment Tax Credit (ITC)

ITC is an up-front, one-time credit

> Equal to 30% of the cost of the qualifying portion of the project (tax basis)

> The credit can be claimed only for “new” project in the year the project is “placed-in-service”

> Recapture Risk: if there is a change of control with respect to the interests held by the party claiming ITC, a portion of

the ITC may be subject to recapture according to a sliding scale (100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, and 20% in years 1 to 5 respectively)

Solar Projects can qualify for ITC. Other generation technologies can choose between PTC and ITC

> Wind, Geothermal, Biomass, MSW (including methane gas), hydro, marine, hydrokinetic

Solar projects have similar wind-down and IRS guidelines for the qualification similar to the continuous construction and 5% safe harbor for wind projects claiming PTCs

On December 27, 2020, the Tax Extender Bill extended the beginning of construction deadline for wind projects claiming PTCs by two years. The Act also increased the ITC rate to 26% for projects beginning construction in year 2021 and 2022

ITC in Lieu of PTCs

American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 (ARRA)

> Provided irrevocable election for certain PTC-eligible properties placed in service after December 31, 2008

PATH Act extended credits in similar phase out schedule to PTCs:

> 30% ITC for projects beginning construction on or before December 31, 2016

> 24% ITC for projects beginning construction in calendar year 2017

> 18% ITC for projects beginning construction in calendar year 2018

> 12% ITC for projects beginning construction in calendar year 2019

The IRS beginning-of-construction guidance for PTC eligible properties also applies to properties claiming ITC in lieu of PTCs.

ITC in lieu of PTCs is valuable for projects that may have high capital costs (e.g., offshore wind projects) or projects with low expected generation profile (e.g. wind projects located in New York and New England)

The Tax Extenders Bills extended ITC in lieu of PTC for wind projects commensurate with the projects claiming PTCs

The Tax Absorption Problem

Renewable tax credits are available only if for-profit entities (that pay corporate income taxes) are the owners of the projects claiming the credits

Tax-exempt or non-profits may be disqualified unless investing through a taxable blocker corporation or choose to pay tax on their income.

In many project finance structures, project companies are often special-purpose entities without taxable income for many years

It is common for tax equity investors that invest in the special-purpose entity to take advantage of the ITC or PTC generated by the project owned by the entity through tax equity financing structures.

Overview of Tax Equity Market

Few developers can utilize the tax benefits offered by the federal government. Therefore, a market has developed for well-capitalized corporations with significant tax liabilities to do so through tax equity investments

> Approximately 30 active tax equity investors, mostly from the financial sector, doing the bulk of the tax equity business

- JP Morgan, US Bank, Wells Fargo, Bank of America, Bank of New York, Allianz, and State Street

## Page 24

Structural Considerations

Rev Proc 2007-65

> Initial allocation of all items other than cash 99/1 to tax investor (cash investor must have at least a 1% interest)

> Flip to 95/5% to cash investor (tax investor must have at least a 5% interest)

> No more than 25% of purchase price can be contingent “in amount or certainty of payment;” at least 20% of purchase

price paid up front

> Cash investor purchase option only at FMV

> No guaranteed return by cash investor to the tax investor.

- No production guarantees from the sponsor or affiliates of the sponsor

Additional Considerations

> For ITC transactions, the purchase option cannot be exercised before 5th anniversary of the “Placed in Service” date

due to ITC recapture risk

> Strong aversion to debt at the project level due to heightened risk of tax recapture

> Back-leverage historically more common

PAYGO Variation

> For wind projects, partnership flip transactions can be structured within the confines of Rev Proc 2007-65 so that the tax

equity investors make capital contributions as the PTCs are delivered

## Page 25

Assessment of Partnership Flip Structure

Advantages

> The structure benefits from an explicit safe-harbor, which mitigates tax risks substantially

> Developers like Partnership Flip structure

- 95% of the economics of the project reverts to them automatically once the Flip is achieved.

- Inexpensive buyout option to purchase tax equity investors’ residual interest

> Given the limited supply of tax equity, developers are only paying for tax benefits being monetized (as opposed to the

full Fair Market Value of the project)

Disadvantages

> The structure monetizes only 99% of the benefits

- Even getting to 99% is difficult due to accounting and tax complexities including capital accounts, 754(b) gain stepup, etc.

> Since most tax equity investors prefer an unlevered version of the partnership structure, up-front proceeds to the

developer are limited

## Page 26

Back-leverage Considerations

-  Debt-financing transactions secured by the sponsor’s equity interest in the project company

-  Lender is one step away from project company’s assets

-  If the loan defaults, lender can foreclose upon the sponsor’s equity in the project company and not on the project itself

Sale/Leaseback Transaction Structure

-  Once project is constructed and operational, developer sells the project to a tax equity investor (“Lessor”).

-  Lessor immediately leases the project back to the Developer (“Lessee”)

-  Lessor claims 100% of the depreciation benefits and tax credits

-  Lessee handles upkeep of the project, paying for insurance, etc.

-  At the end of the Lease term, the project reverts to the Lessor.

-  Lessee has early buy-out option at predetermined intervals at fair market value

## Page 30

Assessment of Sale/Leaseback Structure

Advantages

> Step-up in the tax basis from the cost to construct the project at Fair Market Value (determined at the time of

sale/leaseback) – typically 10-15% of the cost of the project

> Provides 100% of the financing for the developer

> Can be executed within 90 days after the project is “placed-in-service”, mitigating construction risk and any start-up

issues

> 100% of the tax benefits can be monetized without any structural complexities

Disadvantages

> Developer gives up control of the project and the buyout option is more expensive compared to those in partnership flip

structure

> Tax equity investor is invested into the project for more capital and a longer time (compared to partnership flip structure)

for essentially the same amount of tax benefits

## Page 31

Inverted Lease Structure

Inverted Lease (or Lease Pass-through structure) has gained popularity in the residential solar sector

An inverted lease is an exception to the general rule that only a tax owner may claim tax benefits

In this structure, the owner leases a project to an investor under a master lease, and the investor claims the ITC (or cash grant)

> The ITC or grant is calculated based on the project’s value.

> The lessee recognizes income equal to 50% of the ITC or grant, ratably over 5 years

Depreciation deductions may not be passed through to lessee.

The lessee then sells power to an offtaker or subleases the project to a customer.

At the end of the master lease, the project ownership reverts to the owner

## Page 33

Assessment of Inverted Lease Structure

Advantages

> Step-up in the tax basis from the cost to construct the project to Fair Market Value (determined at the time of

sale/leaseback) – typically 10-15% of the cost of the project

> Developers prefer the structure because the developers get the project back at the end of the lease term

> Tax Equity Investors prefer the structure because they can monetize only the tax credits alone, which are more valuable

than depreciation benefits

Disadvantages

> Developer cannot monetize depreciation benefits

## Page 34

Critical Assessment of Tax Credits as an Incentive Mechanism

Disadvantages

> Significant uncertainties for market participants due to multiple cycles of extensions, expiries, and reenactments

> Tax equity structures and transactions must be uniquely tailored for each project

> Tax audit risks are difficult to quantify thereby making the recourse inevitable

- Recourse transaction are difficult for private equity firms and other institutional investors

> IRS directive that the tax equity investor shall be a true equity owner, which essentially limits the universe of eligible

projects to invest in. Given the equity-like risks and lower prospects of recovery there is a limited appetite for tax equity in the investment community

> Tax equity investor base is very small and limited to a select few financial institutions

- The investor base has been growing but slowly because each investor needs to work with various constituents within the organization (accounting, tax, regulatory, etc.) and the investors need to have steady and predictable tax appetite

Advantages

> Private investors have more control deciding which projects “deserve” the subsidies

> Subsidies and their direct/indirect benefits stay in the same country
