Third-Party Owned vs Homeowner Owned Systems

- Pros of business model based on Homeowner Owned Solar Systems
- Homeowners are increasingly opting for owning a solar system outright instead of going through a

third-party-ownership channel

- Decreased cost of Solar Energy Systems making outright purchases affordable
- Consumer perception that loans are often more cost effective than third party ownership
- Increased transparency on differences among the financing options
- Increased comfort with Solar Energy System technology and performance
- Less reliance on tax equity financing, which is very constrained especially in a year when tax

reforms are under discussion
- Immediate cash realization for otherwise cash-starved solar developers

- Drawbacks of outright ownership of the solar systems
- Third-party ownership benefits from accelerated depreciation. Homeowners can’t claim

depreciation
- Ownership of solar system imposes additional paperwork for tax filings and risks related to

claiming ITC on the part of the homeowners

- What does the future has in store?
- Perhaps, a co-existing relationship much akin to the car market

## Page 15

Property Assessed Clean Energy (PACE)

- The PACE program works the same way as the Solar Loan Program but it differs in two important ways

- First, the financing is available for a broad range of clean energy projects (solar, energy efficiency,

doors, windows, roofs, central air conditioning, artificial turf)
- Second, the mortgage is paid off as a line item in property taxes

- Financing Underwriting and Terms
- Up to 25-year term
- Underwriting based on home equity value
- While PACE financing repayments are recouped through property assessments, the repayments

are not tax deductible unlike property taxes (IRS publication 503). However, interest payments for the financing can be tax deductible similar to Home Equity Line of Credit
- Homeowner can claim the ITC and any rebates offered through the state programs
- Interest rates are reported to be higher than those applicable for solar loans

How does Community Solar Work?

- A community solar project is a small-scale solar project, typically around 1 MW in size, to which customers buy in or subscribe. Projects are either ground mounted or located on large roofs. A customer owns or subscribes to a portion of the project. Customers can be residential, municipal, commercial or industrial customers

- Purchase Model: A customer makes an upfront payment to buy a panel or series of panels
- Subscription Model: A customer pays the developer for its share of the output, usually a fixed price

per kilowatt hour per month or a fixed lease payment, escalating with inflation. The customer can also prepay the developer for all of the expected output from the customer’s share of the project

- Subscription agreements are similar to residential PPAs with a term of up to 20 years. The customer agrees to pay for all the electricity produced by its portion of the project. Unlike a power purchase agreement, the customer does not receive electricity from the project. Rather, the customer is credited for the output to which it subscribes

- The electricity from the project is delivered to the local utility. The utility then credits each customer for that customer’s share of the electricity output. The customer pays its normal bill to the utility, reduced by the credit

- Community solar is predicated on virtual net metering, which allows for the project and the subscriber to be located at different places

## Page 22

Benefits of Community Solar

- Utilities benefit from community solar because they can recover their fixed costs, while promoting growth of renewable energy to meet state mandates, relieving a key tension between utilities and residential solar developers. The customers remain with the utility, and the utility usually is able to continue recovering its costs in the fixed portion of a customer’s bill

- Customers benefit due to consumer choice. Community solar allows people who do not own their buildings or have a roof on which to put solar panels to subscribe to, or own, part of a community array

- Only about 20% of residential utility customers can host solar projects. The remaining 80% may

be renters, own apartments or own homes with unsuitable roofs. Community solar programs are designed to reach this 80% and often try to reach low-income customers in particular

- Customers also do not have to worry about the complications that come from having solar panels on their roofs and what happens if a customer sells his or her house. The subscription agreement model provides flexibility in case a customer decides to move

- Community solar helps developers with economies of scale, relative to residential solar. Customer acquisition costs may be lower as the pitch to the customer is easier. Developers can also mix-and-match residential customers with C&I customers

## Page 23

Financing Complexities

- In order to receive tax benefits from a project, a tax equity investor must own the project. All three main forms of tax equity structures may be hard to use in community solar projects using the purchase model where the customer owns the panels

- Any tax equity investment would have to use a pooled structure like a master sale-leaseback with

multiple customers as separate lessees

- Community solar projects risk running afoul of securities laws if the sale of interests or subscriptions is considered a securities offering

- Purchase model is more favorable from securities law perspective

- Complex credit risk analysis
- In subscription model, customers are typically a mix of companies, municipalities and individuals
- The revolving pool of the subscribers can make certain tax equity investors and/or commercial

banks uneasy. A long waiting list can help alleviate the concerns but investors may not be certain about the credit quality as the subscriber pool evolves
- Certain states require that a certain percentage of the participants in each project be low-income

residential utility customers. Tax equity investors are accustomed to residential projects whose hosts have FICO scores above 650 or 700

Community Choice Aggregators (CCA) defined

- A non-profit, public agency set up by a municipality or a group of municipalities to serve a combined load of the residents and commercial businesses within its political boundaries

- Local utility has no longer the obligation to procure electricity for the customers served by the CCA but still provides transmission, distribution, and billing services

- The CCAs are created according to the state legislatures. The state of Massachusetts is the pioneer of the CCA model and passed a legislation in 1997 to enable formation of the Cape Light Compact to serve the towns of Cape Cod and Martha’s Vineyard

- Since the passage of the law in Massachusetts, the CCA model has proliferated in California, Illinois, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, and Rhode Island

- CCAs are not limited to renewable energy but have recently been a dominant mechanism to promote renewable energy. Several towns in the state of Massachusetts and across the country have recently aggressively embraced the CCA model. The US decision not to join the Paris Accord may actually accelerate the adoption of CCA model to promote renewable energy at grassroots level

## Page 28

Benefits of CCA

- Competitive, often significantly lower, electricity rates
- Northeast Ohio Public Energy Council (NOPEC), serves over 500,000 customers and estimated in

2016 that its customers have saved more than $215 million since its inception in 2000
- In certain markets, it is possible for consumers to get electricity at a lower rate than available from

the utility even for renewable energy

- Transition to a cleaner, more efficient energy supply

- Consumer choice, consumer protection, and local oversight
- A key feature of the CCA program is opt-out provision. Where CCAs exist, they are the default

energy provider for electricity customers in the applicable service area, meaning once a CCA begins to provide service, residents and businesses are automatically switched over from the utility to the CCA
- The lowest participation rate for opt-out programs that offer renewable energy is around 75%,

whereas the highest participation rate for opt-in programs (those that require affirmative consent for participation) is around 25%. Moreover, the national average opt-out rate is only 3% to 5%.

- Development of new power projects locally to augment contracted power, which may lead to local job creation and economic development

## Page 29

Bankability of PPAs with CCAs

- The key issue is that CCAs generally do not have a credit rating from rating agencies.  In order for a PPA to be financeable, lenders typically require the offtaker to have an investment grade credit rating, the absence of such a rating in the case of CCAs may render the PPAs from CCA unfinanceable

- In at least one recent deal, in order to overcome the absence of a credit rating for the CCAs, the lenders analyzed the metrics used by rating agencies in rating utilities and included cash sweeps that were triggered if the CCA did not meet similar metrics. As financings involving CCAs become more common, provisions of this type are likely to evolve

- In another deal, in September 2015, Recurrent Energy was able to finance the 100-megawatt

Mustang solar project in California on the basis of long-term PPAs signed with Sonoma Clean Power and Marin Clean Energy

- Also, financeability is less relevant if the CCA program allows buying green certificates and brown energy separately through short-term contracts
