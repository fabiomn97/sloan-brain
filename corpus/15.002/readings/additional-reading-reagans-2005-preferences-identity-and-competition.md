Introduction Beginning with Lazarsfeld and Merton (1954), social scientists have analyzed the association between sharing a signiﬁcant demographic characteristic and the strength of a network connection (see McPherson et al. 2001 for a review). Initial evidence indicated that sharing a characteristic could have a positive effect on tie strength, and the term homophily was created to indicate the presence of a strong tie between socially similar people. Firms have experienced two changes that have drawn more attention to the homophily research being conducted inside of organizations. Changes in the demographic composition of the U.S. labor force have created more diverse working conditions, and an increased emphasis on cooperation and communication means that individuals play a more active role in deﬁning network connections during a time when the workplace is becoming more diverse. Research has examined the extent to which individuals’ choices in making network connections are inﬂuenced by demographic characteristics.

Initial explanations for homophily emphasized the importance of individual preferences for social contact (Laumann 1966, 1973; Byrne 1969). People prefer to interact with colleagues who have lives similar to their own because they are more likely to share mutual interests and concerns. While fully recognizing the importance of individual preferences, more recent research has considered how contextual factors affect the association between sharing an attribute and tie strength. That research has established that a variety of contextual factors can affect observed homophily levels. Homophily is reduced when ﬁrm members have experience interacting with colleagues who are different from themselves (Westphal and Milton 2000) or when the ﬁrm’s culture promotes the development of cross-category relationships (Chatman et al. 1998, Ely and Thomas 2001, Polzer et al. 2002). In general, the more two individuals identify with a characteristic, the stronger their relationship. Also, individuals are more likely to identify with a shared characteristic when their numbers are small, which explains why network connections are stronger among minority members of a population (Mehra et al. 1998). In addition to producing more positive relationships, contextual factors can signiﬁcantly weaken network connections.

The available empirical evidence indicates that internal networks are characterized by homophily. Self-categorization research indicates that a person is more likely to identify with an attribute when a small number of people share it (McGuire and Padawer-Singer 1979; McGuire et al. 1979, 1978; Tafjel and Turner 1979; Turner 1987; Brewer 1991). An Asian male is more likely to identify with the attribute Asian when there are few Asians around, but he is more likely to identify with the attribute male when there are relatively few men. In each instance, his choice represents an attempt to enhance his social status. Identiﬁcation with an attribute increases the likelihood that an individual will have strong ties to people who also share the characteristic. Because minority members of a population identify with their characteristic more, network connections among minority members tend to be stronger. As the size of the social category grows, identiﬁcation declines, and category members are less likely to be connected by a strong tie.

Thus, while the empirical evidence indicates that the average association between sharing an attribute and tie strength is positive, there is also signiﬁcant variation. Contextual factors can increase or decrease the association between sharing a demographic characteristic and the strength of a network connection. That variation provides the motivation for this study, and to explain that variation, I emphasize the combined inﬂuence of the three causal mechanisms proposed by previous researchers. In particular, I maintain that tie strength is a function of three causal forces: (a) a baseline level of attraction due to being similar, (b) a positive identiﬁcation effect, and (c) a negative competition adjustment (Reagans and Burt 2002). Identiﬁcation and competition vary with the frequency of the shared attribute to deﬁne a predictable association between sharing the attribute and the strength of a network connection. The predictions are tested using network data among individuals working in a research and development ﬁrm. The organization has several features that make it an appropriate research setting. First, the organization is small. The ﬁrm employs approximately 100 people, so each individual is more likely to be aware of other ﬁrm members. Second, network connections matter. Knowledge transfer is critical to the ﬁrm’s success, and knowledge transfer is more likely when two individuals are connected by a strong tie (Reagans and McEvily 2003). Third, each individual has discretion in initiating and cultivating interpersonal connections, so demographic characteristics can affect his or her choices.

Theoretical Framework Researchers agree that sharing an attribute produces some baseline level of interpersonal attraction. That attraction could be reﬂected in the tendency for similar people to communicate with each other more frequently or for their interactions to be more emotionally involved. The baseline level of attraction could be higher for some attributes than for others (Marsden 1987). Asians could communicate with other Asians more frequently than women communicate with other women, but the magnitude of the baseline effect is thought to be stable from one situation to the next (Skvoretz 1983).

Research in sociology provides another explanation for why the ties between category members grow weaker as their numbers increase. Socially similar people compete for attention, and competition has a negative effect on tie strength (Kanter 1977a, b; Ely 1994, 1995). Competition results from a basic social process. Members of a social category are more likely to be compared, and the more frequent comparisons increase the likelihood that category members will be viewed as competitors (Festinger 1954, Merton 1968, Burt 1979) and evaluate their self-worth relative to each other (Brewer and Garner 1996). Gains to one person increase the likelihood that members of his or her social category will feel deprived, so socially similar people compete to maintain or improve their social status (Burt 1982, Chapter 5). For example, when evaluating how well a ﬁrm member is performing, people are more likely to compare him or her with colleagues who entered the organization at about the same time. The more frequent comparisons among cohort members produce tenure-based competition. Advancement by one cohort member is thought to come at the expense of other cohort members, so other members of the cohort feel like they are falling behind. This kind of category-based competition is not limited to tenure similarity. Competition is a generic process associated with being members of the same social category. The deﬁning characteristic could be race, gender, level, or even source of education.

Self-categorization research accounts for strong network connections in terms of identiﬁcation (Hogg and Turner 1985; Hogg 1992, 1993; Hogg and Hardie 1991; Hogg and Hains 1996; Grieve and Hogg 1999; Zatzick et al. 2003). The Study Population The study population is a research and development ﬁrm that provides consulting service in the area of material sciences. The ﬁrm assists clients with product design, materials selection, development and improvement of manufacturing, scientiﬁc analysis, and product performance and quality measurement. The organizational structure is ﬂat, with no formal hierarchy. Because the work is interdisciplinary, network connections are critical to the ﬁrm’s operations. Network connections ease knowledge transfer, which allows the organization to beneﬁt from the diverse experiences of ﬁrm members (Reagans and McEvily 2003). A network study was administered at the ﬁrm during the summer of 2001. At the time of the study, the ﬁrm had been operating for 15 years and employed 113 people. The network survey had a response rate of 92% and a completion rate of 84%.

The statistical model estimates the combined inﬂuence of the three causal mechanisms. Their unique contributions are unknown. The main effect for sharing an attribute is the sum of the maximum value for identiﬁcation, the minimum value for competition, and the baseline level of attraction. The negative slope associated with the predicted effect is a function of a decline in the positive identiﬁcation effect and an increase in the negative competition adjustment. The fact that the unique contribution of each mechanism is unknown is a source of causal ambiguity. For example, b3 could be a function of the declining positive identiﬁcation effect, the increasing negative competition adjustment, or some combination of the two. Causal ambiguity is a limitation of my framework. While I think that it is important to be mindful of this ambiguity, I also think that it is important to emphasize that causal ambiguity exists in two wellestablished research traditions. Even if we assume that competition has no effect on tie strength, the positive effect that previous researchers have attributed to some baseline level of attraction includes the positive identiﬁcation effect, and the positive effect that selfcategorization researchers have attributed to identiﬁcation includes the baseline level of attraction.

Network Data Network data were collected to examine how different features of network structure affect knowledge transfer (Reagans and McEvily 2003). The network instrument combined sociometric and egocentric techniques (Wasserman and Faust 1994, pp. 45–50), which increases its reliability (Fischer 1982, Burt 1997). Network data were collected by ﬁrst presenting each respondent with a roster comprising a random sample of 15 potential contacts from among the set of coworkers who had worked on the same projects as the respondent in the previous year. Respondents were asked to eliminate the names of individuals with whom they did not “share knowledge” during the previous year.

Second, each respondent was asked to list up to ﬁve contacts in response to each of the following namegenerator questions:

Think of the people who acted as a critical source of knowledge for your projects during the past year. These are people you contacted when you needed assistance with one of your projects.

Now think of the people for whom you have been a critical source of knowledge for their projects during the past year. These are the people who contacted you when they needed assistance with one of their projects.

The network instrument combines the beneﬁts of both data-collection techniques. After the contact list was compiled, each respondent was asked to describe the strength of the connection to each cited contact. Emotional closeness and communication frequency represent different dimensions of interaction intensity, but individuals in the organization studied do not distinguish emotional closeness from communication frequency. Respondents are emotionally close to contacts with whom they communicate daily and feel distant from contacts with whom they communicate less often.

Measures The ﬁrm’s HR department provided demographic data on the level of education, race, gender, tenure, and functional area. This research focuses on how social similarity deﬁned by tenure affects tie strength, for two reasons. First, because a considerable number of studies have used ﬁrm tenure as the primary indicator of social similarity (see Williams and O’Reilly 1998, Tsui and Gutek 1999), focusing on tenure makes the ﬁndings of this study more comparable with past research. Second, functional areas inﬂuence network connections, and analysis indicates that education level, race, and gender are associated with the functional areas in which people work (see Reagans et al. 2004). For example, women tend to work in one functional area, while people of color tend to work in another. Of the available demographic characteristics, ﬁrm tenure has the weakest association with functional area and is uncorrelated with the other background characteristics. The fact that tenure is orthogonal to the other characteristics increases the likelihood that the tenure effect will be independent of the effects for similarity deﬁned by education, gender, and race (Lawrence 2001).

Dyads are the units of analysis, and because the observations are dyads, there are multiple observations for each respondent and multiple observations for each focal contact. This clustering violates a key assumption in regression analysis. Error terms in the regression equation will be correlated across observations from the same source or object of a relationship. Without accounting for this nonindependence, the standard errors of the parameter estimates could be reduced artiﬁcially. There are several solutions to the problem (Simpson 2001). One solution is to introduce a ﬁxed effect for every person who sends or receives a relationship (Mizruchi and Koenig 1988, Mizruchi 1989). Following this ﬁxed-effect approach, a dummy variable is created for each person who receives or sends a relationship. Within a particular dyad, the dummy variables for the focal respondent and the focal contact are set equal to 1. The ﬁxed-effects estimation acts as a control for any unobserved heterogeneity across individuals, including, for example, any tendency for some individuals to be strategic in how they initiate and maintain network connections.

Homophily is considered by many to be a law of social relationships. The term homophily was created by Lazarsfeld and Merton (1954) to describe the presence of a relationship between similar people. Lazarsfeld and Merton were interested in a very basic question: Do people who share a demographic characteristic hold similar values and beliefs? Friendship was the critical path in the equation. Lazarsfeld and Merton reasoned that socially similar people were more likely to be friends and that friends were more likely to inﬂuence each other’s thoughts and opinions. They found that friends were similar in their opinions and behaviors, but the path from social similarity to friendship was more tenuous. There was support for homophily in some situations but less support for it in others. Instead of concluding that sharing an attribute always produced a strong tie, Lazarsfeld and Merton concluded that homophily was an empirical question.

The coefﬁcients in the statistical model provide support for the proposed framework. While the results provide support for the predicted effects, it is important to remember that the coefﬁcients represent the combined inﬂuence of three causal forces, which introduces some causal ambiguity. The causal ambiguity is particularly problematic for distinguishing the declining identiﬁcation effect from the increasing competition adjustment. The estimate for b3 could reﬂect a combination of identiﬁcation and competition or the coefﬁcient could reﬂect the impact of one mechanism and not the other. Some of the ambiguity is resolved by focusing on additional tests for competition. Those tests indicate that competition has an effect on tie strength. First, the effect associated with having the same tenure turns negative. The baseline level of attraction could explain the more frequent communication between people with the same tenure, and the identiﬁcation framework could explain why this positive effect declines. These frameworks, however, could not explain why the same-tenure effect turns negative. Second, the main effect for crowding describes how connections between people who do not have the same tenure change as crowding increases. The main effect for crowding is positive and signiﬁcant. The estimate indicates that as competition among people with the same tenure increases, an individual is more likely to interact with dissimilar colleagues, people with whom he or she competes less.

Over the last 50 years, empirical research has found considerable support for homophily (McPherson et al. 2001). That research also indicates that contextual factors affect the association between sharing an attribute and the strength of a network connection. I took the observed pattern of effects as a given and asked a different question: What if the mechanisms identiﬁed by previous researchers were combined? What if relationships are a joint product of a baseline level of attraction, identiﬁcation, and competition? And what if identiﬁcation and competition varied with the size of a social category? Sharing an attribute could then be expected to have a positive effect on the strength of a tie when similar people are the only two of their kind. That positive effect should become less positive as people of a kind become more numerous and should turn negative as similar people continue to crowd a situation. This idea was tested with network data from a small research and development ﬁrm, and I found support for the contingency framework.

The signiﬁcant amount of evidence in support of the competition adjustment raises an alternative objection to the framework I propose. The observed pattern of effects could be explained using two mechanisms, a baseline level of attraction and a negative competition adjustment. Preferences for social contact could account for b1. Adding the negative competition effect to the baseline level could account for b3 and the negative effect associated with having the same tenure. While it is possible to explain the observed pattern of effects using the baseline level of attraction and the competition adjustment, the large body of self-categorization research suggests that identiﬁcation plays a role as well. Therefore, based on that research and the evidence observed in this ﬁrm, I conclude that all three causal mechanisms shape interpersonal dynamics.

Discussion and Conclusion We do not have many laws in the social sciences. Economists have the law of diminishing returns. Organizational ecologists have density dependence.

While I found support for the framework, this research is not without limitations. In particular, the framework that I propose emphasizes the combined inﬂuence of three causal mechanisms, but I was unable to identify the unique impact of each mechanism. The causal ambiguity created by my theoretical and empirical approach is a clear limitation of this research, but as I discussed earlier, it is also a limitation of other research in this area. A direct test of the proposed framework would be possible with more detailed data. Instead of assuming that identiﬁcation and competition vary with the frequency of a shared attribute, the antecedents of these causal mechanisms and their impact on tie strength could be examined directly in future research. Despite these limitations, the argument and evidence presented here should make scholars more critical of theoretical frameworks that assume homophily as a general rule. For example, demography researchers have used the homophily assumption to explain why management and research can focus on demographic variables and avoid analyzing more proximate indicators of performance outcomes such as network variables (Pfeffer 1982, 1985). When the homophily assumption reﬂects the true situation, demographic and network variables are equivalent, so researchers conclude that the former are preferable to the latter because demographic variables are easier to observe and measure. Despite the intuitive appeal of this argument, researchers have begun to question it on empirical (Lawrence 1997) and on theoretical and practical grounds (Reagans and Zuckerman 2001, Reagans et al. 2004). Lawrence (1997) maintains that in most ﬁrms, the observed level of homophily is too low to justify using demographic variables as substitutes for network variables. The framework presented here provides one explanation of why it is problematic to assume a particular homophily level. It is difﬁcult to know how sharing an attribute will affect the strength of a network connection because the magnitude and direction of the association is contingent upon where similar people are located in the surrounding attribute distribution. For some people, sharing the attribute will produce a more positive relationship, while for others it will produce a more negative relationship. Instead of assuming that homophily is a generic feature of social ties, demography researchers would beneﬁt from considering how contextual factors shape the association between sharing an attribute and the strength of a connection. How sharing an attribute affects the strength of a network connection is not only a quality of what people have in common, it is also a quality of the social context in which similar people interact. Some situations produce dynamics that enhance the tendency for similar people to be attracted to each other, while other situations produce dynamics that work against the baseline level of attraction.
