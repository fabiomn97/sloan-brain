The Shield Framework and Planning Tool A Note from the MIT Sloan Managerial Communication Group Strategic communication comes down to a collection of choices. To persuade audiences to respond in a certain way, skilled communicators make thoughtful, well-reasoned choices about their message, channel, and delivery. They think deeply and critically about what will resonate with audiences. They prioritize clarity. The focus in this note is on message structure and message content. These two building blocks work in concert to create well-supported, easy-to-follow, compelling oral and written communications. The most effective persuasive communications build naturally and logically from introduction (hook, essential context, and recommendation) to body (supporting points and evidence) to conclusion (recap, next steps, and final statement). We’ve developed The Shield Framework and Planning Tool to help communicators organize their thoughts and outline persuasive arguments. Why The Shield? Because the framework and tool are designed to protect communicators from making poor choices. To do that, The Shield includes all the elements of effective persuasive messages and illustrates how they should naturally and logically fit together. The Shield was adapted from The Minto Pyramid, a framework developed by consultant Barbara Minto. She has described the pyramid as “a tool to help you find out what you think.” The pyramid asks communicators to carefully consider the reasoning that supports the main idea in their presentations. The Shield does the same. It also goes further and takes a more holistic approach to persuasive communications. It asks communicators to think through how they will introduce their reasoning and reinforce their message with a strong conclusion, and how all the elements will build and play off each other.

Breakdown of The Shield Components Introduction Hook and Essential Context With any communication, first impressions matter. The opening sentences of a persuasive presentation, email, memo or LinkedIn post set the stage for the content to come. Communicators should use their opening to accomplish two key objectives related to the communicator-audience relationship. First, they should make an effort to connect with the audience, grab its attention and get it to care about what’s being said or written. This is often referred to as “the hook”.  There are a variety of ways to hook audiences. Present a surprising, relevant piece of information. Share a short anecdote. Ask a question (rhetorical or not). Acknowledge how the audience feels about the communication situation, particularly if a negative bias exists. Communicators can also use this section to build trust with their audience. Trust, also known as credibility, comes from a variety of sources (e.g. title, subject matter expertise, time spent on data analysis, life experiences, career successes or project failures). It is important to consider different options and determine what will leave the strongest impression on the audience. Second, communicators should make sure the audience is familiar with the essential context of the situation. This is the information necessary to easily follow the presentation and understand its significance. Any essential context should be common knowledge, previously agreed upon and/or easily accepted by the audience. The goal here: lay the foundation for the recommendation ahead, not distract or confuse the audience with information open to debate. Also, communicators should keep this section brief and get to their recommendation quickly. After all, the audience is most curious about the recommendation, the reasoning behind it, and its potential impact on the workplace. Some questions to help you, the communicator, think through essential context:

- What challenge or opportunity sparked the recommendation and analysis to come?

- What reminders about the team, company and/or industry would be helpful?
- Why should the audience care?
- Why should the audience trust you and your recommendation? Recommendation With the stage set, it is time for the big reveal: the recommendation. Communicators should state clearly and concisely what action or decision they want their audience to take. Emphasis on clearly and concisely. A well-considered recommendation should require no more than a sentence or two to convey. And it should focus on what audience members should do. Constructing recommendations with active verbs can help with that focus. Maybe it is time to invest in new technology or restructure bonuses or increase intern salaries or start a mentorship program. Recommendations should be tangible, measurable, and doable by the audience. Tangible recommendations are clear, specific, and easily grasped. Measurable recommendations include parameters for what communicators want audience members to do (e.g. a percent increase, a time frame, a dollar amount). Doable recommendations present actions or decisions that audience members can act upon. Building on the mentorship example above, imagine a company that is struggling to retain less tenured leaders. In a presentation to the company’s top executives, one of those less tenured leaders might recommend that top executives set aside one hour each week to meet with the company’s most promising junior managers and provide career guidance. This recommendation ticks all the boxes—it is tangible, measurable, and doable. Also, recommendations should be properly scoped for the time, number of pages, or word count allotted. If a recommendation is too big or too general or both, it can be challenging to offer sufficient evidence to support it and to get the audience to accept it by the end of the communication. For a short presentation (5 to 10 minutes), short email, short memo or short post, communicators should consider different ways to narrow down an ambitious recommendation. In 5 minutes, it may be a tall order to persuade a CTO to purchase an expensive software tool for a product design team. A better scoped, narrowed down recommendation: a first step—for example, a request to set up a longer meeting for a demo of the software or a short pilot program with opportunities for team members to provide feedback on the software. After the recommendation, there should be a preview of the supporting points. This preview provides a road map for the audience so it can easily follow the communicator’s argument. Again, a sentence or two should suffice. There will be time to provide more detail in the sections ahead.

Some questions to help you, the communicator, think through recommendations:

- What action or decision should the audience take?
- Is the action tangible, measurable, doable, and reasonably scoped?
- What’s in it for the audience?
- What impact will the action or decision have on the workplace? Body Supporting Points All communicators want their audiences to get to “yes” and support their recommendation. When considered thoroughly and chosen wisely, supporting points start to build the argument for a recommendation. They provide audiences with the reasoning they crave. They explain the “why?” behind a recommendation. When necessary and/or helpful given the communication situation, supporting points also explain the “why not?” behind the status quo or an alternative option. In situations where communicators enjoy a high level of trust, a positive audience bias, and lower stakes, they often need only explain the “why?” behind their recommendation. This usually leads to a one-sided argument. In situations where communicators face a low level of trust, a negative audience bias, and/or higher stakes, they should answer the “why not?” questions that arise. This usually advances a two-sided argument. When audiences face a choice between two or more competing alternatives, a two-sided argument allows communicators to compare and contrast the options. Supporting points typically describe how one course of action will be better than another (e.g.  option A makes better use of existing infrastructure than option B, option A offers more flexibility than option B). A two-sided argument is almost always stronger and more logically rigorous because it addresses the negative aspects of a recommendation, potential audience objections and/or possible counterarguments. By talking through negatives, objections, or counterarguments, communicators get a chance to proactively diffuse them. Communicators should also be aware of the cultural norms regarding message structure. In some workplaces, a two-sided argument is a cultural norm and is expected as a part of almost every presentation. As communicators build their reasoning for either one-sided or two-sided arguments, they can turn their recommendation into a question to tease out the most relevant supporting points. Let’s imagine an organization where employees are extremely frustrated with the

Now, let’s consider an example for a two-sided argument. Imagine an organization where employees want more feedback and consider annual performance reviews insufficient, especially given the number of shorter-term projects they take on. Meeting with executives to address the issue, the head of HR recommends, “We should transition from annual to quarterly performance reviews.” This recommendation faces strong resistance from the executives. They worry about the extra time spent on three additional performance reviews. So, when the recommendation gets reframed in question form, both “Why?” and “Why not?” should be asked. That leads to “Why should we transition from annual to quarterly performance reviews?” and “Why not stay with annual performance reviews?”  Asking both questions helps the head of HR, the communicator in this example, think about why the audience might support the recommendation and why it might not. With two-sided arguments, communicators will want to use supporting points to proactively address potential objections or counterarguments. In this example, the head of HR can do that by constructing supporting points that compare the recommendation, quarterly reviews, with the status quo, annual reviews.

Supporting points provide a critical bridge from recommendation to evidence, helping the audience understand and appreciate the significance of the quantitative and qualitative data to come. When determining the number of supporting points, communicators should keep in mind the “power of three”, the complexity of their argument, and time restrictions, page limits or word counts. The “power of three”, also known as the “rule of three”, refers to the fact that people tend to remember things grouped into three. Sharing three supporting points can make both oral and written communications more memorable. One word of caution: if communicators have only two high-quality supporting points, they should not feel the need to invent a third, low-quality supporting point to reach three. Finally, the most persuasive arguments showcase rigorous analysis.  When it comes to complexity, more complex communications with larger, more broadly-scoped asks often require more supporting points. That said, communicators need to carefully consider their communication situation, specifically how much information they can cover in 5 minutes or 15 minutes or two pages or 800 words, and avoid the temptation to cram in content. In shorter presentations, for example, two supporting points may leave more time to go into greater depth with evidence. Also, if communicators find themselves with more than three strong supporting points for a shorter presentation, they may want to narrow the scope of their recommendation.

Evidence Audiences will not blindly accept supporting points. They want a better understanding of how and why communicators chose certain supporting points. So, evidence should build logically and methodically from the supporting points, making the reasoning behind the recommendation abundantly clear. To determine the evidence that most effectively backs up the claims made in the supporting points, it helps if communicators ask themselves, “What proof do I have of that?” Evidence can take many forms, including numbers, graphs, images, workplace anecdotes and personal stories. Above all, it should be accurate, credible, current, aligned with the supporting points and compelling for audience members. It should also be sourced. Let’s return to the two examples above—meeting-free Fridays and quarterly performance reviews—and add evidence for each supporting point. The amount of evidence communicators should provide depends on the communication situation. Not all supporting points require two pieces of evidence. Not all presentations leave time for two pieces of evidence for each supporting point. For choices about evidence, communicators should be rigorous but not overwhelm audience members.

The progression from recommendation to supporting points to evidence should build the audience’s confidence in the recommendation. By the time the communicator finishes presenting the evidence, the audience should have a firm grasp of the reasoning behind the recommendation and recognize the value of endorsing it. Conclusion Recap Repetition is almost always helpful in communications, especially considering audiences remember the beginning and the end of presentations most clearly. Knowing that, communicators should take the time to restate their recommendation and recap their supporting points. This brief but essential part of the conclusion makes it easier for audiences to retain and accept the argument put forward. Next Steps Communicators should prepare for success. That involves looking ahead to what’s next. To identify the best way to proceed, communicators should ask themselves, “If the audience agrees with my recommendation, what should follow?” Next steps provide the answer. Briefly state how the audience should move forward. Be careful not to re-scope or revise the ask here. Also, avoid a detailed to-do list. This is not the time or place to go into any depth about implementation. Next steps should simply and quickly outline what the follow-on task should be. It is wise to highlight one or two next steps, especially with shorter communications.  For example, if a communicator

recommends setting up a working group to build a new product business case, then the next steps might be sending around a list of potential working group members for review and asking for feedback on the list by the end of the week. Final Statement Final impressions matter, too. The most effective communications finish strong. To do that, communicators typically end with a sentence or two that convey a critical takeaway. It’s an opportunity for communicators to issue a call to action, to reinforce an important aspect of their message, to ensure audience members leave the room with a certain fact or feeling or insight top of mind. For example, with the “Meeting-Free Fridays” policy, the communicator could wrap up with this final statement: “We know we can put our time to better use. And we’re excited to unleash our company’s true potential with the help of Meeting-Free Fridays.” For the transition from annual to quarterly performance reviews, the communicator could conclude with this: “We may always have a love/hate relationship with feedback. But we love the opportunity to create a better feedback culture here and we hope everyone will embrace that opportunity.” Above all, final statements should be memorable and meaningful to the audience.

The Shield and Indirect Structure We live in an increasingly global, digitized culture. That impacts the way we communicate and influences the message choices we make. Typically, workplaces prize information delivered quickly and concisely. For that reason, this note focuses on direct message structure where communicators reveal their recommendation early on. Direct structure is particularly well suited for persuasive presentations, allowing communicators to build their argument step by step while audiences easily follow along. If communicators like, they can choose an indirect message structure where the recommendation comes during the conclusion. With The Shield framework/planning tool, indirect structure essentially flips direct structure upside down with supporting points and evidence preceding the recommendation.

For any persuasive communication, indirect structure should be used with extreme caution. The situations where indirect structure can convince an audience to take a certain action are very limited. Still, it is included here because we recognize that some workplace cultures and/or national cultures may prefer that communicators use indirect structure.

Conclusion: Be Rigorous and Think Critically

Becoming a skilled communicator takes time and experience. The more communicators develop their skills, including their critical thinking around message structure and message content, the more effective their oral and written communications. Above all, communicators should be rigorous in their preparation and ready to challenge their own ideas as they draft and revise each component. They should also be hyper aware of what their audience needs. We designed The Shield and this note to help communicators engage in critical thinking as they build out their message and, in the process, test out their logic. Rigorous, critical thinking is the core of any communication strategy. It pulls communicators away from poor choices and pushes them towards better reasoning, greater clarity, and stronger audience engagement.
