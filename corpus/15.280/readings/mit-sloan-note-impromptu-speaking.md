Notes on Impromptu Speaking (or Speaking Spontaneously) Christine Kelly Speaking spontaneously, often in response to a question, is a natural part of conversation.  At work, you are often called upon to answer questions in meetings, telephone conversations, and other encounters, typically without advance preparation.  In some cases, these questions may mean the difference between success and failure on the job.  You typically must respond on the spur of the moment, attempting to speak clearly out of your expertise about the topic at hand.  Many times such questions will be poorly phrased or will refer to an unfamiliar subject.  At those times, you will need to think on your feet, reframing the question to produce a coherent response.  The impromptus we will do in class attempt to simulate such conditions and give you practice with them.  The following tips should help you think about the impromptu question and answer situation.

1. Do not rush.  Take your time to think. Mentally, take note of your reactions to the question. What grabs you? Take your time. Show the audience you’re in the game. Enjoy it. 2. Manage the question.  Out loud, restate the question using your terms.  Then refine it, focusing it and beginning to create a context. Or, list possible opinions on the question and then choose one. 3. Tell them why they should listen to you.  What is your expertise or your authority?  It may be no more than an acknowledgement that you have an interest, or once had a related experience, or, indeed, have a degree in the subject. 4. Preview your structure.  Give the audience the comfort of knowing the structure.  Tell them the two or three points you want to make.  Remember the classic rule for any presentation: “tell ‘em what you’re going to tell them, tell them, then tell them that you told them.”  Obeying this dictum means that you must provide and follow a structure, not just ramble.  Some useful structures include the following:

- Pyramid structure, which states a position and provides several supporting points
- PREP, or “Position-Reason-Example-Position”
- Past-Present-Future, the classic chronological storyline
- Problem-Cause-Solution, a typical business speech structure
- AIDA, or Attention-Interest-Desire-Action, a good persuasive structure 5. Connect with the audience.  What's significant for the audience?  Why should they care? 6. Create a "big idea" statement. What's the point? What’s the core?  Be clear about what you want the audience to remember.  Then repeat it.  In his most famous speech, which was delivered spontaneously after he dropped his prepared remarks, Martin Luther King stated the theme, “I have a dream,” over 8 times. In doing so, he moved them to see his vision of the world and to act on it 7. Provide evidence.  Tell a story, give a fact, and cite statistics, examples and quotes that support the position you are taking. 8. Close emphatically.  The classic mistake ad-libbers make is rambling on and on, without a clear beginning, middle or the end.  Don’t go on too long.  To conclude, you might simply restate the opening line, then stop. If you follow these tips, you will be able to turn a question or impromptu speaking situation into an opportunity to shine.
