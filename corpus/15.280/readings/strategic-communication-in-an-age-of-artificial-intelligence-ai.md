Strategic Communication in the Age of Artiﬁcial Intelligence Perspectives from the MIT Sloan Managerial Communication Group At MIT, we spend time with scientists, engineers, and mathematicians, so perhaps it was inevitable that their way of thinking has inﬂuenced ours. We have come to see strategic communication much like an equation with several variables. During any interaction between a communicator and an audience, these variables combine to produce a response from the audience. When developing a strategy, communicators often make choices on the variables to either increase or decrease the likelihood that the audience responds in the way that they want. Skilled communicators know what choices to make and how to implement them eEectively depending on the audience and situation.

The rise of artiﬁcial intelligence is introducing a new set of variables in every phase of communication strategy, forcing communicators to choose whether and how to incorporate these increasingly powerful tools into their work. To inform their choices, this note explores the potential implications of artiﬁcial intelligence on strategic communication. It considers how the presence and use of AI aEects the communicator-audience relationship, the roles of both humans and AI in creating, adapting, and delivering messages to audiences, and the possible ways in which AI can better measure communication eEectiveness.

This note is organized into two main parts. Part 1 oEers a review of communication strategy. Before examining the potential impact of artiﬁcial intelligence, it is important to understand key concepts in communication strategy – the variables that work together to produce a response from the audience. Then, working from this conceptual foundation, part 2 explores how AI tools are presenting new challenges and opportunities across all the key variables in strategic communication. PART 1: A REVIEW OF COMMUNICATION STRATEGY At its most fundamental level, strategic communication focuses on the interaction between a communicator and an audience. In this interaction, a communicator sends a message to an audience, and the audience responds to the communicator. Responses can take several forms (e.g. written, verbal, nonverbal) depending on the channel of communication. Even no response can send information back to the communicator.

The Communicator-Audience Relationship If the communicator wants the audience to respond in a speciﬁc way, the communicator usually needs to develop and implement a strategy. To do so, understanding the dynamics of the communicator-audience relationship is essential. This relationship can be analyzed extensively, but at the core, it comes down to questions of identity, trust, and objectives for both the communicator and audience.

- Identity – People have multiple, often intersecting identities in the workplace that can range from demographics (e.g. gender, race) to cultural (e.g. geographic, organizational) to work skills and styles (e.g. analytical, creative). In communication situations, the identities of both the communicator and audience can aEect how an audience responds to a message. Strategic communicators therefore can think about which version(s) of themselves they emphasize in a situation. They might also anticipate the identities that their audience will bring to the interaction as well.

- Trust – The modern workplace functions largely on the strength of interdependent relationships. Central to strong relationships is trust, also known as credibility. Trust can be established in several ways, including through mutual understanding and respect, shared network and aEiliations, and consistency in words and actions. Importantly, you may think you are credible, but the real arbiter of your credibility in any given situation is the other party. Strategic communicators should consider realistically why an audience should trust them, as the answer could aEect their messaging strategy. Relatedly, a communicator’s level of trust in the audience might also inﬂuence their approach to the interaction.

- Objective – Entering an interaction with an audience, the communicator may have an objective, something speciﬁc that they want the audience to know, feel, and/or do. Like any relationship, it takes two to tango. The audience is also entering a communication situation with agency. They may have their own objectives, wants, and needs – some of which may be explicit, others implicit and very diEicult for the communicator to discern. An oft-cited concept to estimate the audience’s objectives in a situation is WIIFT, or what’s in it for them?1

Communicator Relationship Dynamic Audience Who are you? Identity Who are they? Why should you trust them? Trust Why should they trust you? What do you want? Objective What do they want?

Depending on your analysis of the communicator-audience relationship dynamic, there may be strong congruence between both the communicator and audience on the dimensions of identity, trust, and objectives. But gaps could also exist. A communicator and their audience may have diEerences in one or all three relationship dynamics, which could present barriers that the communicator must overcome to achieve their desired response. This is where messaging comes in. The Message: Creation, Adaptation, Delivery Messaging is the main event in strategic communication. Analysis of the communicatoraudience relationship provides the foundation, but it is ultimately in service of the messaging strategy. How a communicator applies their analysis through messaging is what often spells the diEerence between success and failure in a communication situation.

Fortunately, the ﬁeld of strategic communication oEers a systematic approach to messaging. It involves breaking down the message into three component parts – message creation, message adaptation, and message delivery. Along the way, communicators can make choices at each phase of the process to maximize their chances of eliciting their desired response from the audience. Let’s review each phase in turn.

Message Creation is an exercise in creativity. The communicator thinks creatively about what message or messages will resonate with the audience based on their identity, trust levels, and objectives. The building blocks of the message are content and structure.

- Content – the content is the raw material of a message. It can include a mix of logical appeals (arguments, facts, quantitative data, and qualitative data) and emotional appeals (stories, examples, metaphors, analogies, and persuasive tactics).

- Structure – if content is the raw material of a message, structure helps sculpt the message into a ﬁnal form that is clear and compelling to the audience. Communicators have a variety of structures to choose from, but two of the most

## Page 5

useful are direct structure (main point, followed by subsidiary points) and indirect structure (subsidiary points followed by main point). Message Adaptation is the process of adapting the message a communicator creates to the speciﬁc context of a communication situation. The context most often includes both the channel of communication and the underlying cultural norms, values, and languages that shape the situation.

- Channel – In-person, virtual, email, messaging apps, social media, and virtual reality are just some of the available channels that leaders and organizations are using today to communicate. Channel adaptation is important because the same message may look and sound diEerent when communicated in a 10-minute in-person presentation versus a text-only post on LinkedIn.

Sometimes the communicator will have the option to choose the channel. Under these circumstances, they can consider the available options, the audience’s preferences, and how constraints such as time, location, and information sensitivity aEect channel choice. In other circumstances the audience will decide the channel for them, and the communicator will have to adapt accordingly.

- Culture – Communicators can also consider the cultural context in which they are communicating. Culture can refer to the geographic culture in which an organization operates or an audience identiﬁes with. It can also refer to the speciﬁc culture of an organization or even a team within the organization. A message that resonates in one culture might need to be communicated diEerently in another culture if a communicator is hoping for a similar response. Message Delivery is the act of communicating the message. A communicator may create a persuasive message and adapt it strategically to the channel and culture, but if it is not delivered eEectively – in a way that the audience understands and is inspired by – it could result in failed communication. It is also important that the communicator calibrates the delivery of the message based on the content. Sharing bad news with a smile on your face will assuredly not land well. To help with message delivery choices, communicators can consider style and visual aids.

- Style – It’s often not just what you say or write, it’s how you say or write it, and that’s where delivery style becomes a critical variable in any communication situation. Depending on the channel, style will manifest in diEerent forms. For in-person and other visual contexts, verbal and non-verbal delivery will be essential. For written contexts, the voice and personality of the writer, as seen through word choice, sentence structure, document design, etc. will add stylistic details that can improve the eEectiveness of the message.

- Visual Aids – To help deliver the message to an audience, a communicator may also use support, usually in the form of visual aids. These visuals, which can take the form of slides, images, videos, and even props, complement what the communicator is speaking or writing. The key for the communicator is ensuring the visuals help the audience process the message. Too often visuals distract the audience from what’s important to understand and remember.

By focusing on the distinct processes of creating, adapting, and then delivering a message, communicators can position themselves to achieve the desired response from their audience. But how will they know they are successful? Deﬁning Success Whether it’s a presentation, email, Slack, or LinkedIn post, strategic communicators can evaluate how eEective they are in communicating with their audience by setting success metrics. They can also use the feedback they receive to iterate on their strategy for next time.

- Success Metrics – Success metrics should be closely aligned with the communicators’ objective – what they want the audience to think, feel, and/or do at the end of the communication situation. In many situations, communicators do not need to take a big data approach to measuring success. If a sales professional is hoping to land a follow-up meeting to discuss pricing options because of their pitch, the success metric is simple: did you get the follow-up meeting? Yes or no?

- Iteration – Strategic communicators can also pay close attention to what resonates and doesn’t with their audience. Perhaps a seemingly minor point in a memo ends up being a major point for the audience, something the communicator did not uncover in their communicator-audience relationship analysis. Next time they will emphasize that point. Like engineers, software developers, and Formula 1 teams, setting up after action reviews to help iterate on communication strategy can be beneﬁcial to communicators.

Strategic communication starts with a simple proposition: a communicator sends a message to an audience, in the hopes that the audience will respond with what they want. To increase their chances of success, communicators can make a variety of choices on messaging – what message they create, how they adapt the message to the channel and culture, and how they deliver it. And if they are clear on their objectives and success metrics, they will know if they were successful in their communication after the fact and identify valuable insights to apply to future communication situations with this audience or others. The advancement and widespread adoption of artiﬁcial intelligence may introduce new complexity to this framework.

PART 2: ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE IMPLICATIONS ON STRATEGIC COMMUNICATION To explore the implications of artiﬁcial intelligence on strategic communication, we ﬁrst need to understand what artiﬁcial intelligence is. So we asked ChatGPT 4 for an answer and within seconds it responded with this:

"Artiﬁcial Intelligence is the ﬁeld of study and creation of computer systems that can perform tasks which would require human intelligence. These tasks include learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and understanding language."

The answer sounds conﬁdent and plausible, but should we believe it? On what data is it based? What were the original sources of the data, and should they be credited? If so, how?

It turns out ChatGPT did a good job with this question (perhaps unsurprisingly so, it should know the underlying technology on which it is built!). The answer complements well how colleagues across MIT, other academic institutions, and in industry deﬁne the technology. But it also brings up some of the questions about the complications that arise from its use.

Indeed, across all variables in strategic communication, AI introduces varying levels of complexities that communicators and audiences are beginning to negotiate. The remainder of this section explores the potential promise and peril of artiﬁcial intelligence in (1) the communicator-audience relationship, (2) message creation, adaptation, and design, and (3) deﬁning success. In some cases, the solutions will be straightforward. Others will require a test and learn approach. It is also highly likely that advancement in AI will surface new questions not considered here. AI Implications on the Communicator-Audience Relationship Communicating with other human beings can be hard enough. Introducing human-like artiﬁcial intelligence technologies into the communicator-audience relationship could alter the dynamics in various ways. To unpack the possibilities further, let’s return to the concepts of identity, trust, and objectives in the communicator-audience relationship. If both are using AI in a communication situation, what impact, if at all, does it have on the interaction? A key implication: AI use introduces a third party in the communication-audience relationship. The AI, as that third party, could aEect how identity is shaped and perceived, the level of trust on both sides, and what both parties want and need from the interaction.

- Identity – In the age of AI, the question of identity – who you are, who they are – is complicated by the question of what is real? We have already seen AI used to create deepfakes of celebrities and political ﬁgures. If a CEO creates an avatar to connect with employees in multiple locations, is that avatar considered a real and authentic representation of the CEO? If an employee decides to send their bot to a meeting,

## Page 8

what does that decision communicate to the meeting lead about the audience’s level of interest and commitment – real and perceived?

- Trust – The communicator-audience relationship is built on trust, trust in each other. Adding a third element in AI on either side of the relationship could impact the ability to build and strengthen trust. If a communicator uses AI to write an email, should the audience trust that the communicator believes the thoughts and ideas in the email, simply because they have put their name on it? Or from the audience’s perspective, if they use an AI tool to record and then synthesize every word in a meeting, how might that change the level of information the communicator will share?

- Objectives – In a world of only human power, communicators rely on audiences to act and behave in the way they want to get things done. If AI can take on more humanlike tasks, communicators and audiences alike may see a shift in their objectives – wants and needs – in a communication interaction. If a machine can do many things that a human can do, how might the nature of communication interactions between humans change? Could these interactions shift more toward shaping perceptions and feelings? AI Implications on Message Creation, Adaptation, and Delivery The most prevalent use case of artiﬁcial intelligence, and speciﬁcally generative AI, in communication today is in messaging. As a result, a new division of labor between humans and AI is emerging in the creation, adaptation, and delivery of messages, which in some cases requires humans to enhance or learn new skills. Of course, it is still possible for humans to create, adapt, and deliver messages without the assistance of AI, and that will likely be true for the foreseeable future. But for the cases where AI is used, understanding, preparing for, and managing the diEerent roles of humans and AI is a strategic consideration. Let’s explore the emerging roles of both AI and humans in the messaging process. Message Creation – the creative exercise of message development.

- AI Role – Generator – Generative AI tools have amazed users with how quickly they can create and structure content depending on the prompt and the ways in which they respond to feedback and further direction. In this respect, the tools will increasingly be used to provide drafts of content in multiple formats, including text, images, video, and audio.

- Human Role – Editor – In this model where AI helps with the creative process, humans may shift more toward an editorial role, and less as sole creators of content. In this role, they can do what editors have traditionally done: provide the right direction to the content generator (i.e. where to focus their eEorts); objectively

critique and improve the content once it is generated; and deliver feedback to make any course corrections and enhancements. Message Adaptation – the strategic process of adapting the message a communicator creates to the channel and culture.

- AI Role – Advisor – The more AI is used to communicate in various contexts (channels and cultures), the more it may learn about the various values, norms, and languages that resonate. For example, it may oEer a perspective on how to adapt a message to an Instagram post in a way that can maximize engagement, based on its knowledge of how the Instagram algorithm works. Or it may oEer a primer on communication norms in a particular geographic area.

- Human Role – Decision Maker – While AI tools can provide a point-of-view on channel and cultural adaptations, the human communicator still plays an essential role in deciding how speciﬁcally to adapt to the nuances of channels and cultural contexts. Message Delivery – the act of communicating the message.

- AI Role – Producer – AI tools can be used to help enhance the delivery of the message. This could be a bot providing feedback to a communicator on a presentation in practice rounds. Or it could be through audio and visuals an AI tool creates to support the presentation. Like a producer in the arts, the AI stays more in the background when it is time to deliver the message to an audience.

- Human Role – Performer – In many communication contexts, the human will still need to be front and center, delivering the message. In presentations, for example, the ability to captivate and engage an audience still remains squarely with the human. Although some contexts in the future might allow for a human’s AI bot to present on their behalf, humans still have an essential performative role in communication. AI Implications on Deﬁning Success In a rapidly digitizing workplace, more data about how organizations work and communicate is being created than ever before. This presents signiﬁcant data privacy and security questions. How should employee emails and chats be treated? Should meetings be recorded and can AI take notes? Where is this information stored and for how long? Who owns it? Is it personally identiﬁable? As organizations negotiate the answers to these questions, there will be important implications for communicators for how they can ethically and responsibly use the data available to them to improve their eEectiveness. If a presentation is recorded, for example,

communicators can review what worked, what did not, and how the audience responded. This is not new. What is new is the assistance that AI tools can oEer in these situations. Through computer vision tools, the communicator could see statistics on how many audience members were looking up at the presenter versus looking down at their phones – a measure of engagement. Through natural language processing, the communicator can see an analysis of a brainstorming conversation – who participated, how many times, and what their speciﬁc contributions were – an indication of team communication dynamics. The more data that are collected ethically and responsibly about communication interactions, the more communicators can improve their eEectiveness with audiences. Measurement in a pre-digital world has often been an inexact science for communicators. In a digital world aided by AI tools, a new frontier of communication analytics is upon us. AI as Audiences: Getting the Most Out of AI The AI opportunities and challenges in strategic communication discussed thus far assume that humans are leveraging AI to its fullest potential, but this may not be the case for a variety of reasons – you may not understand how the AI works, what it likes/doesn’t like, what its objectives are. In some respects, sounds a lot like questions we would ask about humans!

Viewed from this lens, we have some precedent to draw on. In the modern workplace, leaders have been focused on getting the most out of their people for decades. How do we organize and inspire our employees to reach and surpass our goals? Management literature is ﬁlled with excellent research-based principles and strategies to help answer this question.

Strategic communicators can ask a similar question of the AI tools they use – how can they get the most out of them? From this perspective, many of the same strategic communication principles apply. An example is the emerging ﬁeld of prompt engineering, or what we might deﬁne as how the human communicates with the AI tool. Like human-tohuman communication, if you are unclear in your messaging, you will not get the desired result. Prompt engineering is really an extension of strategic communication applied to AI – what appeals will work best for the AI – that the AI will understand and be able to act on? CONCLUSION: TECHNOLOGICAL CAPABILITY VERSUS AUDIENCE IMPACT The hype around artiﬁcial intelligence, and speciﬁcally generative AI has been signiﬁcant, and in many respects for good reasons. The capabilities of these tools are not only unprecedented but have also improved exponentially in such a short period of time. It is reasonable to assume that technological progress will continue, and communications need to be prepared. At the same time, we should not forget the fundamentals of strategic communication. At its core, this is about a communicator sending a message to an audience and the audience responding. As this note has explored, AI tools can assist in this process, but only to the extent that they are eEective in helping the communicator elicit the desired response from their audience. AI might have impressive capabilities. If they do not help communicators become more persuasive and eEective, how useful are they?
