## Slide 5 — Common stock

- Common Stock - Basic residual ownership share in the corporation.
- Common stockholders:
  - Have the right to any residual value in the firm after other obligations (i.e., debt and preferred stock) are met
  - Can vote on certain corporate issues. Examples?
- Common stock is divided into:
  - Par value
    - Par value is the stated value on the face of the security.
  - Par value has no relation to market value.
  - Take par value as given and use to record stock transactions.
  - Additional paid-in capital (APIC)
    - The difference between capital raised and par value

## Slide 6 — Preferred stock

- Preferred Stock (for completeness)
- -   General term for a class of stock.
- Has preference to common stock in bankruptcy.
- Has different rights than and/or preferences to Common Stock.
- These preferences may include:
- -  Dividends: a pre-specified dividend stated when shares are issued.  Rights to annual dividends are typically first to preferred stock, then to common stock.
- -  Cumulative Dividends: unpaid dividends accumulate and must be paid before common shareholders can receive dividends.
- -  Convertible: can be converted in common shares at a pre-specified rate.
- -	Venture capitalists often use convertible preferred to fund pre-IPO companies. Get dividend pre-IPO and convert to common at IPO.

## Slide 7 — Other terminology

- Authorized shares
  - Number of shares that can be sold/issued.
- Issued shares
  - Number of shares that are sold/issued.
  - There are often shares authorized to be issued but not issued.
  - Issued shares ≤ authorized shares
- Outstanding shares
  - Number of issued shares actually owned by shareholders.
  - Outstanding shares = Issued shares – shares held in treasury
  - Outstanding shares ≤ issued shares ≤ authorized shares

## Slide 12 — Dividends

- Dividends are payments made to shareholders from a company’s profits, representing a return on their investment. They are typically issued in cash or additional shares.
  - They are not an expense, but they do reduce retained earnings.
- Why are dividends important?
- -  Dividends provide a direct financial return to shareholders.
- -  They reflect the company’s profitability and financial health.

## Slide 16 — Stock Dividends

- Sometimes dividends are paid in the form of a company’s stock (as opposed to in cash).  If the stock dividend is small (A dividend less than 25%) the firm will record the transaction at the Market Value of the firm’s stock.
- If the dividend is large, (greater than 25%) then the firm will record the transaction using the par value of the firm’s stock

## Slide 19 — Stock Splits

- Another alternative to a stock dividend is a stock split.  When a firm does a stock split, like a 2 for 1 stock split, the number of shares outstanding doubles, and the par value (if there is one) is cut in half.
- There is no accounting transaction associated with stock splits.  Instead, the balance sheet is updated to reflect a new number of shares outstanding and a new par value

## Slide 20 — Financial Statement Analysis

- When doing any sort of financial statements analysis with per share data, be careful of stock splits and stock dividends.  These transactions can have a big effect on the denominator, and thus a large change in the ratio, that does not reflect any underlying change in the economics of the firm.

## Slide 21 — Treasury Stock

- Treasury Stock (Share Repurchases): stock which has been repurchased by the company
- Why do companies repurchase shares?
  - Because managers believe the stock is “undervalued”
  - As an alternative to dividends
  - To meet option requirements or increase earnings-per-share
- The accounting treatment of a stock repurchase is to reduce cash and to reduce Shareholders Equity. Thus, treasury stock is not an asset. Why else?
  - Recording treasury shares as an asset would make the firm’s assets dependent upon its market value and vice versa
