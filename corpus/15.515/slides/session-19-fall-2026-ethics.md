## Slide 9 — What is a crypto derivatives exchange?

- A crypto exchange is just like a stock exchange, in the sense that it facilitates trading between people who want to buy or sell an asset (like stock) in exchange for cash.
- A crypto derivatives exchange allows customers to borrow and lend cryptocurrencies
  - E.g., FTX, Binance
  - Much riskier than a traditional exchange
  - Derivative trading of crypto is prohibited in the U.S.
- Traders on a derivatives exchange are typically required to deposit assets with the exchange (collateral or margins) to ensure there are sufficient funds to cover their bets.
- 9

## Slide 10 — What is a crypto derivatives exchange?

- At any given point in time, derivatives exchanges such as FTX have balances owed to them by their customers. Such balances due from customers can be in dollars and cryptocurrencies. Money due from customers are the assets of the exchange
- At the same time, such exchanges also hold dollars and cryptocurrencies belonging to their customers, which are the exchange’s liabilities
- Customers can withdraw their dollars/crypto at will, and sharp changes in the price of crypto can result in situations where the exchange does not have sufficient assets to cover its liabilities
- Should several customers choose to withdraw their dollars/crypto following a big price change, the exchange might not be able to honor customer requests

## Slide 16 — Why did Alameda’s leaked balance sheet bring down SBF’s empire?

- Alameda’s balance sheet showed that it had $14.6 billion in assets
- Its single biggest asset was $3.7 billion of “unlocked FTT”
- The third largest entry was $2.6 billion of “FTT collateral”
- The liabilities were dominated by $7.4 billion of loans, and had significant borrowings from FTX

## Slide 17 — Why did Alameda’s leaked balance sheet bring down SBF’s empire?

- As Alameda’s liabilities became due, they may have had to liquidate a large portion of its FTT holdings to settle its liabilities, but doing so would put significant pressure on the price of FTT
- Investors concerned about Alameda’s ability to pay off its liabilities would also be concerned about the price of FTT (…let me sell my FTT before Alameda sells and pushes prices down)
- If you believe that FTX loaned customer assets to Alameda and got back FTT as collateral, you are especially concerned because a drop in the price of FTT will lower the likelihood that Alameda will repay FTX, destabilizing FTX

## Slide 22 — Internal Controls

- Internal controls are a series of processes such as reviews, approvals, checks and balances, and other procedures that firms use to:
- Safeguard assets
- Ensure accurate financial reporting
- Ensure compliance with applicable laws, regulations, and internal policies
- Internal controls can be as simple as a random spot-check of inventory on hand, or a complicated system of analytical procedures to identify misreporting or fraud.
- 22

## Slide 23 — Internal Controls

- FTX had a startling lack of internal controls
- No internal audit department
- No segregation of duties
  - (for example, you typically don’t want the person that receives cash from customers to also deposit the cash, and to reconcile the bank statements and have the ability to write checks)
- Keep track of assets (SBF “lost track of” millions of dollars)

## Slide 25 — Board of directors

- The board of directors plays a crucial role in a company’s system of internal controls.  For public companies, they are elected by shareholders, and they have a fiduciary responsibility to protect shareholders’ interests.
- The board typically has an audit committee that hires external auditors, and interacts with external and internal auditors to evaluate the effectiveness of the internal control system.
- They ratify business decisions, select and appoint CEOs, are engaged in strategic planning, and are responsible for the financial stewardship of the organization.
- In the U.S., public companies face rules on who can be on boards, including requirements that some board members have financial expertise, and some board members be independent from the organization.

## Slide 27 — Auditors

- Auditors are hired by firms to conduct a series of procedures (confirming account balances, verifying the existence of assets, testing internal controls, etc.) to provide an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared in accordance with GAAP.
- In the U.S., auditors are required to provide reasonable assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatements.
- There is an inherent independence problem: the firm pays the auditor to provide an opinion on the firm’s financial statements.
- Auditors face legal liability if they fail to do their job (because of gross negligence or fraud).  The extent of this liability varies around the world.

## Slide 31 — creditors

  - When firms borrow money from banks, banks typically place a series of covenants in the contract that allows them to monitor management and take actions to protect their cash flows if they receive signals that the firm is performing poorly.
  - Collateral can often reduce the need for these covenants. For example, I don’t send the bank that holds my mortgage my tax forms each year, as my house serves as collateral.
  - In this case, the collapse was so quick it is not clear a bank could take any actions. Also, Alameda fraudulently used customer deposits as collateral.
  - FTT tokens as collateral is more disturbing.
