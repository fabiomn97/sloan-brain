## Slide 7 — CECl and Accounts Receivable (PART 1)

- CECL stands for Current Expected Credit Losses – and it is a major change in the accounting for assets like Accounts Receivable and Loans (i.e. assets which represent debts owed to the firm by the firm’s customers).
- Under CECL (Technically ASC 326) firms should recognize EXPECTED losses on their receivables, at the time that financing is provided and update that estimate over the life of the receivable.  They then “write-off” the receivable when they identify the specific customers that failed to pay.
- This was one of the most controversial changes in accounting in the past 10 years, as it fundamentally changed the way financial institutions accounted for loans on their balance sheets.

## Slide 16 — Allowance for Bad Debts

- 16
    - The allowance method attempts to recognize bad debt expense on the income statement in the period revenue is earned
    - There is a trade-off here – firms will provide more relevant info (i.e. more timely) but it will be less reliable
    - The allowance for bad debts is also called:
      - Allowance for doubtful accounts or uncollectible accounts
      - Reserve for bad debts / uncollectible amounts
      - Allowance for credit losses / expected credit losses
- Firms will “write-off” accounts receivable balances as they identify specific customers that are not going to pay.  In this setting write-offs do not affect the balance sheet or income statement as the loss was already estimated.

## Slide 22 — What happens if we make a “mistake” in our estimate of losses? (I of II)

- We must adjust the Allowance for Doubtful Accounts balance, so the net accounts receivable accurately reflect what we expect to collect on our accounts receivable.
- But this also means that we must also either recognize additional expenses in future periods or increase future income to recognize errors in accounting for bad debt expenses.
- As a result, inaccuracies incurrent period estimates affect future income (we do not restate old balances).

## Slide 28 — CECl and Accounts Receivable (PART 2)

- Practically, accountants used to rely on historical estimates of losses to estimate losses on their accounts receivable portfolio. While these historical estimates are still an input into the accounting for expected losses, the accountant also has to forecast the expected losses over the life of the receivable at the time they provide financing
- This requires the accountant to evaluate the current business environment and forecast changes, like changes in interest rates, and deteriorations in credit quality due to macro economic factors. This complicates the process of developing these estimates
- KEY TAKEAWAY:  Your accountant has to do this!  You want to know what they are doing, but don’t have to get into the weeds on how they estimate future losses on the receivables portfolio. Firms that have large receivable balances (like Universal) may have a lot more (or less) variation in these losses depending on the quality of the model used to estimate future losses. Potentially significantly more corrections in accounting estimates

## Slide 30 — What about notes receivable?

- The financial statements are a little vague, but it appears that the Accts Receivable are due before the student graduates, so Universal Tech can hold the degree as “leverage” to ensure collections.  So the bad debts for A/R are most likely related to receivables for students that are expected to dropout.
- Notes receivable are due after graduation, are not guaranteed by the government, and thus there is a lot more uncertainty if they will be collected.  The uncertainty is so great that the revenue recognition transaction is recorded at the expected collection amount, as opposed to the amount billed the student.
- The industry that this happens the most in is healthcare.  Patients may be billed hundred of thousands of dollars with 10% collection rates.

## Slide 31 — Financial Statement Analysis Issues: Ratios Involving Receivables

- Operating Efficiency
- A_R Turnover   =   Revenue / Average Accounts Receivable
- (Strictly speaking, we should use credit revenues (not revenues) for this ratio but analysts often use revenues for want of full data)
- The ratio measures how quickly you collect cash on credit sales
- If company has lots of credit revenue, but very low receivables, it implies that you collect cash on your credit revenues quickly
- Days receivables   =   365 / A_R Turnover
