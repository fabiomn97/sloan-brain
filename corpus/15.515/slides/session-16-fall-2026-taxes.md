## Slide 6 — Tax Cut and jobs act (“TCJA”)

- In Dec 2017, Congress passed the Tax Cut and Jobs Act (TCJA)
- Lowered US corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% in 2018
- Income parked overseas was “deemed repatriated” and taxable.
- Pre-TCJA, corporations did not pay US tax on foreign income parked overseas until money was brought back to US (“repatriated”)
- Resulted in cash trapped overseas

## Slide 8 — Reminder: accrual accounting

- Scenario 1: firm prepays $24,000 for 2 years of insurance
- Scenario 2: firm pays $24,000 after being insured for 2 years
- Scenario 3: firm pays $1,000/month cash for insurance
- Cash flows differ across three scenarios…
- But insurance expense is same under all three! $12,000/year
- And total $ insurance payments = total insurance expense over two years
- You will see parallels with tax accounting…

## Slide 9 — Key point: If you forget everything else, remember this

- Accounting income ≠ Taxable income
- Tax expense ≠ Cash taxes payable
- Cash taxes payable is the cash outflows owed to the US tax authorities (typically paid a few months later)
- Tax expense on the income statement is an accrual number just like every other expense on the I/S
  - Reflects a cost incurred by firms for operating in the US
  - Accrual  Tax ‘cost’ matched to income when it is earned; not when settled through cash receipts/outflows

## Slide 10 — Accounting Income vs. Taxable Income

- Companies maintain (at least) two sets of books, one for reporting to their stakeholders and one for reporting to tax authorities
- Financial accounting is concerned with presenting information in the financial statements 	that are useful to investors and other stakeholders
    - “Accounting Income” or “Book Income”
    - Uses Accrual Accounting (Prioritizing relevance)
- Tax returns are prepared to report to tax authorities and must comply with tax regulations
    - “Taxable Income”
    - More like cash accounting (Prioritizing reliability)

## Slide 33 — Net Operating Losses (NOL) and Deferred Tax Assets

- 33
- Citigroup Inc. … and General Motors … announced a combined $29 billion charge against earnings Tuesday due to the new tax law
- Citigroup took a $19 billion charge to write down so-called deferred tax assets, akin to IOUs that can be used to pay future tax bills … Such assets often result from past losses
- The primary reason for the charges is … The tax law’s reduction in the U.S. corporate tax rate to 21% from the old 35% rate
- Deferred tax assets are past tax credits and deductions that companies can hold on to and use to defray future tax bills. But when the tax rate declines, companies’ tax bills shrink

## Slide 34 — Net Operating Losses (NOL) and Deferred Tax Assets

- Intuition: If a U.S. individual investor has a capital loss:
  - They can deduct the capital loss up to $3,000
  - If they have losses in excess of $3,000, they “carry forward” the excess and offset future taxable income
- If a firm incurs a loss for tax purposes:
  - After 2018 TCJA, can typically no longer carry the loss back and get a refund
  - The firm can carry the loss forward and offset 80% of future taxable income in a given year
- Net operating loss (NOL) carryforwards or “Loss Carryforwards” create a deferred tax asset
  - Intuition:  Firm will pay less cash taxes in future.
- 34

## Slide 35 — Net Operating Losses (NOL) and Deferred Tax Assets

- 35
- Deferred tax assets arise when future taxes payable will be less than future tax expense, as in prior examples and as with Loss Carryforwards
- This asset is like a “pre-paid” asset because past losses can be used to offset future 	taxable income and thus reduce tax burden
- But if this asset is not “realizable,” then it should be reduced.  An analogy is accounts receivable that are not expected to be collected
- Firms reduce deferred tax assets by creating a “Valuation Allowance”, a contra-asset that is similar to ADA but for deferred tax assets
