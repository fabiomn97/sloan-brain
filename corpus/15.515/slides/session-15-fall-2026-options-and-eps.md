## Slide 5 — What are stock options?

- Some useful definitions:
  - Exercise price (or “strike price”): the price the option holder pays to acquire the share
  - Expiration date: date when employee can no longer exercise the option
  - Vesting period: how long the option holder must work before being able to exercise all of their options
  - Cliff: how long the option holder must work before being able to exercise any of their options
  - “In-the-money”: the current share price is more than the exercise price
  - “At-the-money: the current share price is equal to the exercise price
  - “Out-of-the-money: the current share price is less than the exercise price
- 5

## Slide 6 — Why award stock options?

- Recruiting, motivating and rewarding employees
- Aligning employee and shareholder interests
- Retention tool (vesting requirements make it costly to leave)
- Compensate employees without burning through cash
  - - Employees exercising options are a source of cash
  - - Also, less pressure to lay people off in bad times to conserve cash
- Encourage risk-taking

## Slide 11 — Accounting for stock options

- Measure cost of compensation at the fair value of the award at the date of grant using an option-pricing model
  - Usually, Black-Scholes model
  - Model considers variables including the current price of stock, term of the options, risk-free rate of interest, expected volatility of stock price, expected dividend yield (you’ll cover this in Finance class)
    - In accounting class, we’ll provide the option value so you do not need to know how to use this model

## Slide 12 — Accounting for stock options

- At grant date: No entry, but measure fair value of options
- Expense fair value of options over the vesting period
  - E.g., if options are worth $60,000 and vest over 3 years, expense is $20,000/year
- Record cash received from employee upon exercise, adjust Paid in Capital

## Slide 17 — Accounting for stock options

- The decision to expense stock options was hotly debated:
- Not cash
- Often zero intrinsic value at issuance (i.e., issued at-the-money)
- Many options go unexercised: stock price falls, or employee turnover before vesting
- Difficulties estimating inputs to Black-Scholes model
- Debates about whether Black-Scholes model correctly prices stock options used for compensation

## Slide 18 — Earnings per share

- The amount of earnings for the period available to each share of common stock outstanding during the reporting period
- = Net Income / Weighted Average Shares Outstanding
- Commonly used valuation, performance, and compensation metric
- 18

## Slide 19 — Calculating weighted shares outstanding

## Slide 23 — Convertible Bonds

- Convertible bonds are securities with the potential to convert to common stock
  - Example: Delta airlines issues a convertible bond with a $1,000 face value that pays 5% interest per year. The bond has a maturity of 10 years and a convertible ratio of 100 shares for every convertible bond.
- The conversion effect is considered when computing diluted EPS
  - Assume a hypothetical conversion at the beginning of period (or issue date if later)
- Effect of Convertible Bonds on Diluted EPS
  - Numerator: add back interest expense, net of tax (the firm no longer pays this interest exp)
  - Denominator: add new common shares issued

## Slide 26 — Discussion of earnings per share

- EPS calculations can depend a lot on assumptions about dilution.
- EPS includes non-cash expenses (e.g., depreciation) and non-operating expenses (like interest expense), so firms often make supplemental disclosures, which are referred to as ”Non-GAAP” Disclosures.
- These Non-GAAP disclosures are often included in the 10-K and earning call
  - Non-GAAP metrics can provide more granularity, but are highly subjective
  - Covered in more detail in BAUFS (15-535)
- 26

## Slide 27 — Why use non-gaap measures?

- Better captures industry economics
  - E.g., subscriber growth for cell phone companies, same-store sales growth for retail…
- Fit to company’s strategy and growth stage
  - For some companies (e.g., startups), sales growth is more important to achieve than profitability
  - Consistency: report metrics that are used in the company’s incentive plans and target setting
- GAAP income includes (undesirable?) deductions for:
  - Interest expense (a financial cost, not an operating cost)
  - Depreciation and amortization (non-cash)
  - Non-recurring items

## Slide 30 — Restricted Stock Plans

- Restricted stock units (“RSUs”)
  - Employer grants an employee the right to receive stock, under certain conditions (e.g., performance or passage of time)
  - RSU value determined at grant date
  - Employee must satisfy vesting requirements for the stock to be issued
  - Employee forfeits RSU if they exit the company before the vesting period
  - Accounting for RSUs:
    - Increase Compensation Expense and Paid-in Capital: Restricted Stock over the vesting period
