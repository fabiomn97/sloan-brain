Universal Technical Institute, Inc.1

Sushmita Das, Christopher Noe, and Joseph Weber

For most of the past half-century, the surest path to a stable, well-paying career in America was thought to run through a four-year college. By the 2020s, that belief had begun to fray. Tuition had risen far faster than inflation, outstanding student loan debt had swelled past $1.7 trillion, and a growing number of young people questioned whether four years and tens of thousands of dollars in loans were worth it.2 Attention turned instead to the skilled trades. On social media, videos extolling careers as electricians, welders, and mechanics attracted millions of views and spawned hashtags such as #nodebtneeded. Nationwide, trade and technical school revenue reached $5.1 billion in the final quarter of 2025, up 41 percent from four years earlier.3 The most affordable pathways into the skilled trades, however, could not keep pace with demand. Community colleges and union apprenticeships, which offered training at little or no cost, were often filled to capacity. In Philadelphia, for example, a steamfitters’ union apprenticeship program received 609 applications for just 85 available slots.4 Students unable to secure a place in these programs increasingly turned to private, for-profit trade schools, where training was more readily available but tuition could reach tens of thousands of dollars. One of the largest operators in this market was Universal Technical Institute (“UTech”).

Company Background

UTech was founded in 1965 in Phoenix, Arizona, as a training school for automotive technicians. Over the following decades, it grew into one of the nation’s largest providers of post-secondary education for the transportation, skilled trades, and energy fields. As of fiscal year 2025, the company operated two reportable segments. The first, carrying the Universal name, ran 15 campuses across nine states and trained automotive, diesel, collision repair, motorcycle, marine, and wind power technicians. Much of the instruction took place in hands-on labs, and its programs were often developed in partnership with original equipment manufacturers such as American Honda, BMW, and Harley-Davidson, which helped design curricula and frequently hired graduates. The second segment, Concorde Career Colleges, acquired in December 2022, prepared students for careers in the allied health, dental, nursing, patient care, and diagnostic fields at 17 campuses across eight states as well as online. Seeking to capitalize on the increasing interest in the skilled trades, UTech launched 23 new programs across existing campuses between 2023 and 2025 and began construction on two new transportation and skilled trades campuses expected to open in 2026. CEO Jerome Grant emphasized the financial discipline underlying the company’s growth strategy, telling The Wall Street Journal, “We won’t teach in areas where the ROI’s not there.”5 Investors appeared to reward this strategy, with UTech’s stock price rising sharply during this period (Exhibit 1). Tuition at UTech could be substantial. In fiscal 2025, tuition for Universal programs ranged from approximately $18,000 for the 30-week Wind Power Technician program to $69,000 for the 90-week Automotive and Diesel program, with the average Universal student generating $35,100 of revenue per year. Concorde’s programs ranged from approximately $1,000 for an eight-week phlebotomy course to $99,000 for its 90-week Dental Hygiene program, with the average Concorde student generating approximately $30,000 of revenue per year. Because few students could afford to pay these amounts out of pocket, UTech relied heavily on student financing. Most students funded their education through federal financial aid programs, supplemented by scholarships, grants, employer-sponsored training, and other sources. When these resources still left a funding gap, however, UTech was willing to do something most schools were not: lend students the money itself.

Federal Student Financial Aid

All of UTech’s campuses were accredited and eligible to participate in federal student financial aid programs under Title IV of the Higher Education Act of 1965, administered by the U.S. Department of Education. The company was also approved to participate in education benefit programs administered by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs.

Because federal student financial aid sat at the center of UTech’s business model, federal regulations shaped many of the company’s operating decisions. Under the 90/10 rule, a for-profit educational institution had to derive at least 10% of its cash-basis revenue from sources other than federal education assistance funds or risk losing its eligibility to participate in Title IV programs. As a result, institutions had to demonstrate each year that at least some portion of tuition payments came from non-federal sources, regardless of where those funds ultimately originated. In fiscal 2025, approximately 78% of UTech’s cash-basis revenue came from federal education assistance funds (Exhibit 2). The version of the rule in effect in 2025 was more restrictive than in prior years. Historically, veterans’ and military education benefits were treated as non-federal funds in the calculation, creating what critics referred to as the 90/10 loophole. The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 closed this loophole for fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2023, shifting approximately 11 percentage points of Utech’s cash-basis revenue from the non-federal to the federal side of the ratio.6 A second Title IV requirement focused on student loan defaults. Institutions could lose their eligibility if the three-year cohort default rate on federal student loans exceeded 30% for three consecutive years or 40% in a single year. In fiscal 2025, all of UTech’s institutions reported a three-year cohort default rate of 0%, reflecting the COVID-19 student loan payment pause (Exhibit 3). Before the pandemic, however, UTech’s cohort default rates had consistently remained below the regulatory thresholds. Federal student financial aid rules continued to evolve. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law on July 4, 2025, conditioned Title IV eligibility for degree programs on new earnings benchmarks. In addition, beginning July 1, 2026, Workforce Pell expanded Pell Grant eligibility to certain short-term career and technical education programs for the first time.7

School-Sponsored Payment and Financing Programs

Students whose tuition was not fully covered by federal aid or other funding sources faced a remaining funding gap. To help bridge that gap, UTech offered several school-sponsored payment and financing options. Many students participated in in-school cash payment plans, which allowed them to pay tuition in installments while completing their programs rather than when tuition was initially due. Students requiring additional assistance could obtain longer-term financing. Universal and Concorde schools offered retail installment contracts that allowed students to repay tuition over periods of up to five years. Some of these contracts, particularly those involving students who remained enrolled, did not bear stated interest. For selected students who met specified eligibility criteria, Universal schools also offered a proprietary loan program. Under this program, a partner bank originated loans whose proceeds

Regardless of the specific mechanism, UTech was under no obligation to provide students with schoolsponsored payment or financing options when their available funding fell short of tuition. Accounting for Revenue and Credit Losses

UTech recognized tuition and fee revenue ratably over the term of a course or program of study. For the proprietary loan program, because experience showed that some loans would never be collected, the portion of a student’s tuition financed through UTech loans was treated as a form of variable consideration under revenue recognition rules. At the inception of each loan contract, the company estimated the amount it ultimately expected to collect, based on its actual collection experience over the previous ten years, and recognized only that amount as revenue while the student was in school. The expected collections were recorded on the balance sheet as notes receivable. Highlighting the judgment involved in accounting for the proprietary loan program, UTech’s auditors identified the expected collection rate as a Critical Audit Matter. For UTech’s remaining receivables from in-school cash-payment plans or retail installment contracts, the company maintained an allowance for credit losses, an estimate established in advance of the losses it expected to incur over the life of the receivables. The allowance was based on historical loss rates, borrowers’ creditworthiness and payment behavior, and assessments of current economic and regulatory conditions. The allowance increased as UTech recognized a provision for credit losses, which was reported within selling, general, and administrative expenses. When a receivable was ultimately deemed uncollectible, it was removed from the balance sheet and the allowance for credit losses was correspondingly decreased.

Fiscal 2025 Record Year

Exhibit 4 provides UTech’s financial statements for the fiscal year ended September 30, 2025. By all accounts, this year was the best in the company’s history. Revenue rose to a record $835.6 million, up 14% from the prior year and 38% from two years earlier. Similarly, net income reached a record $63.0 million, up 50% from the prior year and five times its level two years earlier. UTech also achieved double-digit increases in both average full-time active students and new student starts. Commenting on the strong performance, CEO Jerome Grant remarked, “We exceeded every major operational target we set and even surpassed our twice-raised revenue guidance range with 14% year-
