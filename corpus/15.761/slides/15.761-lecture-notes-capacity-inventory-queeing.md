1 Capacity Planning

Deciding the capacity of a system is a common yet complex managerial decision. It can be faced in manufacturing industries when deciding the installed capacity of a new factory, in service industries when deciding the staﬃng levels, etc. In general the risk of deciding to build to much capacity is related to capital expenses, labor expenses, resource waste, environmental damage and cash ﬂow issues. On the other hand, the risk of not building enough capacity is related to customer wait/death, brand damage, customer dissatisfaction, lost sales, low employee morale and high turnover.

This note discusses a simple but general model of a production process. We will sometimes refer to the production process as a ”work station”. The model can be diagrammed as follows:

This model consists of two elements. One is a server, which could be a doctor in a clinic, a numerically controlled machine tool in a job shop, an entire paper mill, or a directory assistance operator for AT&T. The second is a holding or waiting area where patients or steel castings or logs or telephones calls wait for their turn to be processed by the server.

Many production processes can be usefully represented by this model; virtually all of them are subject to variability of one kind or another. For example, patient arrivals to a clinic vary by the day of the week (Monday is typically the busiest day) and service times by the doctor vary depending upon the ailment of the patient. In a typical job shop, the mix of job types is always changing so that any given machine in the shop is likely to experience variable demands on its capabilities. The demand for the output of a paper mill varies with macroeconomic cycles, and the arrival of logs depends on the weather patterns in logging areas.

Classifying sources of variability by whether they are predictable (that is, deterministic or nonstochastic) or unpredictable (that is, stochastic, uncertain, random, or probabilistic) is often a useful ﬁrst step to analyzing processes subject to variability. Predictable variability is often referred to as ”seasonal.” Annual sales patterns of toys, snow shovels, air conditioners, and wedding dresses are quite predictable, but highly variable, due to seasonal e↵ects. Similarly, customer arrival patterns at a fast food restaurant and telephone calls to an airline reservations oﬃce are highly dependent on time of day, but in a predictable manner.

In other processes, the unpredictable component of variability will dominate the predictable component. Variability due to weather changes (that can a↵ect farming, transportation, or tourism operations), arrivals of jobs to a given machine in a complex job shop, service times in a hospital emergency room, changes in the volume of stock trading, calls to an urban ﬁre department, and machine breakdowns in a factory are often best modeled as stochastic.

At this point it is relevant to give the following deﬁnitions.

- The bottleneck of a system is deﬁned as the process within the system with the highest utilization factor

- The capacity of a system is deﬁned as the capacity of the bottleneck within the system

For example, consider the ER depicted in Figure 3, if we were told that the service rate of each station are given by µTN = 150 patients/hr, µNP = 85 patients/hr and µMD = 60 patients/hr, then we could compute ⇢TN = .66 patients/hr, ⇢NP = .94 patients/hr and ⇢MD = .9 patients/hr. The main managerial insight here is that if we want to increase the capacity of the system, then we need to increase the capacity of the bottleneck. Namely, in the ER example hiring one more MD would have no impact on the capacity of the system and therefore little impact on the average waiting time of the patients.

1.1 Predictable Variability

For a typical ﬁrm, the most important kind of predictable variability is that associated with demand for ﬁnished goods. The following discussion, concerning a plant with seasonal variation in its input, is intended to introduce inventory build up and throughput calculations in the simplest possible setting.

In our analysis we will only consider predictable variability, and we will use build-up diagrams to analyze it. This analysis is a good approximation for short run analysis of a system, where we will allow the service rate and the demand rate to vary over time.

1.2 Unpredictable Variability

This section discusses management of a single service facility that is subject to stochastic variability. One objective of the model to be presented is to predict system performance associated with di↵erent decision options. As above, we analyze how the system performs in serving customers who arrive demanding service at the facility. This terminology is very suggestive of customers being people, but this need not be the case. In the previous section, the customers were actually tons of ﬁsh. One could also easily imagine that the service facility is a drilling center (machine) and that customers are batches of similar pieces of metal each needing the same kind of service.

Similarly, one might think of a bank’s backroom operations, where the

## Page 10

”customers” are bundles of checks requiring cancelation and transaction recording, and the service facility does cancelation and recording. Or you might imagine the operations of a consulting business. The sta↵makes up the service facility and the customers are consulting jobs. Finally, you might imagine operating a tanning salon so that the customers are people arriving and wishing to tan their skin.

Figure 10: A Deterministic Queue Setting

To understand the sense in which the unpredictable variability term is intended, suppose that you are watching customers arrive for processing and, in particular, you observe the interarrival times (the elapsed time from one arrival to the next). Suppose some time has elapsed since the last arrival. If interarrival times are deterministic, the additional time you will have to wait until seeing another arrival is known precisely, based on how long it has been since the last arrival. In this section we assume that the interarrival times and the service times are random, and therefore not known with certainty, although we know their mean value and their standard deviation, and therefore taking the ratio of these two values we also know their coeﬃcient of variation (more on this later).

To make it more concrete lets consider a simple example. First consider a deterministic queue, initially empty, where jobs, or customers, arrive exactly every minute and each server takes exactly 45 seconds to process each job as depicted in Figure 10. Without unpredictable variability in the system we expect trivially not have any queue formed at any point in time, this is depicted in Figure 11.

The analysis tool we will use in this section to capture unpredictable variability is queuing theory. Queuing theory uses sophisticated analysis to derive relatively easy formulas with the objective of predicting long-term impact of unpredictable variability on congestion. Several remarks are in order. First, queueing theory is a relevant and very powerful tool for long run analysis, as a consequence we will necessarily restrict ourselves to systems with ﬁxed demand, or arrival, rate λ and ﬁxed service rate µ, where this ﬁxed values are the long run averages of the rates over time. Moreover we will assume throughout that the system’s utilization factor is less than one, namely ⇢= λ

µ < 1. Notice that in a system where ⇢> 1 the arrival rate is larger than the service rate, and therefore in the long run customers will keep queueing up to inﬁnity, which is not an interesting system to study.

The fundamental system we will study in this section is what is known in queuing theory as a G/G/N queue. This means that the arrival process and the service processes can be random and follow any general distribution (ﬁrst and second G respectively), and N identical servers operate in parallel in the system to process customer. Additional assumptions are that customers served in FIFO order. This is a general enough modeling tool that has been successfully used in di↵erent settings such as airline check-in counters, bank ATMs, retail cashiers, computer processing, manufacturing, call centers, 911 response, etc.

1.2.1 Little’s Law

We will introduce now a fundamental formula called Little’s law (ﬁrst proved by Professor John D. C. Little of the Sloan School), which applies in very general situations, even more general settings that the G/G/N queue we are considering here:

L = λW (1)

This formula formalizes the idea of conservation of ﬂow in a system in equilibrium, and essentially says that the expected number of customers in the queue equals the arrival rate (mean number of customers arriving per minute) times the expected waiting time that a customer spends in the queue. For your intuition, you can imagine a customer arriving and having to spend W minutes in the queue. Customers arrive at a rate of λ per minute, so we expect λW customers to arrive during that time. And that is what you

## Page 14

Figure 14: G/G/N Queuing Model

expect the average number of customers in the queue to be. This formula may appear trivial, however it is not; in fact its proof for the general case is rather complex and relatively recent. Little’s is conceptually depicted in Figure 15.

For practical purposes, Little’s Law allows us to ﬁnd the third performance measure -between average waiting time W, average queue length L and arrival rate λ- when the other two are known. In general, measuring, or estimating, the arrival rate λ to a system is the easier performance measure to obtain. Luckily for us, we will learn in the next section a formula to estimate the average queue length L in the G/G/N queue setting that we have been studying in this section, which together with Little’s Law will give us a complete picture of the performance measures we have considered here.

1.2.3 Managing the Psychology of Queuing

We end this section by pointing out a few important insight to better manage the psychology of queues.

- Unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time

- Process waits feel longer than in process waits

- Anxiety makes waits seem longer

## Page 19

- Uncertain waits seem longer than known, ﬁnite waits

- Unexplained waits seem longer than explained

- Unfair waits seem longer than equitable waits

- The more valuable the service, the longer the customer will wait

- Solo waits feel longer than group waits
