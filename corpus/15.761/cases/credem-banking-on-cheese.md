Credem: Banking on Cheese

On a sunny afternoon in October 2014, Credito Emiliano SpA (Credem) Credit Manager Antonio Benatti strolled through one of the warehouses owned and managed by Credem subsidiary, Magazzini Generali delle Tagliate SpA (MGT), in the Emilia Romagna region in Northern Italy. Credem was Italy's eighth largest bank by market capitalization with strong local roots in the region. The warehouse’s high walls were lined with wooden shelves, on which lay almost 300,000 ageing wheels of Parmigiano Reggiano cheese—each weighing approximately 40 kilograms (kgs)— gradually turning a dark golden color as they matured over a minimum period of one year. (See Exhibit 1 for photos of the warehouse and Exhibit 2 for specified characteristics of the cheese.) Credem accepted Parmigiano Reggiano as collateral for loans granted to producers of the cheese and MGT was entrusted with the storage and management of the cheese for the duration of the loan. If the producer was unable to repay the loan, or the current valuation of the collateral did not support the outstanding loan, Credem could sell the collateral to mitigate credit losses.

William Bizzarri, General Manager of MGT, was eager to show Benatti a new custom machine that had just gone into service, and was speedily turning and brushing the wheels of cheese. The warehouse was an impressive sight and Benatti was grateful for the opportunity to visit it before his meeting with Bizzarri and Vanni Ferrari, Food and Agricultural Specialist in Credem’s research department. The meeting had been called to discuss the fall in wholesale price of Parmigiano Reggiano, from €9.12/kg at the beginning of the year to €7.45/kg in September 2014,1 a consequence of weak demand worsened by the Russian embargo in response to international sanctions imposed because of the crisis in Ukraine. In particular, Benatti was concerned about the Lorenzo Verdi producer, a borrower who currently stocked 25,000 wheels in Credem’s warehouses as collateral. It was clear to Benatti that the collateral was no longer sufficient to back Verdi’s loan. Some recent quality issues that surfaced after a routine inspection of Verdi’s pledged cheese were also of concern.

Benatti had asked Bizzarri to look at wholesaler alternatives to offload the cheese. Ferrari had been tasked with putting together some likely price scenarios. As Benatti walked to the warehouse’s offices for the meeting he wondered if the bank should liquidate Verdi’s cheese and try to recoup what value it could immediately, or wait to see if the price would recover? Were there other scenarios, such as potential government action, to consider?

Credem

Founded in 1910, Credem was a regional bank, listed on the stock exchange since 1997. Headquartered in Reggio Emilia in the Emilia Romagna region of Italy, the bank had more than 600 branches and 45 points of sale for retail and corporate clients and was mostly focused on the service of families and SMEs.2 Credem’s main shareholder (77%) was Credemholding, a non-listed company controlled by Reggio Emilia entrepreneurs. (See Exhibit 3 for the group’s structure.)

Credem was characterized by a very conservative profile, both in terms of asset quality (best in class in Italy) and capital positioning (zero impact from new Basel 3 rules).3 Paolo Pratissoli, Senior Consultant for Investor Relations at Credem, said, “We are the cleanest bank in Italy. We are not the kind of bank that keeps people awake at night.” Daniele Morlini, Head of Investor Relations at Credem, commented, “For example, we didn’t have to restructure after the crisis, we didn’t have problems with risky financial exposure. We actually increased the number of employees. As other banks were in trouble we were able to gain market share.” The company reported revenue of €995.3 million during the fiscal year 2013, an increase of 2.5% compared to the prior year. The company's net income of €115.9 million was down 4% from 2012.4 (See Exhibit 4 for Credem’s income statement, financial ratios, and balance sheet.)

With a significant portion of total income attributed to agriculture, Credem had deep roots in the sector. Benatti said, “Before being named Credito Emiliano the bank was called Banca Agricola Commerciale di Reggio Emilia, bank for agriculture. So it has been a focus of the bank since the very beginning.” The agricultural sectors in which Credem was active included wine, tobacco and Parmigiano Reggiano.

Parmigiano Reggiano

Parmigiano Reggiano was a hard granular cheese with a long and natural maturation. Traceable to 1344, one of the earliest records of the word “Parmigiano” could be found in a purchasing ledger for Benedictine monks, who were experimenting with cattle breeding and cheese production in the Emilia Romagna region of Italy. It was a highly concentrated cheese containing 30% water and 70% nutrients, making it rich in proteins, lipids, calcium and phosphorus. Quickly digested and easily assimilated, dieticians often recommended it for children and the elderly, as well as for those practicing sport.5

Parmigiano Reggiano cheese was produced from full and partly skimmed milk, without additives or preservatives of any kind. Production came in characteristic wheel shapes called “forme.” Each forma weighed 38 to 42 kg and its production needed 13 to 16 liters of milk per kg. Before being consumed, the cheese needed to be matured for 18, 24, 30 or 36 months. Parmigiano Reggiano was used as a dish on its own, and as a dressing for other foods. Morlini elaborated, “An 18 month cheese has a lower value and is used on pasta whereas a 30 month cheese is a product to enjoy by itself. So you have different maturing periods and then different qualities and prices.” For its taste and nutrition qualities, it was considered one of the symbols of Italian food excellence and was known as “The King of Cheeses.”

Protected Designation of Origin Parmigiano Reggiano could only be produced in a welldefined territory in Northern Italy encompassing the provinces of Reggio Emilia, Parma and Modena, as well as part of the provinces of Mantova and Bologna. Parmigiano Reggiano was a “Protected Designation of Origin” (PDO) product, which was the highest level of protection and defense against adulteration for Italian food products. (a) Production Process

The production process began on the fields of the specified territory, where cattle were fed on locally grown forage. The cows were milked twice daily—in the morning and in the evening. The morning and evening milk was transported to the dairy within two hours of milking.12 Pratissoli explained, “There is a truck that goes to every farm that supplies milk to a particular dairy to collect the milk. All of the milk is poured in the same tank and the farmer is paid based on the quantity of milk he supplies.” Regular controls were carried out on the milk by the dairy to ensure high quality.13

At the dairy, the evening milk was left to rest in large vats overnight. The cheese maker skimmed the evening milk and combined it with whole milk from the morning milking. The milk was gently warmed in large copper cauldrons. Some naturally fermented whey was stirred in and started the acidification of the milk. The last ingredient added was veal rennet, a combination of enzymes harvested from veals, which coagulated the milk. After around ten minutes a curd formed, which was then broken down into minuscule granules. The mixture was cooked at 55 degrees Celsius over a period of about one hour. The curds dropped to the bottom of the boiler where they formed a spongy mass.14 The cheese maker cut the mass in two parts: the gemelle, or “twins.” They were placed into

Each wheel was given a unique, progressive number using casein (a protein naturally found in milk).16 A band was placed around the wheel to permanently stencil it with its identification details: the date of production, the identification number of the dairy, the trademark and the words PDO,17 as well as the dotted inscription “Parmigiano Reggiano” around the complete circumference of the cheese wheel.18 The wheels, after being left to rest, were moved into the salting room, where they were immersed in a solution of salt and water. At the end of this period, the wheels were moved to a warehouse for the remainder of the maturing process if the cheese producer did not have these facilities in-house.

(b) Maturing Process

The long-term maturing process took at least another 10 months, during which time the cheese had to remain in a temperature and humidity controlled environment.20 In addition, each wheel was brushed and rotated every 15 days for the first 12 months, after which time these procedures were carried out every 30 days.

Due to the live nature of the product, there was a risk that it could go bad during the maturation process. Bizzarri explained, “It’s a fermentation process so many things can go wrong. In normal conditions the quality of the cheese depends on the milk. Some bubbles could appear in the wheel after 3-4 months, that is really down to the milk, and therefore the producer.” The most serious problem was if the wheel began to swell, which could happen at the 4/5 month mark. Bizzarri said, “As a result you can have cracks, it’s visual. The cheese has a value close to zero.” In MGT the scrap rate was 1%, compared to an industry scrap rate of 2%. Bizzarri remarked, “On average the quality is quite high. The quality of the milk itself is strictly monitored by the producer. And the controls continue throughout the chain.” In the whole market 10% of cheese suffered some form of quality deterioration due to minor problems, which still allowed the cheese to be sold, but at an adjusted price according to its lower quality. In such a way producers generally lost another 3-4% of the value of their cheese production. Cheese testers tapped the cheese to check for tones that indicated that the cheese had matured as it should.21 If deemed necessary, a small sample was extracted using a screw needle as a further check.22

An inspection of each wheel using X-ray technology by the consortium took place after the minimum ageing period of 12 months. All wheels that passed the inspection received the firebranded mark of the consortium. Cheese that did not meet the PDO requirements was engraved with parallel lines to render it immediately recognizable by the consumer.23 (See Exhibit 8 for the commodity classification.) The strict PDO guidelines also extended to the packaging, grating, and portioning of Parmigiano Reggiano, which had to be carried out in the area of origin.

(c) Supply Chain

Overall, more than 50,000 people operated within the Parmigiano Reggiano supply chain that consisted of farmers, producers, wholesalers and retailers. It began with approximately 3,500 dairy farms, most of which were family owned and run. Farmers supplied producers with fresh milk on a daily basis.

Producers were single-product businesses,24 which had become more consolidated over time (the number of producers decreased by 43% from 1993 to 2008). In 2013, there were 373 qualified Parmigiano Reggiano producers, most of which were in fact limited liability co-operatives of farmers, including Lorenzo Verdi. On average, co-operatives had between 10 and 30 farmers, which owned the co-operative’s assets and shared its profits at the end of the year. Once the cheese had been produced, it generally remained in the producer’s possession until at least the 12-month maturation period when it could be sold to wholesalers. Very few producers carried out the maturation process in-house. They outsourced to operators such as specialized warehouses.

Wholesalers were industrial companies in the middle of the value chain. The largest operators easily exceeded €100 million turnover. The wholesaler continued the maturation process until the cheese was ready for sale25 to retail distributors (large-scale retail chains, small stores, and importers). It was quite common for the cheese to remain in the warehouse where it began the maturation process, even when it changed hands. Wholesalers had huge negotiating power with producers. Depending on the market conditions at the 12-month mark, the wholesaler could impose two or three additional months of maturation. Morlini said, “From the wholesaler’s perspective the Parmigiano Reggiano from one producer is the same as another. Therefore wholesalers start with a low price and set producers against each other.”

The majority of Parmigiano Reggiano cheese was finally sold to the consumer by large retailers, which used pricing and quality policies to attract customers and retain loyalty. They ultimately drove the retail price of the cheese. The remaining fraction was sold through specialty shops.26

Looking at the business model of the industry, it was clear that producers faced long cycles and working capital constraints, as a consequence of the lengthy maturing process. (See Exhibit 9 for stakeholders' key numbers and Exhibit 10 for Parmigiano Reggiano pricing through the chain.) Borrowing capacity was therefore a must. Local banks as well as bigger banks with a local presence were available to grant credit lines to Parmigiano Reggiano producers.

A Mature Attitude to Lending

Credem issued collateralized loans to producers at the euro interbank offered rate plus a spread that could vary from 100 to 300 basis points, depending on the standing of the borrower measured by its rating. Credit lines were revolving and were usually for one year, after which time the bank revised the line. The loan contract specified every aspect related to the value of the collateral, the insurance that had to be underwritten on the cheese, and all the procedures that had to be followed to ensure the quality of the collateral.

Credem valued the collateralized cheese at its current market price. The bank capped the loan amount at a fraction of the collateral value. The typical loan-to-value ratio was 70% to 80%. The resulting haircut of 20% to 30% protected the bank from extraordinary quality degradation and negative fluctuations of the market price.

To this end, if the market price of Parmigiano Reggiano fluctuated very unexpectedly and significantly in a short period of time, Credem performed checks to ensure that the loans issued were still covered by the collateral. It also carried out a programmed check every 12 months, as unexpected quality degradation or weight loss could also reduce the collateral value. Benatti said, “If you have the sense that the haircut isn’t going to protect you, you need to do a revision.” For that purpose, Credem routinely included loan covenants that required the value of the collateral, after a haircut, or margin requirement, was applied, to remain higher than the outstanding loan amount at all times.

At industry level, Credem’s Parmigiano Reggiano-backed loans, together with its warehouse approach, were similar to other competitors. Total industry loans to producers amounted to around €300 million, out of which approximately three quarters were collateralized by cheese in stock. Credem’s lending to subjects involved in the production and distribution of Parmigiano Reggiano cheese totaled approximately €100 million. This type of lending involved approximately 55 clients; out of these the two biggest accounted for a significant portion of the total. Storage of the cheese and the monitoring of the maturing process were managed by MGT, which played a critical role in supporting the bank’s lending practice.

Magazzini Generali delle Tagliate

MGT was the largest player in the sector with two warehouses in the heart of the official zone of origin of Parmigiano Reggiano that offered storage capacity for 440,000 wheels of cheese.27 The ability to store the pledged cheese in MGT provided multiple benefits to Credem. The location of the collateral was indeed an important aspect of secured lending. In early examples, such as mortgages, the collateral employed, for the purpose of the creditor, was stationary. A creditor that granted credit secured by inventory that was moveable property under the debtor’s control assumed additional risks.b Monitoring the value of the collateral became more challenging and costly.

The risks involved, even when a warehousing facility was used, were illustrated in a dramatic way by the so-called ‘Salad Oil Swindle of 1963’ in the U.S.: Tino De Angelis, a trader of soybean oil (an important ingredient in salad oil) futures, used his inventory of soybean oil as collateral to borrow large amounts from Wall Street banks, planning to use the cash to buy soybean oil futures. De Angelis deposited the oil with American Express’s warehousing subsidiary and, as time went on, he reduced the amount of oil he submitted as collateral. Inspectors sent to check the collateral were duped by tanks filled mostly with water and a minimum of oil floating on top, or pipes that connected the tanks and allowed oil to be transferred between tanks as officials carried out their inspection.28 When the scheme eventually unraveled the loss was over US$100 million in 1960s

Possession of the collateral allowed for immediate monitoring. Loans backed by collateral stored in a warehouse not owned by Credem were subject to a different loan contract. Benatti said, “There’s a big difference between working with a warehouse that you own and you know perfectly, in terms of quality control, insurance, and all the things associated with the maturation period, and working with an external warehouse. When we work with another warehouse we have to ask for a lot of additional documentation to certify that everything is done properly. If something goes wrong you have more trouble repossessing the cheese. When we work with the in-house warehouse we are in control and can be assured that the quality of the collateral, and therefore its value is protected.”

In addition to the security of holding the collateral in its possession, the interplay between the various elements of the value chain assured Credem that the product brought to the warehouse was of a very high quality. Quality control of the milk was carried out by the co-operatives, which made up the bulk of the warehouse’s customers. Morlini said, “It is of interest to the producer to analyze the milk that comes from every farm because if you mix poor quality milk from one farm with milk from another farm, the whole production is affected.” Credem worked only with authorized producers. Bizzarri said, “The producer has no interest in bringing cheese to the warehouse that is not of the highest quality. He has to pay a fee for the warehousing and he knows that we will inspect it, and if there is any problem we can sell the cheese. If the producer knows that a product is not of the highest quality he is better off keeping it in other facilities or in his own warehouse, and trying to sell it as soon as possible to a company specialized in selling such a product.” As an example, while at industry level 10% of the cheese suffered some sort of quality issues and needed to be downgraded during the maturation process, that figure was only 1% for MGT.

MGT’s warehouses offered excellent facilities: they had modern climatization installations that ensured consistent temperature and humidity. The possibility of customizing temperature for each individual storage unit within the facilities enabled customers to adapt time-humidity-temperature to their specific requirements, until the cheese had reached an ideal stage of maturity.30 MGT warehouses were also certified in quality management systems by an international body.31

Adding value MGT’s warehousing services came into existence to fill a void in the Parmigiano Reggiano supply chain. Bizzarri commented, “Back in the 50s, in this small area there were 1,800 cheese producers. Production was very low per producer and the fixed costs to build a high quality warehouse were too high for the production level they had. It was only the bank that had the critical mass to make that kind of investment. At the time it was meeting a social need. It was very much welcomed by cheese producers because they knew they could access a much better service than the one they could do in-house.”

MGT continued to add value to the chain by investing to improve the services it offered. Its technology included custom-built, specialized machinery that was programmed to move along the warehouse row by row, to turn and brush each wheel, taking three wheels at a time. The machinery reached heights that would not be possible with manual labor, and made the maximum utilization of the warehouse possible. Another machine cleaned the exterior of the wheel using a naked flame to give it a more attractive appearance. (See Exhibit 11 for photos of MGT’s custom-built machinery.)

Credem’s Unique Philosophy

Credem’s way of doing business in the Parmigiano Reggiano industry reflected many aspects of its unique business model. The bank focused not only on the product itself but also on the technology that surrounded its production. Bizzocchi elaborated, “It’s important to have a broader view when we approach a sector. We also try to develop our own technology that can be used in the production process and that is unique to us.”

Credem also invested in operational knowledge through its own research department, which focused on the main sectors in which the bank was involved. Its specialists in food and agricultural business performed analysis on the production chain of food products that were of interest to Credem, and constructed benchmarks and cash flow forecasts. Credem also organized conferences and meetings with producers and wholesalers of food products to gain a greater understanding of the market. The bank was also represented at various trade fairs. Bizzocchi said, “We went to Vinitaly, one of the most important wine expositions in Europe. There were not only the wine producers, but also the producers of all the technology and all the equipment that is needed to make wine, it was impressive.” For Credem these activities were a worthwhile investment. Bizzocchi said, “First of all, the cost is not excessive. Second, the cost of being left outside would be much higher. You need to understand the needs of your clients, and know what’s going on in the value chain in order to be able to serve all the subjects and to get value.”

In addition, the Parmigiano Reggiano value chain held potential to benefit from economies of scope. Bizzocchi said, “You have 400 cheese producers, for example, so that means that you also have much more than 400 families. We don’t only do lending, we also do banking and asset management, so our retail customers provide us with deposits and the money to be managed. If, like us, you are right in the middle, you have a 360 degree playing field and you can do a lot of things.”

Credem had begun to investigate applying its Parmigiano Reggiano model to other products in the agricultural sector. Bizzocchi said, “The Italian economy is made of districts and being in the center of a district is extremely important. We are very strong in the Parmigiano Reggiano area and we can use our national presence to export this approach to areas which have a tradition in certain agricultural products, such as Prosecco in the Veneto region. It’s a small part of our business but it’s still important for us that we have this level of expertise in the food industry and on top of that we have never lost a cent.” Credem was able to differentiate itself from larger competitors in its approach by offering more personalized products. Morlini said, “If you want a standardized product, there are plenty of banks which can eventually be more competitive than us, in terms of pricing. If you want to enter a new market, it is important to be flexible and to be able to design a product that meets the needs of the customer. We can do that because of the fact that we are not that big, we are not that small. It’s a sweet spot.”

Credem recently received a request from a wine producer for a loan backed by Brunello, a wine made exclusively from grapes grown on the slopes of a hilltop village in the region of Tuscany.32 However, because of its particularities, it was unclear whether the product lent itself as well to the warehouse model. Bizzocchi said, “From a product perspective, it’s quite similar to Parmigiano Reggiano, it has a long cycle—at least 5 years—and it has quite a high unit value. But it is very difficult to monitor the quality. You can’t smell it or tap it. If you open a bottle to check the quality you can't sell that bottle.” Before assessing this possibility though, Benatti had to resolve the issues with the Lorenzo Verdi Parmigiano Reggiano producer.

To Liquidate Or Not Liquidate?

Benatti, Bizzarri and Ferrari had convened at MGT headquarters to discuss the question of the collateral that was backing Lorenzo Verdi’s loan. (See Exhibit 14 for details of the loan status.)

Lorenzo Verdi had been a customer of Credem for the past 15 years. The co-operative experienced plenty of financial difficulties during the financial crisis, missing several scheduled loan payments to Credem. Bankruptcy was only averted by supplementing the loan collateral base with additional freshly-produced cheese. Since then, Lorenzo Verdi had made numerous changes to its operations and infrastructure in order to cut costs. The most recent one, almost one year ago, was the installation of a new energy efficient air-conditioning system in its facility where the cheese was stored for the first two months of the maturing process, prior to being shipped to MGT.

New problems for Lorenzo Verdi surfaced only a couple of weeks ago. The producer’s pledged cheese was almost two months away from receiving the consortium’s certification, when Bizzari warned Benatti of the results of a routine visual inspection at MGT: the quality of the producer’s cheese in stock appeared to be below standard. Having been already concerned by the recent plunge of the market price, Benatti had no other choice but to raise the alarm, as it was clear that the collateral value was insufficient to support the loan. Credem asked Lorenzo Verdi to provide additional cheese or cash. However, the co-operative responded that it would be unable to honor such a request in the foreseeable future, due to the recently undertaken capital expenditures. This further cast severe doubts on its ability to repay the loan.

Benatti was contemplating two options: either force the producer into bankruptcy in order to seize and liquidate the collateral, or take no action at that point. Forcing the producer into bankruptcy would allow Credem to sell the collateral at the current market price. The process would also involve litigation fees of approximately €35,000, however, Benatti was most concerned about the potential repercussions to the bank’s image and reputation.

To make a decision, Benatti needed also to assess the future prospects of the loan in case he decided to take no action. To this end, he had asked Bizzarri and Ferrari to conduct a thorough analysis of the collateral’s current valuation, as well as forecasts for the future. Among other things, the forecasts needed to account for the upcoming consortium inspection for the cheese to receive the fire-branded mark. A government intervention was also on the table, as numerous other producers were facing financial difficulties due to the Russian embargo and the plummeting market price. Ferrari said, “We have seen the government step in, in the past, to help producers.” The consortium expected the government to clarify its stance sometime between January and February of 2015. (See Exhibit 15 for the analysis by Bizzarri and Ferrari.)

Benatti remained unsure; he knew that it was a big decision to pull the plug on the co-operative, especially given the bank’s local image, but at the same time his credit department had a responsibility to ensure that the bank managed its risk. What was the best course of action?

Exhibit 8 Commodity Classification of Cheese

1. “First-grade” Parmigiano Reggiano This qualification shall be given to those wheels that are free from any external and internal defects (size, rind, hammer, screw needle, paste texture, aroma, flavor), which can be detected both visually and by a screw needle and hammer inspection.

2. “Zero (0) and One (1)” Parmigiano Reggiano This qualification shall include: a) zero: wheels that, although meeting the specifications for first-grade cheese, have surface splits on the rind, small erosions, slightly worn edges and some limited rectifications that, however, do not cause wheel deformation. b) one: wheels having slight texture anomalies, specifically:
- one or two holes (round and oblong cavities that have formed in the paste) with diameters not exceeding 3-4cm and upon condition that no aroma defect are found with the probe inspection;
- localized hornet’s nest (area of spongy paste) of a few centimeters without any aroma defects;
- a few “fish mouths”, i.e. oblong holes, not exceeding 3-4cm in size;
- light shivers, formed by some slits in the paste, not exceeding 3-4cm in length;
- widely-spaced and not excessively recurrent small holes; -so-called “loose” wheels, i.e. wheels that have a dull/hollow sound when tapped with the hammer.

3. “Medium-grade” Parmigiano Reggiano This category shall include wheels having:
- holes exceeding 3-4cm in diameter without any aroma defects;
- hornet’s nests without any aroma defects;
- small holes spread throughout the wheel (glossy, round small holes, small-medium in diameter);
- some slits and splits arranged horizontally;
- horizontal slits and splits located near one of the wheel faces and/or concerning part of the sides;
- rectifications on the sides or faces without any aroma defects, which have been well made, and of such a size that they do not significantly affect the external appearance of the wheel.

4. “Downgraded” cheese This category shall include wheels having:
- very evident swelling of the wheel faces;
- spongy paste with large and widespread holes;
- multiple and widespread horizontal slits in a folded configuration;
- large slits and splits scattered on most of the wheel;
- large cavity in the centre or around the centre having a circular or oblong shape, with or without spongy paste;
- deep and extensive rectifications to the sides and/or faces;
- wheels with evident aroma defects.

5. “Reject” cheese This category shall include all those wheels characterized by the presence of a number of serious defects, that is to say all those wheels that cannot be included in the above categories, due to their quality.

Exhibit 15 Lorenzo Verdi Collateral Valuation

Visual inspection of the collateral raised some concerns about the collateral’s quality. Based on current market price and the visual inspection, its market valuation is €6.67/kg. We expect this valuation to remain stable, until we revise it in early December, when the inspection using X-ray technology is due.

The most likely outcome of the inspection would be that the quality turns out to be as we anticipated, in which case the valuation would remain unchanged. With probability 20%, the cheese quality might be higher than expected, a scenario that would lead to a valuation of €7.41/kg. However, with probability 20%, the cheese quality might be in fact worse, leading to a valuation of €5.93/kg.

After the inspection takes place, we anticipate the collateral valuation to remain stable, until the government reveals its plans on whether it takes action or not. Based on historical observations, we anticipate a sizeable and immediate market reaction to the news: an intervention, which will occur with probability 40%, would drive prices up by 12.5%, whereas a lack thereof would likely drive prices down by 9%.
