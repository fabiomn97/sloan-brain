JetBlue Airways: Deicing at Logan Airport

Ian Deason looked out of his office at the tarmac of Boston Logan International Airport.  Just a month earlier, in July 2010, he had taken over as JetBlue Airways’ Director of Airport Operations for Logan.  Although it was a bright, summer day, snow and ice were at the top of Deason’s mind. Multiple recent discussions with Mike Parkinson, JetBlue’s Director of Ground Services, had focused on JetBlue’s deicing process at Logan Airport.  Poor weather during the prior winter had led to significant challenges, with JetBlue being forced to cancel or delay a significant number of flights due to a lack of deicing capacity.  Boston was one of JetBlue’s five focus cities and served approximately 60 departures a day in the winter of 2009 - 2010.  The effects of these delays and cancellations were thus felt throughout JetBlue’s flight network.  It was clear something needed to be done – and quickly
- to address this issue for the coming winter.

Multiple options had been proposed for addressing the deicing capacity issues.  The most promising options involved the use of “off-gate” deicing at one of two airport tarmac locations (also known as pads): (1) the Juliet pad, which was shared with other airlines but was available with no additional cost or (2) the Earhart pad, where JetBlue could lease space from Logan and develop its own deicing operation. Exhibit 1 provides a map of the south cargo area at Logan airport, highlighting each of these options.

JetBlue Airways

JetBlue was founded by David Neeleman in 1999 after he had established a strong record as a leader in the airline industry.  A key aspect of JetBlue’s operating system was its modified point-topoint route structure.  The point-to-point model was pioneered by Southwest Airlines, the largest of the so-called low-cost carriers (LCCs).

JetBlue entered the market by connecting large, typically northeastern, U.S. cities (e.g., New York) with warmer cities in the southeast (e.g., Ft. Lauderdale, Florida).  Starting with just 10 airplanes in 2000, the company achieved major-airline status in 2004 by exceeding $1 billion in annual revenue.1 After taking over as JetBlue’s CEO in 2007, Dave Barger’s goal was consistent and sustainable growth.  By 2009, JetBlue had grown to serving 23 million passengers annually through a network of flights out of 60 cities on 151 planes—110 Airbus A320s and 41 Embraer E190s.2  Exhibit 2 provides information on financial performance and selected operating statistics for JetBlue from 2005 to 2009.

Unlike the large, legacy carriers,a JetBlue focused primarily on direct passengers, with just 11% of passengers flying on multiple-leg itineraries in 2009 (i.e., routes with one or more stops).  In lieu of the “hub-and-spoke” strategy utilized by these competitors, JetBlue maintained five focus cities, with most of its flights operating into or out of one of these cities.  As one of these five focus cities, Boston had played a significant role in JetBlue’s growth strategy to date.  Out of Boston, JetBlue offered nonstop service to 33 destinations and connecting service to another 11 destinations.

JetBlue at Logan

Boston Logan International Airport

The first plane landed at Boston Airport on June 13, 1923.b  At the time, the airport had a single cinder runway and resided on 189 acres of tidal flats adjacent to Boston Harbor.  Between 1923 and 2010, the airport was expanded four times by filling in portions of the harbor (in 1929, 1941, 1965, and 1974).  As a result of these expansions, the by-then renamed Logan Airport was surrounded by the harbor on three sides and part of a fourth (Exhibit 3).  Combined with the continued growth in flight and passenger demand, this led to an extremely tight operating environment with little excess room for maneuvering or holding airplanes.  As of 2010, Logan operated four terminals, three for domestic flights (Terminals A, B, and C) and one for a mix of domestic and international flights (Terminal E). 3

As with many airports in the U.S., Logan offered airport gates for lease according to a variety of contract options, each of which stipulated utilization requirements (i.e., gates would be offered to competitors if they were not sufficiently utilized by the controlling airline).  After leasing a gate, an airline would need to invest in systems and facilities to support its operations within the corresponding terminal.  Additionally, to facilitate passenger connections, it was desirable for all of an airline’s gates to be in relatively close proximity.  Because of these two concerns, each of the airlines at Logan chose to operate their domestic arrivals and departures from within a single terminal (e.g., Delta and Continental in Terminal A; US Airways and American in Terminal B; and JetBlue and AirTran in Terminal C).

JetBlue’s Growth at Logan

By 2010, JetBlue had grown into the largest carrier at Boston Logan based on the number of destinations served and seats offered for sale.4  Exhibit 4 provides information on JetBlue’s annual operating statistics at Logan from 2005 through 2009.  Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 provide a typical daily departure and arrival schedule from the winter of 2009 – 2010.

JetBlue was planning for continued growth at Logan in 2010 and subsequent years.  For the winter of 2010 - 2011, JetBlue was scheduled to operate approximately 80 departures per day up from 60 in 2009 and peaking around 90 in the February-to-March timeframe.  As a service-oriented company, JetBlue depended on being able to meet this growth without incurring excessive cancellations or delays.  In particular, during winter operations, JetBlue set the goal of consistently meeting or exceeding its departure capacity as assigned by air traffic control.  In the presence of inclement weather, air traffic control would slow the rate of departures and arrivals to meet airplane spacing requirements, ensuring safe operations. Winter Operations

Winter weather, especially freezing rain, snow and other icy conditions could wreak havoc on an airline’s departure schedule.  One of the primary reasons was federal regulation surrounding deicing strategies.  Ice buildup on an airplane’s wing was recognized by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) as a serious safety concern, with the potential to upset the aerodynamics of the plane. Depending on the specific weather conditions, an airline was required to treat each departing plane with a combination of Type I (deicing) and Type IV (anti-icing) fluids.  Further increasing these operational challenges, the FAA defined strict holdover limits specifying the maximum amount of time an airplane was allowed to wait after being treated for ice and prior to taking off.

In fair weather, turning an airplane (i.e., readying an arriving airplane for a subsequent departure) was a complex, time-sensitive procedure that required the coordination of many functions, from the handling of baggage, food, and waste to the management of passengers and crew (both arriving and departing).  During the turn process, the airplane needed to remain at the gate so that arriving passengers could deplane and departing passengers could board.  JetBlue operated two types of planes, each with a different target turn time.  For the 150-seat Airbus A320, JetBlue aimed to turn the airplane in 40 minutes.  For the 100-seat Embraer 190, JetBlue aimed for a 30 minute turn time. Without any outside considerations, JetBlue was able to meet these timeframes around 90% of the time.  Additionally, a small amount of padding would typically be built into the schedule to ensure sufficient time for the airplane turn, but when this padding was exceeded, delays would result for both the turning airplane and any airplane waiting to use the gate.  For both 2009 and 2010, due to JetBlue’s rapid growth at Logan, the turn process represented a particularly important challenge.  As of July 2010, JetBlue operated roughly 75 departures per day out of 11 gates.  Exhibit 8 provides the layout of JetBlue’s gates in Terminal C as of July 2010.  In comparison, US Airways operated roughly 50 departures per day out of 17 gates.

Deicing added another layer of complexity to the process of preparing an airplane for departure. Deicing and anti-icing were performed using two to four deicing vehicles (deicers) per plane (see Exhibit 9) with each deicer staffed by three to four people.  At Logan, airlines could choose to perform deicing either on-gate or off-gate.  In on-gate deicing, after the airplane was turned and the doors were shut, the plane would be pushed back 15 feet from the gate (2 minutes).  At this point, the deicers would position themselves around the airplane while the plane was configured for deicing (3 minutes).  The time taken for deicing depended on the weather conditions and the number of deicers treating the plane.  Exhibit 10 lists the deicing times for a team of three deicers operating under four categories of weather conditions – frost, light to moderate snow, heavy snow / freezing rain, and ice pellets – as well as the number of days classified in each category during the winter of 2009 - 2010. After completing deicing, the deicers would pull back from the plane (1 minute), and the flight would be free to proceed to the runway for departure.

In off-gate deicing, the deicers would be positioned in stations in a separate deicing area outside of the gate area.  After leaving the gate, each airplane would be directed to one of these stations. Once the plane reached the station, deicing would follow the same process as above, starting with the deicers positioning themselves around the plane.  For both on-gate and off-gate deicing, time spent deicing an airplane typically translated into departure delays, because deicing could not be performed until after the airplane departed from the gate.  At Logan, JetBlue operated 14 deicers, although at any time only 12 machines could be expected to be active due to anticipated breakdowns and maintenance requirements.  Purchasing a new deicer would have cost JetBlue approximately $250,000.

Options for Off-Gate Deicing

During the winter of 2009 - 2010, JetBlue performed all of its deicing operations on-gate, but with 12 active deicers, it was able to release just four flights per hour under moderate snowfall.  As a result, JetBlue faced excessive delays and cancellations.  From November 2009 through March 2010, JetBlue’s Logan departures faced an average departure delay of about 10 minutes, with 230 flights canceled (representing 2.5% of the total schedule).  Each schedule disruption forced JetBlue to apply costly recovery actions to ensure that aircraft were appropriately positioned for future operations and that crew work limits were satisfied.  Particularly troubling was that in the winter of 2009 - 2010 JetBlue had been unable to meet its internal goal of zero cancellations due to light snow.d  Thus, for the winter of 2010 - 2011, Deason and Norbert Strissel, JetBlue’s General Manager at Logan, had agreed that the airline would need to move some of its deicing operations off-gate.  At least one team of deicers would need to be reserved for on-gate operations to provide flexibility for time- or fuelcritical flights.e  Two options were being considered.  The first was to operate two deicing stations using shared deicing space provided by the airport at the Juliet pad, while keeping two teams ongate.  The second option was to lease a separate space from the airport at the Earhart pad where the airline could operate three deicing stations, with just one team remaining on-gate.  Exhibit 3 provides a map of Logan airport, highlighting both the Juliet and Earhart pads.

Juliet Pad

Because of the limited space available at Logan, the airport provided shared tarmac space for airlines to use for off-gate deicing.  There was no charge for airlines to use the space, which was sufficient to accommodate 5 deicing locations (marked as J1 through J5 in Exhibit 1).  These locations were assigned to airlines on the day of operations based on requests, flight frequency, and need. Based on the scale of its operations, JetBlue could count on reserving two of these locations. Additionally, the Juliet pad provided space for three shared hold locations (marked as H1, H2, and H3 in Exhibit 1) where planes would wait before being deiced.  The assignment of a deicing location to an airline lasted for the full day, so JetBlue was able to setup its deicing stations prior to its first flight departure and operate them through its last departure of the day.

Earhart Pad

Alternatively, working with Vincent Cardillo, Massport’s Deputy Director Aviation Operationsf, JetBlue had discussed the potential of utilizing space in an unused passenger terminal near the Juliet pad.  Logan airport was willing to lease this space to any airline, provided that the airline would develop the space (at a cost of approximately $160,000)  and sign a 3-year lease at a cost of $535,000 per year, roughly the same cost as leasing one airport gate for a year.  JetBlue had developed a proposal for operating three private deicing stations (marked as D1, D2, and D3 in Exhibit 1) along with two hold locations (marked as OD4 and OD5) in this space.  One challenge with this proposal would be convincing JetBlue executives to invest in space owned by the airport.

The Decision

Deason needed to quickly make a decision about which option to select and present to JetBlue’s executive team.  It was clear that control of the Earhart pad would be operationally advantageous, but was the benefit enough to justify the cost?
