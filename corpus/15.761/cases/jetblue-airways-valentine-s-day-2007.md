JetBlue Airways: Valentine’s Day 2007

On February 14, 2007, JetBlue Airways filed its requisite Form 10-K for 2006 with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. That same day, Goldman Sachs updated JetBlue’s stock to a “buy” rating. It was business as usual for a company that had garnered consistently high rankings in customer satisfaction since its founding in 1999 on the principle of “bringing humanity back to air travel.” No one expected that a day that began with such normalcy would launch the company into what CEO David Neeleman would later call “the worst operational week in JetBlue’s seven year history.”1

Early that morning, the National Weather Service predicted snow turning to rain for the New York City area. At John F. Kennedy (JFK) International Airport in New York—Jet Blue’s largest location in terms of both incoming and outbound flights—thousands of JetBlue customers were boarding their flights, many bound for warmer climes such as Aruba and Cancun. It was the Wednesday before President’s Day, and the number of leisure travelers was particularly large given the upcoming three-day weekend and the fact that it would soon be school vacation week in many eastern states. As a result, JetBlue’s JFK flights were heavily booked to a load factor of 94%.2

JetBlue’s planes were loaded and ready to fly, but the weather was not acting as predicted. The snow turned to ice instead of rain, prohibiting takeoffs. Nonetheless, JetBlue staff continued loading flights and allowing them to taxi toward their runways in anticipation of the predicted changeover to rain. As the air turned colder, however, it became clear that the rain was not coming. Runways at JFK were freezing over, making it difficult to get planes back to their gates. Further, because inbound flights continued to land at JFK, there were no unoccupied gates to which the delayed outbound flights could return. At JFK, nine JetBlue flights spent six hours waiting on the tarmac because they could not get to gates.3 By the end of the day, only 17 of JetBlue’s 156 scheduled JFK departures had taken off.4 The resulting disruption to JetBlue’s operations left crews and aircraft out of position, a

JetBlue Airways

Neeleman founded JetBlue Airways in 1999, having previously managed a small budget airline called Morris Air—which he later sold to Southwest Airlines—in his home state of Utah. From its inception, JetBlue was a growth company that aimed to use low fares to stimulate demand for air travel among customers who might otherwise have chosen bus or train transport. Low operating costs, resulting from efficient scheduling of aircraft and low distribution costs, allowed JetBlue to offer lower-than-average fares to typically high-priced destinations, such as resort areas.10 In addition, JetBlue offered amenities such as leather seats and individual televisions with DirectTV® programming.

As of February 2007, JetBlue served 50 destinations in 21 states, Puerto Rico, Mexico, and the Caribbean, with 98% of flights originating or terminating in one of five “focus cities”: New York, Los Angeles (Long Beach), Boston, Ft. Lauderdale, and Washington, D.C. (see Exhibit 1 for the cities served by JetBlue and route maps for selected locations). By the end of 2006, JetBlue—which had 10,634 employees, 8,393 full time and 2,231 part time11—was the eighth-largest passenger carrier in the U.S. based on passenger revenue miles.12

A key aspect of JetBlue’s operating system was its modified point-to-point route structure. Pioneered by Southwest Airlines, the largest of the so-called low-cost carriers (LCCs), the point-topoint model offered direct flights between selected cities served by the airline and prevented passengers traveling between those cities from having to change planes en route. This model differed from the “hub-and-spoke” route structures that were used by most major carriers—also known as legacy carriers—such as American Airlines, United Airlines, and Delta Air Lines. “Ice-Pellet” Regulations

Weather, especially freezing rain, snow, and other icy conditions, could wreak havoc with any airport’s departure scheduling. One of the main reasons planes sat on the tarmac in inclement winter weather was regulation surrounding de-icing strategies. On February 14, the East Coast was pummeled by ice pellets, officially defined as chunks of frozen water that begin as snow but—upon descending through a band of warmer air—melt and refreeze to become pellets of ice. According to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), these pellets could stick to an airplane’s wings, upsetting the aerodynamics of the plane and causing numerous safety hazards. The FAA had various regulations related to airline flights in the presence of ice pellets; these regulations had gone through several iterations over time, resulting in some ambiguity surrounding the exact definition of unsafe flying conditions.

Until 2004, pilots had been allowed to visually inspect their airplane’s wings and determine whether it was safe to take off. A chemical de-icing spray, followed by a second pilot inspection, was considered sufficient to clear a plane for takeoff. During 2004 and 2005, however, the FAA decided that research had shown the human eye to be “very poor” in detecting ice formation and human touch as “only marginally better.”20 In October 2005, the FAA formally banned takeoffs in the presence of falling ice pellets. When the airline industry sent up a chorus of protest, the FAA relaxed its rules slightly, but vaguely, saying that pilots would be allowed to “sometimes make their own calls.”21

This nonuniform enforcement of the standards led to mass confusion and frustration among carriers and passengers alike during sleety conditions. That the ice-pellet rules did not apply to international flights further compounded this confusion. “A number of carriers kept operating while we were sitting there telling passengers we couldn’t fly,” said one US Airways pilot and director of technical flight operations early in the winter of 2005.22 The carriers violating the FAA warning were made to look like heroes, while those who adhered to it angered passengers.

Crisis and Response

JetBlue’s crisis on February 14 by no means represented the first time weather created major operational difficulties for a U.S. airline. Since 2002, both the percentage of flights delayed and average length of delay had increased (Exhibit 7). On December 20, 2006, a blizzard at Colorado’s Denver International Airport stranded 5,000 people for several days. The following week, an American Airlines flight was stranded for nine hours on the tarmac at Austin, Texas, due to a thunderstorm-related closure of the Dallas-Fort Worth, Texas airport. On the same day, Southwest Airlines diverted 11 flights to Austin, Texas, where nine of them experienced on-board ground delays of between 60 and 90 minutes.24

Further, JetBlue was not the only airline that experienced significant delays during the February 14 storm. That day, 97% of all scheduled flights at JFK were either delayed, cancelled, or diverted, with over 83% of those flights cancelled. Delta had more flights delayed at JFK than JetBlue, with 15 arriving flights and 39 departing flights delayed over 60 minutes. American Airlines had 15 arrival delays and 8 departure delays over 60 minutes. JetBlue’s average delay length, however, was the longest of the major carriers (Exhibit 8). Delay times for most major airlines were all elevated over the period between February 13 and February 15, 2007; the average delays for flights from Delta Air Lines, American Airlines, and JetBlue were 205 minutes, 202 minutes, and 230 minutes, respectively, according to FlightStats.25

Dealing with the Storm

On February 14, other carriers at JFK decided early in the day to cancel their flights (see Exhibit 9 for data on weather-related cancellations by airline for the period from February 13 through February 15). JetBlue continued loading its planes to prepare for takeoff, in hopes that the storm would clear. When the weather did not improve, JetBlue was stuck with a substantial backlog of fully loaded airplanes, and an overwhelming amount of reservations activity clogged JetBlue’s phone lines and Web site. While most other airlines were able to resume normal operations one or two days later,26 JetBlue’s problems continued beyond the storm.

Flight operations JetBlue used SkySolver, a tracking system from Minneapolis-based Navitaire Open Skies, to adjust the routing of planes affected by service disruptions. During the storm, managers plugged various scenarios into SkySolver, such as flight cancellations and crew re-assignments, and the system calculated which combination of actions would minimize disruption to passengers and get operations back to normal as quickly as possible.27 In the early hours of February 14, planners used SkySolver to develop contingency plans to get as many passengers as possible to their destinations in spite of the inclement weather. Typically, these planners transferred the SkySolver information into a Sabre Airline Solutions application that translated the data into usable flight plans that could be shared across the company. Customer relations From the outset of the crisis, Neeleman accepted responsibility. At the end of the day on February 14, he announced that passengers delayed on board an aircraft for more than three hours would receive a full refund and a voucher for a free round-trip flight.32 This offer exceeded the future reparations afforded passengers by the Customer Bill of Rights, which would not be publicized until February 21. Neeleman further addressed customers’ complaints by announcing on February 15 that customers booked through that day could rebook a flight to the same destination for travel through March 20, 2007, without being subject to change fees or fare differences. Later that same day, a press release from JetBlue expanded that provision, stating that customers booked through February 19 could rebook without penalty through May 22, 2007, or request a full refund of their fares.

On February 16, JetBlue announced that customers booked through February 19 would receive the full benefits of the rebooking policy without having to contact the airline prior to the originally scheduled departure. The Customer Bill of Rights Also on February 21, Neeleman unveiled the company’s Customer Bill of Rights. The Bill of Rights was made retroactive to February 14, so that those directly affected by the Valentine’s Day storm would be covered under its provisions. It delineated JetBlue’s responsibilities to its customers in the following areas: information sharing, cancellations, departure delays, overbookings, and on-board ground delays for arrivals and departures (Exhibit 11).

The Bill of Rights used very specific language to explain the extent of JetBlue’s accountability for changes to flight schedules, categorizing schedule interruptions as either uncontrollable or controllable irregularities. In the glossary of terms JetBlue included with the Bill of Rights on its Web site,36 an uncontrollable irregularity was defined as a delay, cancellation, or diversion caused by a force majeure event (i.e., “an event beyond the reasonable control of JetBlue, such as weather conditions; acts of government or airport authorities; runway closures; airport construction that interfered with JetBlue’s operation and other events that were not reasonably foreseen, predicted, or anticipated by JetBlue.”)37

A controllable irregularity was defined as any delay, cancellation, or diversion that was not caused by a force majeure event. Controllable irregularities included crew unavailability due to JetBlue’s scheduling (not due to weather-event-related disruption), delay or cancellation due to maintenance, and “that which [was] considered reasonably within JetBlue’s control.”38 JetBlue took full responsibility for controllable irregularities. If—due to a controllable irregularity—a flight was cancelled within 12 hours of scheduled takeoff time, passengers were able to have their ticket refunded or rebooked and to also receive an additional voucher for future JetBlue travel in the amount paid for the original round-trip ticket.

JetBlue also planned to compensate customers for time spent in ground delays. A ground delay was “a delay where, upon departure from the gate, the aircraft [was] delayed from taking off; or upon arrival, the aircraft [was] delayed from arriving at a gate or hardstand/air stairs for safe deplaning.”39 In the case of arrivals, if a flight landed early (prior to scheduled arrival), delay time was not calculated until after the scheduled arrival time. JetBlue’s Customer Bill of Rights was unique among commercial carriers. The idea of protecting passengers, however, was not. The entire airline industry had been under scrutiny since December 2006 for a spate of delays and cancellations, prompting Congress to act. In both the U.S. House and Senate, legislators introduced bills that, if approved, would require airlines to provide food and water to passengers stuck in a closed aircraft and would allow passengers to disembark a plane grounded for more than three hours. Following the Valentine’s Day storm, U.S. Secretary of Transportation Mary Peters conducted an inquiry into airlines’ contingency planning in hopes of understanding what combination of factors leads to extensive delays such as those experienced by JetBlue’s customers.40

Cost As of February 20, Neeleman believed JetBlue would spend about $10 million in refunds to customers and about $16 million in credit vouchers.41 An additional $4 million of “incremental expenses”—overtime for crews and the cost of chartering airplanes and buses to move stranded customers—brought the financial impact to $30 million.42

Changes to JetBlue’s Operating System

Though the Customer Bill of Rights was the most immediate aspect of JetBlue’s response to the crisis, several interviews with Neeleman in subsequent days suggested that the airline had larger operational issues to address, particularly in the areas of human resource management and information systems.

Pilot and crew tracking The crisis exposed the company’s “shoestring communications system,”43 which failed to connect JetBlue’s operational staff to the airline’s pilots and flight attendants in a timely manner. Neeleman admitted that JetBlue had outgrown its logistics systems and was not able to track the location of pilots and flight crew adequately during the crisis.44 “When one thing, like the [displacement of] crew services thing, gets overwhelmed, it just has a trickle-down effect,”45 said Neeleman. He also noted, “We had so many people in the company who wanted to help who weren’t trained to help. We had an emergency control center full of people who didn’t know what to do. I had flight attendants sitting in hotel rooms for three days who couldn’t get ahold of us. I had pilots emailing me saying, ‘I’m available, what do I do?’”46

JetBlue used Sabre Airline Solutions’ CrewTrac application to track crew assignments and ensure that legal work-limit requirements were met. Pilots and flight attendants logged in to a secure Web portal to access their schedules. There was not, however, a system in place to track off-duty crews. In the past, during emergencies, off-duty crews called JetBlue headquarters stating their location and their availability to work. Mees, JetBlue’s CIO, joined the company in November 2006. During the storm and the ensuing crisis, Mees and his team managed barely an hour of sleep each night as they scrambled to support the airline’s operations. Within 24 hours of the beginning of the storm, Mees’s technology team pieced together a simple database that could track crew locations and their contact information, using a Microsoft SQL server database and .NET tools.48 Following the crisis, Mees decided to keep this system in place and devote resources to developing it further, adding capabilities such as remote data input, whereby pilots and crews would be able to type in their location and availability via Internetenabled devices.

Reservation system Both the technological and human components of JetBlue’s reservation system were overwhelmed by the volume of customer correspondence related to flight cancellations. As customers learned of flight cancellations, they called JetBlue’s reservation line. This was, in fact, their only option for rebooking, as JetBlue had no way for customers to process these requests online or via automated airport kiosks. Mees had been working on a Web-based rebooking system since December 2006, but it was not yet available at the time of the storm.

JetBlue used a Navitaire Open Skies reservation system with a Web portal that could be accessed remotely by its reservation agents working from home. The system was set up to accommodate up to 650 agents at one time, a number that had proved to be adequate under normal operating conditions. As the crisis began, Mees put in an urgent call to Navitaire, asking them to increase the number of agents that could use the system simultaneously. Navitaire was able to expand the maximum number to 950, but beyond that the system was gridlocked. Even with this expanded number of Navitaire slots, JetBlue had difficulty getting additional agents to staff its reservation line during the crisis, as no more than 500 were officially scheduled to work at any one time.49

Neeleman admitted that lean staffing was a culprit, conceding that he would have to adjust the work contracts of reservation agents to require them to work longer hours during busy periods. He continued:

We have also made provisions with our folks out there and come up with a new program whereby we think we will be able to flex up much more effectively on staffing. . . . We think that we can double the number of people that are on the phone up to maybe 800 that can be able to process and take care of customers and really accommodate them on another flight. So that was another area of concern, and we’re addressing that very aggressively with our leadership team out in Salt Lake City, a great group of people out there that are very committed to serving customers.50

Mees set a goal of working with Navitaire to increase the number of agents who could simultaneously use the reservation system to 1,300 by August 2007.51

The Future

Between human resource constraints and technological shortcomings, Neeleman had much to figure out to make sure this type of operational crisis did not strike JetBlue again. Both Neeleman and Mees concurred that JetBlue’s software providers were not to blame, but there were clear areas in which JetBlue could augment its systems.

One of Mees’s first priorities was to get the Web-based rebooking system online so that customers could simply rebook flights themselves in the wake of mass cancellations. This Web functionality would allow passengers to move to another flight or obtain refunds based on the terms of the Customer Bill of Rights.55 Finally, Mees wanted to implement new systems that would allow JetBlue to notify passengers by e-mail, phone, or announcements on its Web site when flights were cancelled or changed.56

In late 2008, JetBlue planned to open a new $740 million terminal at JFK Airport. In an effort to reduce wait times at the airport, ticket counters were to be replaced with as many as 200 computer kiosks where passengers could do their own ticketing and baggage check. Customers would also be able to rebook flights and file lost-baggage claims at these kiosks.

As JetBlue wrestled with its own issues, vendors were also hurrying to develop new and improved solutions for airlines. Sabre Airline Systems, for example, launched a new system in 2006 that allowed operators to figure out the best alternative to a cancelled flight for a single passenger and then automatically rebook it. “An airline can now re-accommodate all the passengers on a fully loaded jumbo jet in five minutes—a process that in the past took hours,” said Ilia Kostov, vice president of product management and marketing for Sabre.57
