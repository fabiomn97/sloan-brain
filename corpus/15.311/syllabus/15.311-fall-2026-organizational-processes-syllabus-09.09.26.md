15.311: ORGANIZATIONAL PROCESSES Fall 2026

COURSE SUMMARY

Before coming to Sloan, it is likely that your success depended on your own individual performance. When you leave Sloan, you will be leading teams, divisions, and organizations, and your success will depend on your ability to lead others. The OP course will give you a set of tools for managing and leading people.

Your ability to lead begins with your development of multiple perspectives on organizations. As humans, we habitually hold particular mental models of how the world works; filters determining what we pay attention to and what we ignore. These habits offer powerful economies of thought. Without them, simple tasks like listening to the radio while driving would be impossible. But they impose costs as well. They lock us into a single view of the world that may not be advantageous and is likely to soon become outdated.

Leading people and organizations involves trading off economy of thought for creativity of thought. Accordingly, we will learn to analyze situations and craft implementation plans using three "lenses" on organizations—structural design, political, and cultural. While leading and managing others always presents challenges, our goal in this course is to teach you to use the three lenses of structural design (division of labor, goals, incentives), politics (resources, power, and status), and culture (beliefs, values, and identities) to understand these challenges and craft implementation plans that address them so that you can be effective leaders.

This course is designed to build both the breadth and depth of your leadership toolkit. We begin with the macro organizational processes shaped by structural design, politics, and culture. We then shift to the micro foundations of work—how people make decisions in groups, perform as teams, negotiate, and build coalitions—before turning to one of the defining leadership challenges of our time: guiding organizations and their employees through technological change. Throughout, you will learn to use the three lenses to understand and influence the processes that shape the fabric of organizational life.

2) Why We Want You to Actively Participate: Class Participation

Learning is a collaborative enterprise. You are not here to receive learning, but to claim it through your active participation. Course concepts require you to unpack a story, dynamic, or pattern to identify the underlying principles that generalize to other settings. This is why the class is interactive and discussion-driven: it is through class discussion that you can identify and internalize the key principles. You will practice speaking in public. You will find yourself presenting and testing new ideas that are not wholly formulated and assisting others in shaping their ideas as well. You should be prepared to take some risks and be supportive of the efforts of others. This class may be different from other Sloan classes in that there is rarely one correct answer, and part of the learning is in discussing multiple perspectives. We expect you to attend class on time, be prepared—including a preparation of the discussion questions related to each session so you can share insights in class—and be an active and thoughtful participant. Participation will be graded both on quality—how your insight deepens discussion—and quantity. Missing classes can significantly impact your performance in the course.

If you have a personal or medical issue that is impacting your ability to attend class, complete work, or take an exam, please discuss this with your program advisor along with notifying your instructors and TAs. The Student Resources tab on the course Canvas site holds information on academic and personal support services and resources at MIT.

Class participation is 40% of your final grade.
