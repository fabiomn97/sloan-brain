Galbraith and Company By Don Moore

Historically, partners have operated with a great deal of independence within the firm. For instance, partners control the fates of employees who report to them. The firm cannot force partners to transfer or promote consultants on their teams whom they do not want to let go. Each partner therefore has the power to hold on to their best people as long as they wish.  These norms are important in helping to establish cooperation among powerful senior partners by making it impossible for them to “raid” each others’ groups. Instead, partners must work together to re assign employees when it is time to do so.

General Information Galbraith & Company is a professional services firm founded in 1968 by D. R. Galbraith, who left a professorial position at a `major business school to found the firm in the relatively new niche that has now become the field of management consulting.  Prof. Galbraith developed the firm into a prosperous and profitable consulting firm. The firm retains a strong reputation for excellence and its success has put it in a strong financial position.

D. R. Galbraith left the firm in 2002 and since then it has been headed by Adams. Adams serves as CEO and chair of the executive committee. Since its founding the firm has been run as a partnership. That means that the firm's most senior members are also its owners, and make all the decisions regarding how to run the firm. The group of partners is large, having grown with the firm over the years. However, the most important decisions at the firm have always been made by the executive committee, which currently consists of the five most senior partners with the largest ownership stake in the firm.

Another area in which partners have tremendous independence has to do with the assignment of their clients. Each client has one senior partner who is primarily responsible for managing their account at Galbraith. The trust and confidence these relationships foster are widely regarded as the keystone of stability in the consultant-client relationship. Because that relationship is regarded as so sacred, no one, not even the executive committee of the partners, can force partners to hand off clients that they don’t want to hand off. For example, Adams is the lead partner for an important Galbraith client called Informatics. Since becoming CEO, the level of service Adams has provided Informatics has clearly declined. It is time for Adams to name someone else as lead, but so far Adams has resisted doing so and that is Adams’s prerogative.

Because Galbraith and Company is a partnership, all its stock is held by its partners. When partners leave the firm, the firm buys back their stock.  Longstanding rules govern the rate at which partners can sell off their stock. They cannot sell it all at once, especially if they own a substantial amount.  The rules were designed to force partners to sell off their shares slowly. The reasons for this are two-fold.  First, the rules are designed to force partners to keep the long-term interests of the firm in mind, even when they are approaching the ends of their careers with the firm. Second, the rules are designed to allow the firm time to put together the capital required to buy back departing partners' stake.  Without this time, there was a risk, especially early in the life of the firm (when cash flows were not so steady) that the departure of an important partner could have produced a cash flow crisis within the firm. These rules also specify the terms by which a partner’s shares may be bought back, and the rules are quite clear. For instance, the firm may issue no loans to departing partners.

In addition to Adams and Downs, there are three other members of the executive committee.

When the executive committee meets, the five of you will have to decide whether to purchase Management Quarterly. Other issues may also come up at the meeting. All matters before the executive committee must be decided by majority vote using a secret ballot. Adams serves as chair of all executive committee meetings. As chair, Adams has the power to decide the order in which the executive committee considers issues before it. All motions that have been made and seconded must be voted on, but Adams has the power to decide when motions are debated and in what order the votes take place, as well as how to deal with redundant motions (multiple motions on the same topic).

The executive committee will gather briefly so that all members may present issues that they hope to discuss later at the meeting. However, at this initial gathering the chair will not entertain any motions nor will any votes be taken.

Following this initial meeting, there will be some free time.
