A CULTURAL PERSPECTIVE ON ORGANIZATIONS1

A cultural perspective on organizations focuses on social and personal identities carried by people, the cognitive or mental maps they utilize to come to terms with the requirements and difficulties they face in their day-to-day activities, and the moral precepts and normative standards they take for granted but nonetheless bring to bear on the world and those around them. Attention is directed to such matters as: the ideas and skills individuals and groups hold, display and value; the motives people accept as legitimate accounts for their actions and thus enact in their ordinary affairs; the special languages and expressions they cultivate and use; and the boundaries they recognize to mark membership in various social groupings. Cultural understandings are collectively shaped and rooted in the past and, as a result, are often difficult to surface and articulate because they are so taken-for-granted as natural, obvious, habituated and unquestionable by organizational members. A cultural perspective focuses, then, on the meanings people assign to their respective work experiences. People are thus viewed not only as workers, but as meaning-makers, identity carriers, moral actors, symbol users, storytellers who are actively engaged in organizational life and, through interaction with one another, continually create, sustain and modify organizational events, processes and products.1 WHAT IS CULTURE?

Culture refers to the symbolic or expressive side of human life—actions, objects and ideas that carry specific meanings to particular people and groups and hence stand for something. Culture provides a template on which meanings are read and actions are based. It is attached to both the material and immaterial, to words and deeds and it shapes and reflects social and material conditions. To take a cultural perspective is to consider the historically grounded pattern of meanings that guide the thinking, feeling and behavior of the members of some identified group. Nations have cultures, so do occupational communities, and so do organizations.2

The cultural lens reminds us that even the most task-focused professionals within the most profitoriented organizations deal not just in the world of financials or technology, but also in the symbolic and expressive side of organizational life. Making deals, establishing or meeting goals and standards, getting new products out the door are complicated everywhere and always by the fact that these activities must be accomplished collectively, and are imbued with meaning. Business organizations are social systems where people must do things together and therefore the management of meaning is as central to this collective task as is the management of money, production and sales. Meaning and Symbols

The concern for meaning in organizations focuses attention on things like values, languages, beliefs, founding legends, social norms, myths, rituals, mental frameworks or maps, metaphors, superstitions and ideologies shared by a few, many, or all organizational members. Meanings guide behavior. The key to a cultural perspective is not meaning per se but the symbol—a more or less arbitrary but conventionalized sign that stands for something else. Symbols are vehicles for meaning. The symbol is the unit on which a cultural perspective rests, and decoding what a given symbol or set of symbols means to a specific group of people is what cultural analysis is all about. From a cultural perspective, symbolism is the elementary or fundamental process that makes organizational behavior both possible and meaningful.

To take a cultural perspective on organizations is to treat symbolism seriously and learn how meanings are created, communicated, enforced, contested and sometimes changed by individuals and groups interacting both inside and outside the organization. For example, Van Maanen describes how Disney managers communicate values of family fun to new employees through language.4 Customers are referred to as “guests,” uniforms are called costumes, accidents are referred to as “incidents, and employees are told that they are always “on-stage” at work.

Cultural meanings can also be contested. For example, the ability to create financial value may be the driving reason behind most mergers and acquisitions between organizations, but these business transactions often take on more than financial meaning for those inside these firms. When iconic, socially-oriented small businesses like Ben and Jerry’s and Tom’s of Maine were acquired by large corporations like Unilever and Colgate, the transactions took on important meanings for those inside these organizations. A cultural perspective sensitizes us to issues like how employees from small, sociallyoriented companies might be troubled by being acquired by firms they perceive to be culturally very different, or how challenges of integrating the merging entities might be unique when this particular culture issue looms large in the post-merger integration process.5 Symbols typically mean different things to different groups of people, so the respective competencies and expectations of those who come into contact with given symbols must be examined. Since each domain plays off the others, the interpretation of symbols—even simple ones—can be complicated. Consider the Facebook logo as a symbol of interest. For different audiences, it takes on different meanings. For some, it’s a symbol of the ability to connect with friends, family and colleagues all over the world. For others, it’s a very different symbol of how big technology companies are harvesting personal data, selling it for their own financial gains, or enabling others to use it to reach their objectives, whether getting us to buy more advertised products, or even helping sway elections. For others still, the Facebook logo symbolizes Silicon Valley culture—which in itself can take on positive or negative connotations. And for others, particularly younger generations who have moved on to different platforms such as Instagram and Snapchat, the logo may symbolize a place their parents go online.

Within a single organization, the same symbol can have different meanings for different groups. For example, engineers, technicians and assemblers working together on production floors have been shown to attach different meanings to artifacts such as engineering drawings and machines. While sometimes this can be problematic, as it leads to difficulty coordinating work, sometimes objects with different meanings for different groups can serve as boundary objects—objects which are flexible enough both to adapt to the local needs of the parties employing them and to allow different groups to collaborate on a common task.6

The interpretation of symbols is at the heart of a cultural perspective on organizations. Symbolism reaches into all aspects of organizational behavior because it is the process by which all organizational activities, ceremonies, objects, products, stories, services, roles, goals, strategies and so on are made sensible and hence logical and perhaps desirable to given audiences both inside and outside recognized organizational boundaries. Leadership can therefore be seen as symbolic action as can other organizational influence attempts such as selection practices, training rituals, and the distribution of rewards. Habits and History Culture emerges slowly and unevenly. It is dependent on what has worked in the past to sustain group endeavors and is consequently built on traditions and the experience of success. Culture thus displays a good deal of continuity over time. As people move into new work settings, they ordinarily find social worlds and the identities they offer waiting for them that are already organized and understood in historically dependent ways. These identities that allow individual decisions and behavior to be handed over to habits of thought and action not only conserve effort but also provide a way of tapping into the collective experience of others since habits and ideas are learned. Their strength turns on the question of the strength of the institutions in which an idea or habit is lodged.

Take, for example, the institutionalized practices involved in the training of doctors. For decades, various stakeholders raised concerns that the very long hours worked by U.S. medical trainees (e.g. 120 hours a week) were problematic—because they exploited or were unhealthy for the trainees, because some saw them as unnecessary for training, and because overtired trainees might be more prone to make errors. Nonetheless, this system of training persisted and continued to be defended by many, including doctors themselves. As a demonstration of how strong the power of habits, history, and institutionalized pressures are, consider that even when regulation prohibited trainees working 120-hour weeks, and even when top managers at hospitals supported this reduction, many interns continued to work the long hours—and willingly. They did this not do this because they were acquiring important expertise in the additional 40 hours, but because they embraced the surgical “iron man” identity, and wanted to be “go-toguys” who could be counted on handle anything the senior surgeons sent their way.7 This example shows how strong and complex institutional pressures and habits can be, and why change efforts are so often difficult to implement. Identity

In many ways, culture and identity are interchangeable concepts for one implies the other. Both are in principle fluid and alterable, but both typically display remarkable stability over time and account for what economists regard as the inexplicable “stickiness” of entrenched ways of thinking and doing that persist in the face of the most compelling and ostensibly rational argument. Once identity is established in the workplace, the pressure is on to live it even if it means sacrificing one’s individuality to do so. While we carry a variety of potentially relevant identities, those that are valued, endure and brought into continual play in the workplace carry heartfelt moral precepts on which our actions are based. Identities are powerful because they build up interests, values, projects and experiences and refuse to dissolve by virtue of the tight and special connection they have with geography, religion, education, family background, supportive others and the specific character of given work tasks.

Work is a key source of identity for people, and scholars have shown how the workplace is an identity workspace for many workers.8 Individuals make meaning that supports their desired identity at work, whether policemen working the streets, craftsmen working on a factory floor, or NASA scientists working with complex mathematical models.9 Because work can be a key area through which individuals

## Page 4

derive their identities—both professional and personal—changes in work that may seem minor for outsiders can deeply affect the identities of those performing the work. For example, when NASA started using crowd-sourcing to pose some of its toughest technical challenges to the crowd, and when the organization received solutions that in a handful of areas were superior to those worked on for years by NASA scientists, many scientists were deeply threatened. Their “problem solver” identity no longer seemed so important and secure. Scholarship examining this identity threat at the time showed how some scientists outright rejected solutions from outside the organization, but those who took on a “solution seeker” identity were able to use crowd-sourced solutions effectively.10

In other cases, one’s work identity may have a more complicated relationship to one’s identity outside of work. For example, some studies show this for workers in the service industry, given the emotional labor demands of job requirements such as smiling when being treated badly, or being forced to interact with customers in highly routinized ways that allow little room for judgement or improvisation.11 However, studies also show that even in service jobs, employees often find ways to exercise discretion over their labors, and sometimes in ways that run counter to managerial goals. For example, many casinos set the pay of black jack dealers so that their income comes mostly from tips, hoping this will encourage them to behave in managerially-desired ways towards customers (e.g. always be courteous and smile). Yet, even when working for tips, studies show that dealers exercise discretion in how they engage customers, quickly segmenting customers on their willingness to tip, and sharing this information with one another.12 Subcultures

Subcultures are present in every organization. Subcultures are groups of people who share common identities based on characteristics that often transcend or override their organizationally prescribed roles and relationships. Subcultures emerge more or less autonomously in all organizational settings and influence the behavior of organizational members in a variety of ways. Groups of people who work together produce their own cultural understandings, but cultural production in organizations is not confined strictly to fixed work positions and units.

As noted, groups of people who work together produce their own cultural understandings, but cultural production in organizations is not confined strictly to fixed work positions and units. The larger social context in which people live beyond the organization can be expected to play a role, and here is where the idea of subculture is most relevant. For example, even though ethnicity is not supposed to count when performing a particular job, it certainly counts in the external world and members of different groups bring different cultures with them to work. Employee subcultures may then form along class or ethnic lines because cultural similarities are strongly felt and people are drawn to others who share their meaning systems.13

Subcultures can also cut across occupational lines.14 For example, engineers and marketers within the same company often have distinctive subcultures, which emerge from their occupational interests and educational backgrounds. For example, it is common for engineers to want to develop cutting-edge technology, whereas marketers are more likely to want to grow the customer base and hence market share.15 Senior managers versus those lower down in the ranks of an organization may have different subcultures, due to the work they do, their age, and their income levels.16 As people live and now work longer into their lives, many organizations are seeing subcultures emerge between employees from different generations; much of the scholarship here points out how millennials, as “digital natives,” have an orientation to the world that sets them apart from older generations.17

Subcultures are not, in and of themselves, good or bad. But, they can become problematic when organizational work requires that members of different subcultures collaborate with one another, and their different cultures prevent them from doing so. However, scholars have shown that various mechanisms can help facilitate collaboration. For example, as described earlier, boundary objects can ease coordination between members of different occupations, whether engineers and technicians on a shop floor, or members of departments like Creative, Account Management, and Technology at a website development firm.18 It can also be helpful to structure workgroups in ways that can help members overcome what are often deeply-entrenched subcultures. For example, one study showed that nurses and patient care technicians (PCTs) working in hospitals struggled to collaborate, due to differences in occupational subcultures but also differences in ethnicity, immigration status, and race (nearly all nurses were white, young, and U.S. born while nearly all PCTs were non-white, older, and non U.S.-born). A group structure characterized by cross-cutting demographics—in which occupational membership was uncorrelated with demographic group membership (e.g. black nurses working with white PCTs)— loosened attachment to the occupational identity and status order, thus allowing the members of the two different occupations, to work together. They did this by drawing on their shared social identities, such as shared race, age, or immigration status, in their interactions.19

Heterogeneity within different occupational groups that struggle to collaborate can be generative. For example, a study of a carsharing firm that was shifting from an engineering to marketing focused strategy showed a high degree of conflict between engineers and marketers. However, within each department, there were some more “moderate” members (e.g. more organizationally versus occupationally-identified) and some more “radical” members (e.g. more occupationally versus organizationally-identified). Once top managers put a high level of pressure on engineers to work on marketing projects, the “moderate” engineers began to do so—but only collaborated with “moderate” marketers, because the two groups could draw on their shared organizational identification when working together.20

People within organizations are capable of taking multiple perspectives on what they do. A single employee is not so much an organization type (a “Googler,” a Zappos employee) or a member of a single identity group within the organization (a saleswoman, financial analyst or engineer) as the individual is a repository for different cultural perspectives. An engineer working in a Silicon Valley tech company may staunchly defend his organization if it is criticized for dishonest dealings with foreign governments, but might openly criticize his company’s handling of a sexual harassment or discrimination lawsuit filed by a female colleague. It is important to recognize that people have multiple identities and, depending on the issue at hand, they may react in ways that reinforce or distance their commitment to their organizations. A cultural perspective suggests that rather than try to find a single organizational culture (or most salient subculture) to explain individual or group behavior, the best approach may be to look for the kinds of issues that call up different meaning systems or cultures for the person or collectivity involved.

IMPORTANT ISSUES RELATED TO A CULTURAL PERSPECTIVE In this section, we address three important issues related to a cultural perspective: cross-cultural dynamics; culture, control and motivation; and how emerging technologies are changing the cultural dynamics of organizations. Cross-Cultural Dynamics In today’s global economy, it is common for organization members to interact frequently with colleagues from different cultures. Despite attempts to leverage communication technologies to keep

## Page 6

workers connected, within many organizations, subcultures often form in different locations. These subcultures can be problematic when they prevent employees from communicating and working with one another. However, bridging cross-cultural divides is possible.21 For example, a study of a team inside a professional services firm that was distributed across the US and India showed problems such as stereotyping related to work ethic issues and challenges communicating; hierarchical differences across the countries (e.g. US employees were generally more senior) compounded the issues. But, an intervention helped ameliorate these challenges. The intervention had two parts: weekly one-on-one calls between junior and senior team members from across the locations, and then a weekly, ninety-minute “pulse check” call with the entire group. The researchers found that the combination of spaces and interaction scripts—concrete guidelines for interaction that specified content parameters and participation rules—helped the team enact counter-normative forms of sharing that led to the emergence of positive relational dynamics across cultures, even though members had once been divided.22 Other studies have shown the value of “brokers” who can initiate, influence, manage or facilitate interactions across cultural boundaries.23

Studies of working across cultures also highlight, however, that changes intended to help employees work more seamlessly with one another can themselves create tensions. For example, when the CEO of Rakuten, an online retailer in Japan, mandated a policy of “Englishization,” whereby all employees in all locations were expected to work in English only, the intent was to ease collaboration. But the policy created tensions, with nonnative speakers fearing status loss and worried about the prospect of learning English along with their other work responsibilities.24

While there is no silver bullet for getting members of different cultures to work together, research suggests that having team members be able to meet in person, giving them access to informal tools that can facilitate for spontaneous communication, and building a shared team identity can help members work together who are distributed across locations and might otherwise divide themselves based on their home cultures.25 Culture, Control, and Motivation

A long tradition of organizational scholarship has examined the relationship between culture, control and motivation. It considers questions like how managers get employees to work hard, to coordinate what they do with others, to put themselves out for the good of the group and, in general, to do things they might not otherwise do if left to themselves.26 Coercive theories hold that most people will respond accordingly only when management holds a (symbolic) whip over their backs. But using coercive power is not the most effective way to motivate workers. Economic exchange theories hold that most people work for tangible rewards like money and the things that money will buy. Individuals’ desires usually outstrip the means they possess, so they will work harder for more pay. But, however plausible these economic incentive or material reward theories appear on the surface, they do not seem to work very well in practice. For example, a long tradition of research examining workgroup dynamics shows how managers’ attempts to motivate workers with pay can be resisted. For example, decades ago sociologist Donald Roy coined the term “goldbricking” while studying workers in a factory. Roy observed that when the piece-rate quotas that managers set for workers seemed unattainable (their point totals were too low in relation to the work required), workers collectively resisted what they saw as exploitative working conditions by withholding effort.27 If sheer economic incentives or the coercive imposition of rules and regulations cannot be used to predictably move people in managerially desired directions, what does work? Managers often attempt to increase worker motivation by providing socialization and symbols that reinforce the values they hope that employees will internalize.28 For example, managers at Disney ran an orientation program for new

## Page 7

employees that emphasized values ranging from “the customer is king” to “everyone is a child at heart when at Disneyland.”29 Similarly, the founders of Pixar Animation Studios deliberately created a culture directed at fostering the collective creativity needed to make their films. While many organizations experience tensions between those doing “art” and those doing “science,” at Pixar, the founders emphasized that “Art challenges technology, and technology inspires art”—meaning, no one side of the studio was more or less important, and their success depended on one another.30 Yet, using culture to motivate employees to act in particular ways was not straightforward. As Pixar came to be increasingly successful, some managers noted that many people entering the organization were reluctant to criticize any of the organization’s processes or practices, assuming they must be successful. To combat this, senior leaders at the Studio began to attend new employee orientation and tell stories about mistakes they had made in managing Pixar—hoping, through this newcomer socialization, to create a culture not just where mistakes were not punished, but where incoming employees understood that the Studio was imperfect.31 Managers also try to motivate employees to act in managerially approved directions by using symbols to promote conceptual models of thought and action for employees to follow. Lobbies of corporate offices may be decorated with pictures of "employees of the month" or sales organizations may publicly honor those high performers who exceed their quotas for the year. Managers’ efforts to engineer culture internally may provoke employee cynicism.32 However, if such ceremonial events and ritualized rewards are not overused, and if employees see them as truly reflecting their day-to-day work in the organization, honored employees may become models of desirable behavior for other members of the organization to emulate. Other models for behavior come from the stories repeatedly told on formal occasions (e.g., orientation sessions, company training events, reward ceremonies, etc.). Models are conveyed, usually more effectively, by the informal exchanges that occur among employees in and out of the workplace about exemplary behavior—like of the computer technician who worked through the night to discover and fix a bug in a system for an important client. Such tales can help create organizational loyalties and motivate employees to act in line with organizational goals. All the above ways of motivating organizational members are usually in operation simultaneously. Many high-tech companies, for example, celebrate norms encouraging information sharing and consensual decision making (e.g., positions and functions are not believed to be necessarily aligned with needed expertise), and a strong emphasis on profit-oriented creativity and entrepreneurial advocacy (e.g., too much bureaucracy is thought to stifle innovation). Control in such contexts is put in place by selection, socialization, stories, rewards of many sorts beyond the monetary, models of exemplary behavior, rituals of solidarity and everyday routines. To a large degree, managers in such contexts tend to be quite aware of the norms they wish to foster in the organization and thus selfconsciously promote an organizational culture to serve what they take to be the firm's best interest. For example, they do things like create culture manuals and make frequent speeches. Yet, because organizations are complex systems, attempts to change culture often do not have desired effects. Changing an organization’s culture is difficult—and often, change initiatives have unintended consequences. For example, sometimes managers design work in ways that allow them to observe employees as they go about their daily tasks, with the hope that this scrutiny will increase productivity. However, studies have found that this can create a transparency paradox, whereby maintaining observability of workers may counterintuitively reduce their performance.33 This is because it can induce workers to conceal their activities through codes and other costly means; conversely, creating zones of privacy can actually increase performance, because it can encourage worker activities like localized experimentation and distraction avoidance. In another example, a factory manager shifted the factory’s management away from a strict hierarchical structure and towards one of self-managing teams. The manager assumed that peers would be less controlling of one another than managers had been; but to the contrary, the organizations members developed value-based normative rules that controlled one

another’s actions more powerfully. In effect, the attempt to loosen bureaucratic control ended up tightening what Weber called “the iron cage,” through a system of peer-based control.34 The Future of Organizational Culture Technological advances—from the Web and social media, to robots, algorithms and artificial intelligence—are having a profound impact on the world of work, and this in turn is affecting the cultural dynamics surrounding work and organizations. A host of changes, many technological, have given rise to a “gig economy,” a market system in which individuals and organizations contract with independent workers for short-term engagements. This leads us to consider that, for an increasing number of people who are not affiliated with an organization or established profession, workplace culture may take on new and different meanings.

As digital and social technologies advance, this allows large-scale projects to be broken down into small work packages that can be distributed among a digital workforce.35 For example, the Amazon Mechanical Turk platform has a standardized technical infrastructure that enables activities to be globally distributed and repeated efficiently and with regularity; the interfaces are inscribed with assumptions and prescriptions that direct the manner that the user is permitted to take.36 Some scholars have pointed to positive outcomes associated with a gig economy. It may enable geographically dispersed people to come together around a common mission or assignment and create significant value,37 affording workers high levels of flexibility and autonomy in terms of choosing what tasks to do, where to work and when to work.38 It may afford better matching between the supply and demand of skills,39 and heightened inclusivity by offering opportunities to workers whose availability or mobility prevents them from working regular hours.40 In addition, outsiders may be more efficient innovators and bring fresh ideas to internal problems;41 there may be an empowering aspect inherent in crowdsourcing that allows for the democratization of idea generation.42

Critics of the gig economy, on the other hand, have used the term “ghost work” to describe the labor conditions that can accompany task-based jobs that are, at least in part, sourced, scheduled, managed, billed, and shipped through a mix of application programming interfaces (APIs) and the internet.43 The scholars suggest that, while the platforms sell themselves as software that can eliminate the expensive frictions of searching, matching, training, communicating with, and retaining workers, the transaction costs associated with doing this haven’t gone away. Instead, critics note, they have been shifted to requesters and workers. Thus, workers on these platforms may experience what these scholars call “algorithmic cruelty” for three reasons: the need for hypervigilance despite claims of flexibility, isolation and lack of guidance despite claims of autonomy, and not getting paid when technical failure is characterized as malfeasance.44

Critics45 have shown that crowdworkers are often hypervigilant, spending many hours sorting through suspicious offers for work, and being on call day and night since most platforms only allow workers to pick up tasks on a first-come first-served basis. In addition, they note that workers on these platforms often have no one to help them understand a problem they are trying to solve, or give them any feedback on what worked and did not work. And, they show that, because platforms decide who has access and who doesn’t, workers can lose their jobs and wages, with no explanation and no opportunity to appeal the cancellation of their accounts. For example, a study of Southeast Asians and Sub-Saharan Africans working on an online labor platform described how, on the one hand, the platforms afforded workers high levels of flexibility, autonomy and task variety; on the other, these benefits were coupled with low pay, social isolation, irregular work hours, and worker exhaustion.46

Scholars studying crowdworkers have shown that crowdworkers often collaborate to fulfill technical and social needs that they often cannot get from the platforms they work on. Rather than acting as independent, autonomous workers, they work jointly with members of their network to manage the administrative overhead associated with crowdwork, find lucrative tasks and reputable employers, and recreate the social connections and support often associated with brick-and-mortar work environments.47 Lacking an organizational “holding environment” and the structure it provides, crowdworkers often endeavor to create one for themselves, through cultivating connections to routines, places, and people; these personal holding environments can help them manage their precarious working lives.48 For example, a group of creative freelancers designing and developing digital goods in a crowdsourced work arrangement with an entertainment publisher, forged an occupational community to help them find direction and meaning for their work, build on collective strategies to smooth compensation, and illuminate the pathway from amateur to expert.49 Some platform-based labor markets attempt to help workers build this support by facilitating teams that are collaborative, cooperative units. For example, a study of CloudFactory and LeadGenius showed that these companies aspired to meet a double bottom line of both fiscal gains and positive social impact. This study suggested that these companies offered examples of two ways in which crowdwork could be done differently.50 First, the companies created two clearly defined streams of crowdwork: one explicitly available for group collaboration (e.g. sales lead verification) and the other requiring independent, subjective results (e.g. survey responses where independent results were required for validity); this allowed workers to collaborate when collaboration did not run counter to requesters’ desired outcomes.  Second, the companies “taskified” management by turning affirmation and training into paid tasks. The LeadGenius platform included built-in, real-time chat tools that allowed groups to speak directly with other crowdworkers assigned to the same tasks.51 They were able to ask each other for help, keep each other company, and reach a junior manager to respond to the questions anytime during their scheduled work shift. Team leaders and junior managers were paid for the time that they spent checking the quality of crowdworkers’ tasks as well as for time spent responding to crowdworkers’ questions.

Emerging technologies are changing how people engage with one another not only in companies that use “crowds,” but in more conventional companies too. For example, a study of a social media marketing company showed how its executives used social media inside the firm’s boundaries to engage employees through radical transparency.52 The study demonstrated that, when the company’s customer retention rate decreased dramatically, an executive posted about the problem on the company’s internal wiki. Along with his comments, he attached a 138 page document that showed proprietary information such as detailed profit and loss and cash flow statements, and a recent customer survey. In many companies, this information would be closely guarded. But at this firm, executives ended up using social media to create what the researcher called a “conversational firm,” where open dialogue up, down and across the hierarchy created a new way of operating to better engage the workforce.

The study relates how this same firm marketed their culture externally for recruiting purposes. Top managers put together a “culture deck” as a way to make an identity claim in the labor market that could help them attract talented individuals to the company. However, this culture deck was initially resisted inside the company both because employees saw gaps between the external representation of their culture and the culture they experienced each day and because the executives, by creating the culture deck, had taken control over external communications in an organization where workers had come to expect voice and conversation. Morale was restored when executives began to call the culture desk aspirational, and to use it as a basis for dialogue with the staff. Thus, marketing a culture externally may help a firm recruit like-minded employees; however, a firm that has committed itself to conversation will need to use such cultural decrees as a starting point for internal voice and dialogue.53

As technological advances forge the way for ever-new ways of working, the cultural issues at hand for managers are certain to change, and likely to grow all the more complex. ANALYZING ORGANIZATIONS USING A CULTURAL PERSPECTIVE When analyzing an organization from a cultural perspective, it is important to ask a series of questions:

-  What are your key objectives and planned initiative?
-  What are the existing organizational or subgroup values?
-  What existing symbols, rituals or ceremonies communicate these values to employees?
-  What socialization practices attempt to get employees to embrace these values?
-  Is the existing culture similar across subunits within the organization (e.g. occupational groups, offices in different locations)? If not, how does it vary? What challenges (and potential opportunities) does this introduce?
-  Does your initiative fit with or try to change the organizational or subgroup culture? In conclusion, a cultural perspective draws our attention to aspects of life such as meaning, symbols and identity. In today’s ever-increasingly interconnected, dynamic and global economy, organizations of all kinds operate in a fast-changing environment. Whether operating within a small market in a single country, or through a vast network of employees, contractors and crowd contributors spread across the globe, all organizations must contend with issue of subcultures, meaning, identity and other cultural issues. Taking a cultural perspective on organizations can help managers predict areas of potential resistance to change, and reduce such resistance, or at least help manage it more effectively where and when it does arise.
