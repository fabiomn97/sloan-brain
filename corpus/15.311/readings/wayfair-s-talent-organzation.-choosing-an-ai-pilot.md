Wayfair’s Talent Organization: Choosing an AI Pilot

Katherine C. Kellogg and Cate Reavis

Kate Gulliver, global head of talent (HR) at housewares e-retailer Wayfair, had just come from a meeting with Alaukik Bhatt, a program manager in Wayfair’s operations product innovation (OPI) team. Several months earlier, Gulliver had told Bhatt about two key challenges her talent organization was facing – recruiting new employees with the technical skills required for Wayfair’s rapidly evolving technical work and engaging current employees with personalized opportunities for internal mobility. In response, Bhatt had investigated several artificial intelligence (AI) technology “use cases” that could help her take on these challenges in a highly efficient way. He gave her information about a set of modular, easy-to-use HR solutions from a reputable technology vendor. Two use cases in particular grabbed Gulliver’s attention. One use case, candidate matching, would help with the recruiting of new employees. The other, which focused on internal mobility, would help with the engagement of current employees. It was the Fall of 2019, and Gulliver’s talent organization only had the bandwidth to implement one use case at a time, and she needed to decide which one to pilot first within her organization.

Wayfair

Wayfair was founded in 2002 as CSN Stores by Niraj Shah and Steve Conine, both of whom studied engineering in college where they were roommates. Within 10 years, CSN had morphed into Wayfair, one of the world’s largest home furnishings websites with operations in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Germany. Wayfair operated five retail sites – Wayfair, Joss & Main, AllModern, Birch Lane, and Perigold – selling things such as rugs, coffee tables, bathroom vanities, and firepits. Together the sites sold over 14 million products from over 11,000 suppliers.

Wayfair’s market capitalization in November 2019 was over $7.8 billion. Between the time the company went public in 2014 and 2018, revenue grew from $1.3 billion to $6.8 billion – a CAGR of 51%. There was still lots of room for growth. The home goods market in the United States was $285 billion by revenue; online sales accounted for 13% of total.1 The company had over 16,000 employees (known as Wayfairians), with over 2,300 engineers and data scientists and 3,200 sales and service staff; the other two thirds of Wayfairians worked in warehousing, operations, and remaining business roles. As an ecommerce retailer, Wayfair depended heavily on recruiting and retaining the best technical talent.

Culture

Wayfair’s culture was “deeply rooted in technology and data,”2 and the work environment was fast, with a priority on speed to execution over perfection, to a large degree. As Moriah Scott, associate director of talent analytics, explained, “We’re a different company every six months. In order to thrive in the market, we need people who can evolve quickly.” The company looked for people with good critical thinking and quantitative abilities who were generalists when it came to industry knowledge and skills. The work environment was highly collaborative (open work spaces, no offices) and face-toface interactions were preferred over digital whenever possible. When it came to technology, Wayfair preferred to build rather than buy. Scott explained why: “We want to make sure that the tools we have meet our exact needs rather than having something out of the box that is 75% of what we need, with the remaining 25% of our needs remaining unmet.” Sometimes, however, it made more sense to buy. The idea of an AI pilot fit well with Wayfair’s culture. As Amit Mohindra, global head of talent strategy and analytics, noted, “Culturally, everyone is used to the construct of an MVP [minimum viable product], so you don’t need to have something completely baked. In that sense, a pilot certainly fits the bill. It’s kind of standard. If you don’t capture everything or fix everything, no big deal. We’ll iterate to perfection going forward.” However, as Laurie Josephson of Wayfair’s talent analytics team stated, “Data integrity issues are crucial. It’s great to come up with a cool AI project, but you can’t just invent AI off of something if the data is incorrect.”

Wayfair Talent Organization

Getting the best people, and engaging and retaining them once hired were two key strategic priorities for Wayfair.

Recruiting

Recruiting was a huge challenge because of the company’s exponential growth. Wayfair’s workforce grew by 60% between 2017 and 2018, and it hoped to grow the corporate workforce by an additional 20% or more and the field workforce by 60% or more. According to Gulliver, one of the challenges

Wayfair had was being in a hypergrowth state: “We can’t actually hire as fast as we want to. And it’s not because there aren’t qualified people out in the world. There are. But it’s hard to find them. So, how do we make the recruiting teams more efficiently able to match the right person to the right job?” In addition to challenges related to growth, recruiting was difficult because of the “war for talent” within the high technology industry. Employees with the technical skills that Wayfair required were in high demand. In a tech-driven city like Boston, where unemployment was very low, recruiting wasn’t all that easy. Direct competitors like Amazon, as well as other large tech companies like Facebook, Google, Apple, and Microsoft were all searching for employees with the same kinds of technical skills. Wayfair had responded to these challenges by dramatically staffing up its recruiting organization. But Gulliver wanted new solutions: “Already I have hundreds of recruiters and hundreds more supporting the HR team. I don’t want a 1,000-person department. So if we keep growing at this rate, we need something to generate efficiency.” Recruiters worked on filling technical roles, frontline positions (e.g., warehousing, operations), and business roles in Boston. Over 400,000 people applied to jobs at Wayfair in 2018, resulting in 32,000 on-site interviews.

Issues and New Experiments in Recruiting

Despite the large investment Wayfair had made in recruiting, demand for new talent continued to outstrip supply. Recruiters spent most of their time doing outbound recruiting, using platforms such as LinkedIn to search for candidates with the required skills. Outbound recruiting was expensive and time consuming. It often meant identifying potential candidates who weren’t looking for a job, or not interested in relocating to Boston. But, there was a major hurdle associated with inbound recruiting (screening candidates who had applied for a job at Wayfair), as well: there was a high volume of applicants to screen at the “top of the funnel” and a lower percentage of them had the skills that Wayfair needed. On any given day, Wayfair recruiters doing inbound recruiting sifted through several hundred resumes to find a handful of candidates with the required skills. Because the screening process was so inefficient, recruiters prioritized outbound over inbound search. One recruiter said, “I don’t spend much time looking at the inbound applications. The people who apply inbound are usually not as good as the ones we can find by doing outbound.” Gulliver explained that despite its inefficiencies inbound recruiting was critically important to Wayfair: “The challenge with inbound is that you have a significant drop off from the screen to the interview because of lack of skill set. Whereas, with outbound, you’re targeting a very specific skill set. So, from outbound screen to interview, most candidates will pass through….The problem is that, in every other stage in the funnel, inbound has a higher conversion rate.” Inbound candidates were both looking for a job and wanted to be in Boston.

Part of the reason inbound candidates appeared less qualified to recruiters was because candidates applying to jobs at Wayfair weren’t always aware of all the different roles for which they might be a good fit. Craig Shoneman, director of OPI, explained: “One of the problems we have is that candidates come to the career site and they don’t always apply to the right job or to the job that they’re most qualified for. And a lot of times that might be because they’re not sure which job is right for them.” Gulliver noted,

We’re growing so fast that we’ve got a huge number of open roles. And we’re seeing poor matching across those roles. So, for example, in our product team, we realized that we had 70 different profiles [job descriptions] for several hundred roles. Our recruiters’ ability to move candidates around [to match them to the right roles] requires that the recruiters not only be knowledgeable across all 70 of those profiles, but also be incentivized to share candidates with one another. Another difficulty that recruiters faced was the need to secure engineers’ time to do interviews with candidates applying for technical jobs. One recruiter noted, “We can’t schedule interviews as quickly as we’d like because we just don’t have the engineering bandwidth. Engineers need to be doing their real job, not just doing interviews all day.” To help recruit new employees with the required technical skills, Wayfair had invested in  platformbased hiring solutions and analytics, both of which were considered critical tools for helping the talent organization carry out its challenging hiring mission. Recently, Wayfair had transitioned to Greenhouse, an applicant tracking system, which would simplify applicant tracking for recruiters by allowing recruiters to track candidates from initial contact through phone and onsite interviews to offer and acceptance. In addition, a taskforce within recruiting had recently run a successful pilot with an outside vendor that provided technical screening for technology companies. The taskforce had done A/B testing comparing the technical screening performance of the vendor to that of Wayfair. The results indicated that the external vendor could do the initial technical screenings more quickly and cost-effectively than Wayfair, and with similar screen-to-hire ratios.

Internal Mobility

One of the key ways that Wayfair kept employees motivated and engaged was to offer them opportunities for internal mobility. Employees were frequently asking themselves, “What next job will allow me to continue to acquire the skills and experience that I need to succeed in my career?” Wayfair talent managers noted that creating a robust way for employees to move positions internally would help drive employee engagement by ensuring that the company’s existing talent was able to develop their careers within Wayfair. As Kelley Burke, senior manager of talent relations, pointed out, “One of the top reasons people leave companies is career development.” Research suggested that

retention rates could be improved with internal mobility processes in place. One 2018 HR study showed that enabling employees to change jobs within an organization could increase retention likelihood by up to 33%.3 Another study found that 75% of employees with internal opportunities were likely to stay with their employers for another five years compared to 56% without them.4 In addition to increasing engagement and reducing turnover, offering current employees opportunities for internal mobility was often more efficient than recruiting new people from outside. Replacing people with external hires was a costly exercise due to the challenges of time and integration. In addition to the time it took a recruiter to conduct a search for an open position, it could take three to six months to train a new recruit. “Our most successful managers are ones that we promote internally,” remarked Recruiting Manager Sam Byrnes. Allowing employees to move from one business to another within Wayfair also facilitated cross-business innovation and collaboration. Thus, talent managers worked with their business partner managers to identify high performers and tailor promotion opportunities for them. While each of the company’s business units focused on different business goals, the competencies required for most of Wayfair’s positions were highly similar. Managers working in supply chain to merchandising to ecommerce all needed people with engineering skills, as well as skills in leadership, innovation, and collaboration. The company’s performance management process also helped with internal mobility. Kelley Burke explained:

Everyone is rated twice a year. Our talent managers get together with our business partners and we do a 9-box exercise by organization and department level. With our business partners we identify the people on their teams who have shown high performance and high potential. We determine what are their ‘at bats’? What are their skill gaps? We originally did this just at the director level, but now we’re doing it at the associate director and senior manager level as well. So, we can say to people, ‘Hey this new initiative is happening. You’re really strong at project management and supplier management. We think you would be great at bringing structure to this new function.’ Employees also learned about new opportunities through a weekly email from the recruiting team, which listed all open positions. From there, those interested could contact the hiring manager and take them for coffee to hear more about the positions. Talent also ran a panel series where current employees came and talked about how they had made different moves within Wayfair that had allowed them to gain new experience. Kelley Burke from the performance management area explained that, “People really like to hear from current employees about how they learned of new opportunities and navigated their job changes.” But Gulliver wasn’t satisfied: “We have all of this data on how people perform, we have all of the reviews, and we know how they’ve been rated.  We also should know what’s needed in other roles

Issues and New Experiments in Internal Mobility

While Wayfair emphasized being an “internal jungle gym” for career opportunities, accomplishing internal mobility in practice was difficult for several reasons. First, employees often didn’t know where to start if they were interested in exploring an internal move. It was difficult for employees in one area of the organization to know whether open jobs in other areas of the organization would be a good fit for their skills and career aspirations. It was also hard to assess what further training they would need to gain the required skills for these positions. “People don’t know where to start,” explained Burke. “It’s too ambiguous. They may hear that operations is hiring. But, they wonder, what types of people does operations look for? What has the career trajectory looked like for people with similar profiles to me?”  Along these same lines, it was difficult for managers in different business units to identify internal talent across the company that might be a good fit for their team. Second, while employees expressed interest in frequently moving from role to role to take on new growth opportunities, managers wanted to keep their highest performers. For them, employees who both had good track records and the particular experience important for their current roles were worth their weight in gold. Bearing this mindset in mind, talent managers tried to encourage business managers to facilitate internal mobility for members of their team. There was, as Recruiting Manager Sam Byrnes explained, an upside for managers helping their reports move on to new jobs: “From a leadership perspective, if you can groom talent that then moves to other parts of the organization, it’s a good look for you, as that person’s manager. It shows that you’re a team player and that you want the company overall to do well.” Being open and supportive of internal moves could influence a manager’s net promoter score, which was considered in every performance review. Yet, success in getting managers to drive internal mobility was mixed. While managers liked the idea of internal mobility in theory, in practice they were for it as long as it didn’t result in other managers “raiding” their best performers. Wayfair had begun to engage employees with personalized opportunities for internal mobility in a number of new ways. Kelly Madden, associate director of talent development, had recently created a Career Mobility Taskforce to identify and pilot different ways to increase internal mobility. Separately, Recruiting Manager Sam Byrnes had tried to address some of these issues by running a rotational program in partnership with Wayfair’s chief merchandising officer (CMO). They set up a pilot where team members from his merchandising area rotated through Byrnes’s recruiting area in 6-month stints. Part of what made the rotational initiative work was that the CMO communicated to his group that he was interested in his people rounding out their skill-sets. And, after their rotation in recruiting, some team members were promoted into higher level positions that required the skill sets they had honed during their rotations.

Choosing an AI Pilot

Candidate Matching for Recruiting

One of the AI use cases that Gulliver was considering was a candidate matching tool. The tool, which had been developed utilizing job data gathered from a wide array of companies, included guided search and a chatbot, which would immerse candidates in a deeper conversation about Wayfair and recommend jobs relevant for them. With guided search, AI technology would analyze a candidate’s resume and rank open positions by fit. The chatbot would then recommend the best jobs for the candidate based on their skills, experience, education, and interests. It would also respond to the job seeker’s questions like a recruiter would, by accessing a vast library of company- and job-specific information related to candidates’ frequently asked questions. Craig Shoneman explained,

The idea is using AI to say, ‘Well, why don’t you tell me a little bit about yourself, and I’ll help suggest jobs that are available that might be right for you, so that you apply to the right job?’ Because if you apply to the wrong job, the likelihood that you actually get hired is poor when, in fact, you might have been a great fit for a job, but you didn’t apply to it. So, you got rejected simply because the match was wrong. Candidate matching would allow more people to apply to the right job. It would also prevent applicants from getting lost in the process. Finally, it would improve the initial screening conversion rate for inbound candidates, which would help make the recruitment process more efficient. As Alaukik Bhatt pointed out, “If we improve the quality of candidates, recruiters will be able to spend less time on candidates who are not going to make it and more time on targeted candidates.” Not only would a candidate matching tool help Wayfair find the right candidates for the right jobs, it would also provide candidates with a robust and uniform inbound candidate experience. A good matching process could also bring in higher quality people, based on Wayfair’s reputation as a good employer and on its marketplace brand of offering speed and reliability as well as unparalleled choices.

Employee Matching for Internal Mobility

Similar matching technology afforded a tool that could help Wayfair build a more structured and proactive internal mobility process. The tool would work as a virtual career advisor for employees. Here, a chat bot would ask employees questions about their interests and also use natural language processing to analyze their current skills and performance on past projects and rank open positions by fit. Bhatt explained, “It would compare employee skill and career interests to open jobs and give employees a score for each job. So, employees could learn that they were a 50% match for a pricing job, and an 80% match for a supply chain job.” Kelly Madden, the associate director of talent development, who had recently created the Career Mobility Taskforce, noted,

What First?

Gulliver was excited about both the candidate matching and matching for internal mobility AI tools since each would, theoretically, help the company with its recruitment and engagement challenges. But, she was also aware that each use case would be very difficult to develop and implement. Furthermore, each carried its own risks. Which pilot should Gulliver choose first?
