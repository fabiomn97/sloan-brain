The Three Lenses:  A Guide to Understanding Organizations

John S. Carroll

Businesses are changing rapidly in response to economic, social, political, and technological forces. Companies have flattened their hierarchies in order to be more flexible and to cut costs.  More and more companies have a global reach, and an internal diversity of people and ideas to match the diversity of their customers and stakeholders.  Companies are networked internally and externally to gather information and combine resources for emerging opportunities.  The idea that companies or firms are the primary actors in economic life is yielding to crowdsourcing, online communities, consortia, and other structures that cut across traditional boundaries. Within this ferment, it is tempting to latch onto a simple theory or model to bring everything into focus, cut through the confusion, explain what is happening, and derive effective actions.  If, for example, we could reduce everything to a classical economic analysis of game theory and economic value added, then surely we could manage with confidence and extract optimal rents.

Complexity is Complex, and People are People

Unfortunately, our best knowledge and theories cannot put a neat package around organizational behavior.  Even though economic analysis has made tremendous progress and has gained widespread respect, economists struggle to predict both the macro behavior of large institutions and the micro behavior of individuals.  Although we may believe we understand how an economy works, we have no recipes for how to get developing nations from here to there.  Although we may believe that people are motivated by monetary incentives, we have yet to design systems that align everyone’s incentives with overall goals.  People in organizations act on the basis of their perceptions and values, and these vary substantially from person to person, company to company, location to location, and country to country.

Take mergers and acquisitions, for example. It is well known in the economics literature that companies pay a substantial premium for an acquisition. Research shows that the stock price of the acquirer drops following the acquisition, and does not seem to recover enough to justify the acquisition.  Why, then, do companies buy other companies? The short answer is, we don’t know.  There are a variety of common answers ranging across economies of scale and scope, misinformation and overconfidence, or executive ego and empire building. Companies often underestimate the difficulties of actually integrating the new company into the combined entity (as illustrated by Daimler-Chrysler; AOL-Time Warner; and Google-Nest). CEOs sometimes seem more interested in building their legacy than raising stock prices.  Executives see the opportunity to advance their careers in a larger company, displacing acquired executives.  Everyone has reasons, but they don’t fit a single model of the rational, value maximizing organization. Your own experience in teams and organizations is probably full of examples that challenge any simple theory or model of organizational life.  Did your organization make a lot of formal rules, or did informal rules emerge from how people worked together?  If there were formal rules, were they followed rigorously?  In many organizations, people go around the rules in order to get the work done efficiently or simply for their own convenience.  In organizations that strive for innovation, the underlying principle is “find a rule and break it.”  But if we throw out all the rules, how can we work together at all?  There must be processes and practices to help everyone sort out which rules can be broken, when, and by whom. Consider the last team you were on in your last company.  Did that team work well together, achieve its goals, and use its time wisely?  If it did, why did it work so well?  Some teams, and some organizations, motivate individuals so strongly that they work long hours for months or years on a project they truly believe in, or for a leader they respect and admire, or for a company that has treated them well, or for the fun and learning in overcoming a tough technical challenge.  Other teams and organizations generate little commitment, and individuals are constantly looking for their next project or job.  How can we explain these different outcomes?

Three Lenses to Analyze Organizations

Given such complexity, it is natural for people to seek simple and powerful models that offer quick answers.  Some people look at an organization and see a rational division of labor in pursuit of agreedupon goals with incentives to motivate individuals.  Others see a struggle for power and status in a social structure.  Yet others see a search for meaning and identity in a context that is continually improvised and reconstructed.  Most people think that their model of the situation is the obvious and appropriate one, and they do not appreciate that others operate with different models. We believe that the richness of organizational life cannot be reduced to a simple model or single theory of behavior.  Models and theories are incredibly useful, but we are far from having, and may never

have, a comprehensive unitary theory of organizational behavior and processes.  Instead, we offer an approach to organizational analysis based on what we call the three lenses.  Each lens is a perspective on organizations that distills the essence of related theories that share ideas about human nature, the functions of organizations, the meaning of organizing, and the information needed to make sense of an organization.  Each perspective has its own assumptions and vast amounts of research that flesh out the ideas.  Yet the three lenses are distinct enough that they cannot directly compete or combine: don’t expect one to “win” a contest among lenses or all three to join in a happy union or comprehensive model.  Instead, each represents a different way of thinking, a different lens through which you can view an organization.  By trying on each lens, you gain new insights and a richer picture of an organization.  For example, in analyzing a proposed organizational change through all three lenses together, you may appreciate that the planners did not consider all three lenses and therefore there may be unanticipated resistance, failures of implementation, or undesirable side effects. The three lenses are:
-  The Structural Design Lens1
-  The Political Lens
-  The Cultural Lens

The Structural Design Lens

People who take this perspective view the organization as a kind of machine that has been designed to achieve goals by carrying out tasks.  The designers of the organization, the Board of Directors and senior managers, have a strategy or purpose for the organization based on rational analysis of opportunities and capabilities.  They are like the conductor of an orchestra who directs the whole orchestra to create a performance, while individual musicians play their instruments.  In order to enact that vision, particular people are hired with necessary skills or given appropriate training, grouped into departments or teams in order to carry out subtasks, connected by information systems and work flows to coordinate tasks, monitored for their performance according to plan, and rewarded to promote continued performance.  The approach is highly rational and analytical.  People, money, equipment, and information are moved around a strategic and operational chessboard using logical principles of efficiency and effectiveness.  The model assumes that, with the right plan and information flow, the organization can be rationally optimized to achieve its goals. Take, for example, a university such as MIT.  Most universities have as their goals to produce welleducated students and high-quality research.  They probably also consider being good community citizens, contributing to society, building a strong reputation, maintaining employee commitment, and enhancing financial stability.  MIT is organized to achieve those goals through a set of teaching, research, and support activities.  Faculty are recruited according to very high standards, and sorted into

Departments are organized into schools such as Engineering and Management according to disciplinary similarity.  The Deans of those schools report to the Provost who reports to the President of MIT.  Because MIT has such a strong emphasis on research, there is a second structure, something like a crosscutting matrix, of Laboratories and Centers. Faculty affiliated with these Labs and Centers work on research grants funded by government and business and help supervise a large research staff.  The Labs and Centers are collectively as large and important as the academic departments.  Lab and Center Directors report to the VP for Research who reports to the President.  Support groups such as human resources, accounting, buildings and grounds, maintenance, campus police, information systems, and public relations report through the administrative hierarchy of MIT to complete the organization. By drawing boundaries around clusters of tasks and activities, MIT has created a strategic grouping. Every organization must decide how to group their tasks by specialty, geography, product, customer, etc.  The major rationales for grouping are to put tasks together that are highly interdependent (subassemblies or information have to flow easily between groups) or to achieve economies of scale or scope.  MIT puts most of the physicists together in one department.  The Sloan Management School puts the finance faculty together, and groups finance with accounting and managerial economics in the same area.  This helps them coordinate their teaching, deepen their specialized knowledge by developing an intellectual community, and work together for faculty hiring and doctoral training. However, because interesting research often cuts across disciplinary boundaries, faculty are encouraged to form projects, Labs, and Centers that are more problem-focused.  For example, the Center for Innovation and Product Development draws on Sloan faculty from marketing, strategy, operations, management of technological innovation and entrepreneurship, and organization studies, and on faculty from other departments across MIT. Managers sometimes think that by regrouping, changing the organization chart, they can change the organization.  Time and again, executives find that they have moved people around and changed reporting relationships yet the desired results have not appeared.  When people are grouped together, boundaries are created that have to be bridged by various linking mechanisms.  Faculty cannot just teach their classes, because classes have to be organized into meaningful curricula or courses of study and resources such as classrooms have to be shared and allocated.  Formal managerial hierarchies, such as department heads and deans, are simple and pervasive, but managers can become overloaded if they have to deal with complex coordination problems.  It is slow and inefficient for information to flow up the hierarchy and then back down before two groups can work together.  Information gets distorted as it passes through people with different priorities and viewpoints.  If managers get too distant from the details of the work and lose their technical edge, they may make poor decisions.  Other linking mechanisms, formal and informal, spring up to bridge the gaps.  Formal linking mechanisms include liaison roles, cross-unit groups such as task forces, integrator roles such as project managers or account

Informal linking mechanisms typically involve networks of personal relationships that develop around friendships, carpools, churches, sports, prior projects, and so forth.  Information often flows much more readily through the “grapevine” of informal networks than through official channels. The third structural design process knits together the strategic grouping and linking into a more coherent whole.  Different strategic groups often want different things: marketing may be rewarded for making sales to customers who want quality, speed, and more features while manufacturing is rewarded for cutting costs.  These groups are naturally bound to disagree from time to time.  Individuals trying to advance their careers may find that they get ahead by doing things that undermine others in the organization.  Employees who believe in environmental sustainability may feel alienated from a company that exploits natural resources and produces products that wind up in landfill.  Mechanisms are needed to align the efforts of diverse individuals and groups with the overall organizational strategy. The structural design perspective assumes that this is fundamentally a design challenge (like a linear programming problem). Alignment mechanisms include reward and incentive systems such as bonuses, raises, stock options, and promotions.  These are typically linked to performance measurement systems that may be more or less complex.  Although base pay, variable pay, and equity-based compensation are the aspects of alignment that most managers recognize most readily, there are others that are equally important. Recruitment, selection, onboarding, work design, and training and development also play important roles in helping to align individual and organizational goals. In summary, the structural design lens assumes that organizations are fundamentally rational, in the sense that they can be designed to achieve shared goals.  From this perspective, to understand an organization you need to ask questions such as:

-  What is the organization’s strategy, and how does any planned new initiative relate to it?
-  What is the formal grouping structure? Does it hinder or facilitate the planned initiative?
-  How does linking between groups currently occur? Will the initiative require new linking mechanisms?
-  Do the key elements of the alignment system (direct compensation, indirect compensation, recruitment, selection, and onboarding, work design, and training and development) reinforce the organization’s strategy and goals? Will the initiative require new alignment mechanisms?
-  Does the organization design fit the demands of the environment, in terms of getting the necessary information and resources and meeting the demands of customers and other stakeholders?

The Political Lens

The political lens shatters the assumption that an organization has goals that “it” is pursuing.  Instead, people who use the political lens view the organization as a contested struggle for power among stakeholders with different goals and underlying interests.  Whereas the structural design lens groups and links units that must work together to accomplish tasks, the political lens combines units with similar interests and goals into coalitions that advocate their side of important issues.  Goals and strategy are either imposed by a ruling coalition or negotiated among interest groups.  As circumstances change, power shifts and flows, coalitions evolve, and agreements are renegotiated. For example, if the MIT Sloan School wants to expand, hire more faculty, and build new buildings, it must enter into a negotiation process with the rest of MIT.  Another school may resent the growing power of the Management School in a technical university.  MIT may want Sloan to build its new buildings on a location that is closer to the rest of the university, while Sloan may want a site with a river view slightly further from the main campus.  Indeed, even talking about what “MIT may want” obscures the fact that, from the political viewpoint, MIT is composed of many subunits that want different things.  The Engineering School wants one thing, the Humanities, Arts, and Social Sciences School another.  Within a school, departments vie for resources and attention, groups try to hire and promote similar colleagues, and individuals negotiate for lab space and talented students. From the political perspective, power is the ability to get things done.  Power is like the energy in the system coupled with the wires or channels to connect that energy to action.  Power is not the same as control over others; the idea of control implies that there is a fixed amount of power that must be contested.  Instead, if we can realign stakeholder interests or find solutions that produce benefits for more parties, everyone can get more of what they want without sacrificing their power or interests.  If I can “empower” you to be able to do more, it doesn’t mean that I have lost power.  Indeed, I may have gained power by having allies or coalition partners with their own power.  Further, different individuals and groups have different sources of power (which emerge when something is needed by the organization, in short supply, and/or hard to replace).  Typical sources of power include position power (formal authority, control over scarce resources), personal power (expertise, track record, social skills), and relational power (network of connections to information and influence). By adding different sources of power or combining them in complementary ways, we may be able to multiply our collective power.  For example, a manager with formal authority and an engineer with technical expertise may support each other. In universities such as MIT, who holds the power to get things done or keep things from getting done? The central MIT administration has formal authority, considerable control over resources such as money and buildings, and the power to make rules and regulations.  However, the faculty holds considerable power.  Faculty bring in a large percentage of funds through research grants and contracts. Not only are the faculty essential for achieving the teaching and research missions, but MIT faculty can

For example, almost a hundred years ago the administrations of Harvard and MIT agreed that the two universities would merge, combining Harvard’s strengths in liberal arts with MIT’s strengths in engineering.  The MIT faculty voted it down, and the merger evaporated. As Woodward and Bernstein were told about Watergate, “Follow the money.”  MIT departments that bring in more money tend to have more say and accumulate more power.  If the Sloan School starts another $20 Million research center, or receives a similar sum from a successful alumna, or starts an executive program that raises millions in tuition, it would provide leverage to hire more faculty, build a building, be more persuasive in conversations with the upper levels of MIT, and so forth.  Sometimes the sources of power are more subtle, such as the department secretary whose 20 years of experience has shown how to get around the rules and regulations and find information through a network of allies in every key office at MIT. Looked at through the political lens, major changes in mission, strategy, organization, or personnel are not simply rational moves to accomplish organizational goals, but also threats to those who hold power and opportunities for those who want more power.  If an organization considers a shift from productbased teams to customer-based teams, it is likely that those with the closest links to the customers would gain power and those who were respected for their ability to generate technically-advanced products may now be devalued unless their products are in demand.  Such a proposed change is sure to have strong supporters and strong opponents.  Since change is almost always a threat to those who were valued and successful in the old system, it should not be surprising that there is considerable resistance. It may take a coalition of internal and external interests, or a dramatic organizational failure, or a change in executive leadership, to get change underway.  Even then, there may be pockets of resistance, overt or underground, that seek to delay and subvert the change process. Consider what a long road it has been for MIT to increase the number of women and minority students and faculty.  The MIT undergraduate student composition has changed dramatically from 5% or 10% women in the 1960s to almost 50% now.  In that process, the character of classes, courses, and campus life has changed greatly.  Some are happy about that and find more support for their interests; others feel their power has eroded.  Following efforts to increase the numbers of women faculty, we learned in the late 1990s that women faculty received less lab space and less important committee assignments than their male colleagues.  These indicators of power were a surprise to most of the faculty, and it took the authority of an internal research study to overturn entrenched beliefs.

In summary, the political lens assumes that any organization is a diverse collection of stakeholders with different and sometimes conflicting interests.  The organization is heavily influenced by those with power, the dominant coalition, but power is constantly shifting and being contested.  Not only do different individuals and groups hold different amounts and kinds of power emanating from different sources, but as the environment shifts or new strategies are developed, groups come to the fore that have the capabilities to deal with these new demands.  From this perspective, key questions to ask about an organization are:

-  What are your key objectives and planned initiative?
-  What are your sources of power (positional, personal, and relational)?
-  Who are the stakeholders who will be affected by your initiative, what are their core interests, and how powerful is each stakeholder?
-  For each key stakeholder, what will be the key source of resistance?
-  What individual and collective power and influence tactics could you use to persuade each key stakeholder to join in your efforts?

The Cultural Lens

Those who use the cultural perspective assume that people take action as a function of the meanings they assign to situations.  We all make sense of situations in terms of our past history, analogies and metaphors, language categories, our observations of others, and so forth.  These meanings are not given, but rather are constructed from the bits and pieces of social life.  For example, the same situation can be presented or “framed” as a gain or loss, depending on the comparison (e.g., a 10% profit seems like a gain, but what if your competition had a 20% profit?), and decisions can change when framed differently. More broadly, cultural elements, the symbols, stories, and experiences from which meanings are derived, are shared among members of a culture and transmitted to new members.  Cultures develop over time as groups solve important problems and pass on their traditions.  Culture is a way of life; it is what we do around here and why we do it.  It is customs and laws, heroes and villains, art and science. Although thinking of science as culture may seem strange, historians and sociologists of science (including Thomas Kuhn, a long-time faculty member at MIT) think of science as a shared way of doing things among a community of practice.  The triumph of one scientific theory over another is not automatic, based on an accumulation of incontrovertible facts, but rather a process of assembling arguments about the meaning of observations among contesting parties.  Often, a new theory wins out over an old theory because of its esthetic elegance or the social status of its adherents rather than its fit to the facts; the proponents of the old theory gradually retire and the new generation accepts the new theory as if it had always been true and even obvious. However, there is a deeper level of culture that is sometimes unarticulated or even unconscious.  What does it mean that MIT has a long list of rules and regulations for everything from hiring to purchasing to registering for classes, yet members of the MIT community routinely ignore or circumvent the rules? No wonder MIT celebrates the famous “hacks” or pranks, such as placing giant Mickey Mouse ears on the great dome. This explains why a faculty advisor rarely asks an MIT undergraduate to take fewer courses: it would violate a cultural assumption that individuals should work as hard as they want, in order to do what they think is right. From a cultural perspective, organizations are social systems in which people must work and live together, and therefore the management of meaning is as critical as the management of money and production.  From this perspective, key questions to ask about an organization are:
-  What are your key objectives and planned initiative?
-  What are the existing organizational or subgroup values?
-  What existing symbols, rituals or ceremonies communicate these values to employees?
-  What socialization practices instill these values in new employees?
-  Is the existing culture similar across subunits within the organization (e.g. occupational groups, offices in different locations)? If not, how does it vary? What challenges (and potential opportunities) does this introduce?
-  Does your initiative fit with or try to change the organizational or subgroup culture?

Putting The Lenses Together

It is tempting to think that the three lenses can be combined into a comprehensive model of organizations or a unitary diagnosis of an organization.  In effect, such an idea is a return to the Structural Design perspective, augmented with some additional political and cultural content.  The Political and Cultural lenses are inherently pluralistic.  There is no rational answer or algorithm to combine stakeholder interests; those with different sources of power will advocate principles that favor their groups.  Those with power prefer the equity principle, which rewards groups that already have legitimacy; those without power often prefer equality, since the disenfranchised usually outnumber the

power holders.  The meaning of fairness or the importance of goals depends on the perceiver.  From these viewpoints, action must be negotiated among interest groups and meaning makers. Consider, for example, the creation of the Schwartzman School of Computing at MIT.  Computing, in the broadest sense, is at the heart of many new technologies, products, and companies.  Computing has changed and continues to change our economy and our society.  Historically, MIT had Computer Science as a part of the Electrical Engineering Department, and tried to balance enormous student demand while maintaining a broad and diverse educational institution. Tensions and conflicts over hiring, curriculum, status and identity increased with the growing importance of computing, AI, Big Data, and so forth. Energized by a large gift from Stephen A. Schwartzman, the senior leaders of MIT initiated a process to engage the MIT community in conversations around restructuring MIT to take advantage of the revolution in computing, with consideration of its social and ethical implications.  The decision was made to create a new entity called a “College” within MIT.  Historically, MIT had no colleges, but instead had Schools such as Engineering and Management.  The intent was not to form yet another school, but to build a structure to encourage connections with all of MIT.  The idea was that, along with the new School of Computing, there would be a set of bridging groups of faculty that were “bi-lingual” in bringing computing expertise to bear on issues and ideas from other disciplines, such as computational physics, computational biology, computational linguistics, and so forth.  New faculty would be hired directly into the new college, but new faculty would also be hired into bridging positions. MIT as a whole was seeking to produce new educational and scientific advances through the interdisciplinary opportunities that emerged. At first reading, the above plan sounds like a structural design approach with redesign of the organization chart and resources allocated according to new priorities.  And in the minds of some of the originators of this concept, it most likely was a structural design approach.  However, there was recognition by many that, by itself, a structural reorganization might not achieve the desired goals. The creation of the Schwartzman School of Computing not only changed the organizational structure of MIT, but also challenged the culture and power bases of MIT.  A top-down redesign would have created turf battles, resistance to change, and deep questions about the identity of MIT and its place in the world. So, the President, Provost, Chair of the Faculty, and other key leaders spent many months holding discussions with faculty from every department at MIT, explaining the opportunities and asking for ideas.  It is often in implementation processes like these that we see the political and cultural lenses in action. A broad range of stakeholders were invited and engaged in the early stages of decision making. Five working groups were formed involving over 100 MIT faculty, students, and staff: Organizational Structure, Faculty Appointments, Curriculum and Degrees, Social Implications and Responsibilities of

Computing, and College Infrastructure.  This implementation process was consistent with MIT’s culture, which dictates broad community involvement, innovation, fact-based decision making, and distributed leadership. Each working group discussed multiple ideas about the Schwartzman college to detail options and understand pros and cons. Together, the groups decided that there would be a continuing iterative process of design and modification including ongoing engagement and interaction among stakeholders. Using all three lenses does not guarantee the right answer.  Complex situations don’t have a right answer.  Systems don’t have root causes; they have causal relationships.  However, an analysis that considers and combines all three lenses is more likely to reveal the complex interdependencies of the organization, the difficulties of implementing change, and the heterogeneity among individuals and groups. A richer analysis suggests more ways to intervene to bring change, support change, overcome resistance, and achieve desired outcomes. So, when you work in a team, analyze the team using all three lenses.  From the structural design lens, think about: What is the goal?  Who is on the team?  How are tasks allocated?  What roles have we created?  How do we communicate to link across roles and tasks?  How have we aligned ourselves with plans, schedules, rewards?  Use the political lens to consider stakeholders and their interests.  Who has what sources of power?  How do we handle conflicts?  From the cultural lens, ask yourself what symbols and rituals the group has developed, what beliefs are espoused, and what assumptions underlie the observable features of group life?  What subcultures exist, and how do they differ in the meanings they assign? One of the best ways to learn to use the lenses is to analyze a real initiative in a real organization.  When organizations try to change in some way (or, more accurately, when someone tries to initiate change in organizations, such as the Schwartzman College of Computing at MIT), this often raises explicit discussions of important organizational issues.  Proponents of change often compare the way things are now (bad) with the way things will be after the change (good).  Contradictions, aspirations, and coalitions surface in observable ways.  Individuals and groups are assigned to gather information, benchmark other organizations, consider the barriers to change, and think outside of the box.  They may even value the insights of outsiders striving to understand where they are and where they could go.  It’s a great opportunity to learn a lot, and to make a difference.
