A POLITICAL PERSPECTIVE ON ORGANIZATIONS1

A political perspective on organizations emphasizes that organizations are political systems. This perspective is concerned with elements of organizational life such as power, interests, conflict, informal influence, networks, and coalition-building. These elements are the reality of organizational life in all kinds of organizations, whether non-profits, start-ups, or large, established firms. For many people, political behavior has a negative connotation; it raises images of backroom dealings and shady behavior. However, the political perspective sensitizes us to the fact that all behavior is political—and failure to recognize this can result in one’s being ineffective in organizations. The use of power, therefore, should be seen not as a ruthless pursuit of one’s own interests, but an endeavor that allows an individual to be effective and potentially create positive change and impact for a broad set of stakeholders. Understanding how to build and leverage power is essential for taking effective action in organizations.1 WHAT IS POWER? Power is “the potential ability to influence behavior, to change the course of events, and to overcome resistance.”2 Actors engage in political action in organizations when they set objectives, and determine the key sources of formal and informal power they can use to accomplish these objectives, when they identify key stakeholders who will be affected by any new initiative, and when they map the core interests of these stakeholders and how powerful each of them are. And, they engage in political action when they identify sources of resistance for each stakeholder, and attempt to individually or collectively influence each key stakeholder to join in a change effort.

Political action is an organizational reality; it is how things get done in organizations. Many otherwise intelligent and effective managers have run into problems because they failed to recognize the political nature of the situation they were in, and were “blindsided” by someone whose position and strength they had not anticipated. Organizational actors who effectively engage in political behavior have the potential to shape their organizational environments for the better, while those who do not are destined to be more highly constrained by their environments. KEY BUILDING BLOCKS OF THE POLITICAL PERSPECTIVE There are several key elements of organizational life that the political perspective draws our attention to: sources of power and stakeholders and interests. Sources of Power

How much power organizational actors can exercise comes from a range of sources— positional power, personal power, and network power.

Positional Power

Positional power is the power we immediately think of—power stemming from formal hierarchical position. From the political perspective, the organization chart is more than a design that specifies reporting responsibilities; it also provides a good guide to the vertical power system of the organization. Those who occupy positions at the top of subunits have formal power over those below them in that unit, based on their control over several processes. These processes include: resource allocation (for example, whether someone gets a new computer or an enhanced travel budget); information flows (subordinates have the formal obligation to share information with the person to whom they report); evaluation of the employee’s performance, (which affects both current rewards and future job possibilities); task assignments (who gets the interesting and high-profile tasks, and who gets stuck with the unrewarding or unpleasant tasks); and conflict resolution (disputes between subordinates are usually decided by the formal superior, although most organizations have some avenues of appeal for certain kinds of conflicts, such as those that involve discrimination or unfair process). One of the paradoxes of power based on position in the organizational hierarchy, however, is that excessive reliance on the control processes listed above can undermine that power. The classic definition of positional power is power that is accepted as legitimate by those who are subject to it. When a boss encounters resistance from subordinates, heavy reliance on sanctions can transform authority into coercion. Coercion is rarely accepted as legitimate, either by those subject to it or by those who are merely observing it. When people talk in negative terms about power in their organizations, they are often thinking about the coercive use of formally conferred power to control behavior (for example, the boss who tells a subordinate, “Unless you stop objecting and work on this project, I’ll see to it that you never get another promotion in this company”).  Coercion often backfires, because it breeds resentment and a desire to get even somehow; the compliance that results is seldom wholehearted, and often conceals covert resistance and even sabotage. Indeed, studies show that when organizational members, especially high-ranking ones, use their power for advancing their individual interests, it can lead to mistrust and destructive behaviors like people in the organization being afraid to speak up. But, power can also be used to achieve collective goals or interests.3 Personal Power

While the organizational chart is a good guide to the vertical power system of the organization, it is not an accurate guide to the horizontal system. In other words, equivalence of position on the formal organization chart is not a good indicator of relative power in the organization. The executive vice-presidents of a company may occupy equivalent positions on the organization chart, but they usually differ significantly in their power relative to each other, because of the importance of two other sources of power—personal power and network power.

Personal power is power derived from an individual’s unique personal attributes and skills such as expertise, track record, and social skills. Expertise—the mastery of a skill or body of knowledge that is both valued by the organization and relatively scarce can be a significant source of power.4 A particularly gifted innovator in an R&D organization, a software engineer with exceptional ability at diagnosing problems, a skilled mediator in an organization facing gender disparity problems—these individuals can become significantly more powerful than their less skilled

counterparts. They also become central in the task and advice networks that are part of organizational networks of power and influence (discussed below).

At the organizational level, individuals, groups or subunits with scarce expertise can also gain substantial power in the organization. A classic study of power in organizations is a study of a manufacturing firm where one of the most powerful groups was the maintenance workers. The factory was highly capital-intensive, which meant that if the complex machinery broke down, the performance of the factory suffered until it came back on line. The maintenance workers had a monopoly on the expertise required for diagnosing and fixing the machinery, and therefore the factory manager deferred to their demands and interests much more attentively than to those of any other unit or sub-group in the plant.5 Sometimes, the power of a given subunit is based on larger macroeconomic trends. For example, in recent decades in the U.S., the finance department of many corporations steadily gained influence because, as firms diversified, financial analysis and access to financial markets came to be crucially important elements of the control systems and strategies of multi-business firms.6 Today, we see a similar phenomenon occurring with individuals or subunits involved in data analytics. Track record, or past performance, is another source of personal power for both individuals and subunits. Individuals who have a history of successful achievement in their assignments generally have greater power than those with less outstanding records. One reason is that they are often identified as “high potentials”—people who are likely to rise higher in the organization. Others are therefore usually more eager to cooperate with them, in expectation of future valuable good relationships. People are also more likely to be eager to interact with them, and therefore they are often better able to expand their access to information, which is one of the most important organizational resources. In addition, they are usually able to elicit a higher level of effort and commitment from those with whom they work.7 Similar patterns hold at the level of subunits. A division or department that has a record of outstanding past performance—the most consistently profitable division, for example, or the plant with the highest quality rating—usually has more power in the organization than similar units without such strong performance records. Successful divisions can often claim higher levels of resources, and they attract skilled and ambitious employees.

Social skills are also a source of personal power. Research on personal characteristics and the effective use of power has often been linked to the concept of “charisma.” Max Weber, one of the pioneers of sociology and of the study of power, used this term to mean the kind of power that derives from a deep emotional connection between leader and followers based on the distinctive personal qualities of the charismatic leader and on the distress or tension experienced by the followers, which the leader promises to alleviate.8 Other social skills that provide a base for effective political action in organizations include: focus; flexibility (concentrating on ultimate objectives and adjusting to what is possible in a particular context); energy and physical stamina; the ability to tolerate conflict; the ability to submerge one’s ego and get along; and sensitivity to others (understanding who they are, their position on the issues, and how best to communicate with and influence them).9 For example, those in staff roles inside organizations (e.g. mental health professionals hired by the US Army to provide rehabilitative services to active duty soldiers suffering from conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder) often struggle to influence the behavior of line managers (e.g. Army commanders), even when their ability to do their job depends on it. Studies have shown that these relatively lower power staff members can achieve influence, but that it requires savvy and sensitivity to what the people they are trying to influence care about. For example, one study shows that it involves seizing

early windows of opportunity to build relationships before conflicts emerge, and using perspectivetaking techniques to try to construct integrative solutions to the conflicts that inevitably arise.10

Since conflict is unavoidable in organizations, a willingness to engage in conflict to further one’s individual and collective interests is a source of power as well, particularly if others are conflict-avoidant. Finally, because interdependence is an unavoidable feature of action in organizations, a willingness to put one’s ego aside in order to listen and expand one’s information and understanding, build alliances, share credit, and get buy-in from others is an essential personal quality in today’s organizational settings. For example, when the top management team of a carsharing company decided to shift its strategy to focus more on marketing, the new strategy shifted the balance of power from engineering, which had built the company’s cutting-edge technology, towards marketing, which was better able to contribute to new demands, like satisfying the needs of mainstream customers. Perhaps not surprisingly, engineers had a strong negative reaction to the new strategy, and began to resist working with the marketers. Marketers relied on the work of engineers to launch important new initiatives, and it was the marketers who were more willing to share power with engineers who were able to secure cooperation from them.11

Network power A final source of power is network power—power derived an individual’s relationships with others (social capital) and knowledge of the social landscape. Both company gossip and social research have long recognized that “know-who” is as important as “know-how” in any organization. As organizations become increasingly flat and distributed, the kind of influence that comes from social networks is often much more significant than the authority that derives from formal hierarchical position. Four aspects of networks that are important to network power are network breadth, network depth, structural power, and network closure.

Network breadth refers to the number of individuals or groups with whom an individual can reach and connect. The number of people in an individual’s network, and the number of powerful people in it, are key factors in the extent to which a social network is a source of power. One of the oldest strategies for gaining power is to do favors for others, thereby building up an extensive network of obligations that can be redeemed later to serve an individual’s own interests or the interests of another person in order to expand an individual’s obligation network.12 This strategy rests on the premise that doing something for others, as a giver rather than a taker, builds a sense of obligation, even if at the time there is no explicit expectation of an immediate return. The tacit expectation is that someday, in some way, the favor will be reciprocated. Network depth is a measure of the amount of time, emotional intensity, intimacy (mutual confiding), and reciprocity which characterize the relationship. The transfer of tacit knowledge requires a strong network connection, usually characterized by trust. Yet, while one might think that strong ties are most important, weak ties can be important also. For example, a famous study—one of the most cited in the social sciences—described the “strength of weak ties,” by demonstrating how job seekers benefitted when they had connections with acquaintances (versus close friends) who could expose them to job opportunities they would otherwise miss.13

Structural power is based on the insight that individuals low in the formal hierarchy can assume and wield considerable power and influence despite having no formal authority to do so.14 Their informal influence is derived from a structural position in the organization that gives them control over important resources. Low level employees may play a central role in an information or

## Page 5

friendship network, perform tasks that are critical for others, or control uncertainty stemming from the external environment.15 For example, a study of two hospitals in which managers tried to persuade primary-care doctors to emphasize prevention over intervention and use evidence-based guidelines to treat patients with chronic illnesses like diabetes showed that managers at one hospital were much more successful at getting doctors to change than at the other.16 At the hospital that was successful, managers enlisted the aid of entry-level medical assistants to help change the practices of the doctors with whom they worked. At both hospitals, the medical assistants had traditionally been seen as the “Maitre D’s” of primary care; they were responsible for bringing patients from the waiting area to the exam room, weighing them, and taking their blood pressure. And yet despite having no formal authority over doctors, the medical assistants had a high capacity for influence because they had a lot of structural power. First, the medical assistants occupied a central position in the doctors’ workflow (workflow centrality). They both could remind doctors to implement new practices such as delivering particular vaccinations, and could give doctors opportunities to review and approve suggested changes before they were implemented. Second, they performed tasks that were critical to doctors’ daily work (task criticality), making it relatively easier for them to ask the doctors for favors; this led doctors to see themselves as implementing the new practices to help their own medical assistants rather than to meet managerial demands. Third, the medical assistants were central in the doctors’ peer network (peer network centrality), enabling them to spread the word about those doctors who had adopted the new practices. When a medical assistant introduced a new process with even one doctor in their group, they could tell that doctor’s colleagues ‘Well, I’m already doing this with Dr. So-and-So,’ which made his or her peers far less resistant to change. And fourth, they were positioned between the patients and the doctors (control over uncertainty stemming from the external environment), so they could suggest changes in doctors’ practices while also shielding doctors from the emotional challenges of upset or angry patients. Managers at the successful hospital leveraged the structural power of the medical assistants to help change the practices of the doctors with whom they worked.

Finally, network closure refers to the connectedness of contacts in a network. While initial research on networks emphasized the importance of whom people knew, we have discovered that it is also important to consider how a person’s contacts are connected to each other. The pattern of the network itself matters.  Also, there is no optimal network. The optimal network depends on the performance goal.  If the performance goal is implementation, those who have cohesive network have an advantage; in contrast, if the performance goal is innovation, those who have a bridging network have an advantage (Figure 1).17

A cohesive network (also called a network with high closure) is important for implementation. Interlocking relationships create a wide bandwidth for information flow, thereby enhancing communication and personal visibility in a group or team.18 Also, individuals are less likely to violate norms in cohesive networks because reputation costs are high for individuals who express opinions or behavior inconsistent with group norms and standards. So, it is less risky to place trust within a cohesive group. And, productivity is enhanced as people become self-aligning in extraordinary efforts (lowering labor costs, monitoring, quality, and increasing speed).19

While a cohesive network is helpful for implementation, a bridging network (also called a brokerage network)—a network rich in structural holes, where there is no direct link between individuals or subunits that could benefit from being linked—is helpful for innovation. Gaps or disconnects between contacts provide quick access to diverse information. In particular, they afford access to information about 1) best practices (something that the other group thinks or does that could be valuable in one’s own area), 2) alternative perspectives (thinking “inside” another box is usually

more valuable than thinking “outside” one’s own box), and 3) synergy (behaviors and processes in the other group that can be combined with one’s own know-how and expertise to create value).20

Acting as the informal bridge or broker connecting the individuals or units across structural holes in a network is a significant source of power.21  Brokers can use two sets of practices— connecting practices and buffering practices—to bridge different groups with disparate expertise, identities, and interests.22 For example, in a website development firm, members of Client Services, Project Management, Creative, and Technology departments had different expertise, identities, and interests. Client Services members had authority over identifying client requirements over time, developing client presentations and documentation, and maintaining client relationships. Project Management members created project work plans, developing milestones and measures, monitoring progress, and adjusting plans and schedules over time. Creative members were responsible for the overall aesthetics of the website. They designed visual images, generated graphic concepts and vocabularies, and created user navigations and web process flows. Finally, the Technology members wrote, reused, revised, tested, and debugged program code, and interfaced with technology standards, technology vendors, and client IT groups. In such situations, brokers can use connecting practices to transfer information across groups by developing repositories, specifications, and standards that support communication across boundaries. They can translate cultural meanings across groups by enabling group members to acknowledge and appreciate the other group’s perspective. And, they can transform interests across groups by facilitating a process of negotiation that allows localized knowledge to be transformed into jointly produced knowledge that transcends each community’s local interests.23

Connecting practices—transferring information, translating meanings, and transforming interests between groups—can be quite useful for facilitating cross-group collaboration in situations where the majority of the tasks required are high status, high value, and enable the targeted groups to use their specialized expertise and display their deeply held identities. However, when the tasks required for cross-group collaboration are low status, low value, or do not enable the targeted groups to employ their expertise and identities, brokers may be more successful using buffering practices— managing information rather than transferring it, matching meanings rather than translating them, and maintaining interests rather than transforming them—in order to accomplish cross-group work. For example, in the past, community health centers in the U.S. treated low-income patients’ medical problems by providing medical diagnosis and treatment; now, many community health centers also address the legal problems that can affect patients’ health and well-being. To treat a child with asthma, for instance, the patient's family now may not only be given treatment drugs, but also legal assistance in pressing landlords to resolve a mold or mice problem in the apartment that exacerbates the condition. By teaming lawyers and doctors together, community health centers can both facilitate legal reform for large numbers of the poor and solve legal problems related to their health problems. However, both doctors and lawyers have hard-won expertise, deeply held values and identities, and strong material and status interests related to performing the core tasks of their profession. Therefore, they may not coordinate with one another across group boundaries if new tasks represent professionally ill-defined problems that require them to acquire information unrelated to their specialized expertise, use understandings and work practices that conflict with their professional identity, or do low-value tasks that threaten their professional interests. Here, brokers can use buffering practices—by managing information (blocking irrelevant information and constructing relevant information so that neither doctors nor lawyers need to acquire any of the other group’s professional expertise), matching meanings (matching understandings and work practices so that doctors and lawyers do not need to make compromises in their own professional values and identities to implement the program), and maintaining interests (doing routine and tangential work so that

## Page 7

doctors and lawyers do not need to make compromises in their valued tasks). By doing this buffering work, brokers can play a key role in implementing reform by acting as safeguards and coordinators for established professions.24

Knowledge of the Social Landscape Given the complexity of social networks, we should not be surprised by the finding in a number of network studies that most managers do not have an accurate picture of the social networks of those who work for and with them.25 One reason is that there are many different kinds of networks, each with its own structure and dynamics. For example, there are task-related networks (with whom do people communicate to solve work-related problems), friendship networks, and advice networks (mentoring relationships). Another reason is that networks are dynamic. Task-related networks change as people take on different tasks, advice networks change as career paths evolve, and even friendship networks change over time as people enter and exit groups and organizations. Just because you understand the network at one point in time does not mean that you have a solid grasp of what it looks like a year later.

But, being able to “see” the overall network is an important source of power for several reasons. First, it tells you the location of resources. Who has (or has access to) key components, inventory, people? Second, it helps you predict what coalitions exist or are likely to form. For example, which people are friends outside work in addition to inside? Third, it helps you see where informal power lies. Who knows the ropes or is well-connected?

Emerging technologies and research methods are helping us to better understand networks. For example, those studying networks can now use tools such as “SocioMeters”—wearable sensors that measure face-to-face interactions between people with an infrared transceiver, a microphone, and two accelerometers; these allows researchers to capture social interactions and model the structure and dynamics of social networks in ways that are more accurate than self-reports.26 They can also use analysis of company communication media, and use this tracking to locate groups of employees who interact frequently, and identify employees who are central and peripheral in organizational networks.27 While these kinds of formal network analysis can be a useful tool for solving particular problems, given the short life of the accuracy of a network map, their greatest value may lie in sensitizing managers to the complexity and importance of informal networks in their organization’s capacity for getting things done.

Summing up Sources of Power Power in organizations derives from a number of sources: positional, personal, and network. Obviously, the more sources of power individuals have, the more power they potentially command, although the cumulative effect of power across sources is never simple and linear. This is because an important source of power is the match between style, skills and capacities and what is required by the situation.28 This is true not only of the fit between personal sources of power and context, but of each potential source of power. Some sources are more important in a particular organization or context than others. In flatter organizations, for example, influence based on informal networking is often the most important source of power, especially if it is reinforced with control of scarce resources and expertise. In more traditional, hierarchical organizations, on the other hand, people in formal positions of authority are the ones best able to enhance their influence through expanding their

informal networks. Someone without formal authority who tries to enhance his or her influence through informal networking is apt to be punished for trying to circumvent established channels. Interests and Stakeholders

In addition to the importance of individuals’ various sources of power, the political perspective draws our attention to the importance of interests and stakeholders.

Interests

Interests refer to what people want—what’s at stake for them in a decision or course of action. The political perspective shares with economics the fundamental assumption that people act rationally to serve their own interests. Where the two approaches differ is in their analysis of “interests.” The classic economic model of homo economicus (“economic man”) is an individual acting on his or her own personal interests, which, however numerous and varied they may be, are reducible to a common economic currency that allows that individual to compare the relative value or “utility” of meeting each interest. The political perspective acknowledges the importance of individual interests, but it broadens their scope beyond what can be calculated in terms of some dollar or net present value amount to include a variety of interests (such as autonomy and status) that are difficult to reduce to economic terms. Depending on how an action affects their interests, people will support or oppose it, and managers often run into trouble because they fail to recognize what’s at stake for other units or for other individuals in their proposed course of action. For example, managers in a software delivery firm launched a change initiative through its product management team that was designed to improve innovation by splitting innovation into three streams: maximizing existing core businesses, diversifying product offerings, and disrupting the core business by developing new technology and delivery. One might have expected that engineers would be most interested in working on the streams that were diversifying product offerings or disrupting the core business, since the work on legacy initiatives might be less interesting from an engineering standpoint. However, it turned out that engineers were most interested in working on maximizing existing core businesses, because this initiative received the most resources since it was directly tied to revenue. Engineers working on this innovation stream had the ability to get the resources they needed for product development.

Simply recognizing that interests are important is the first step in developing an ability to use the political perspective to take more effective action in organizations. The next step is much harder: analyzing what those interests are and what priority they have for key individual and collective actors. Interests are both complex and dynamic. People have multiple interests at the individual level. They have, for example, both short-term goals and long-term ambitions. They want both autonomy and cooperative relations with others. They want to have a reputation for dedication to their work, and to meet personal quality of life aspirations. They are also members of multiple stakeholder groups which have different and sometimes competing interests. For example, an employee at a social media company might on one hand want to limit governmental scrutiny of its firm’s practices in hopes of their stock remaining as valuable as possible; but as a user of the site too, they may have concerns about data privacy.

Furthermore, interests at both the individual and collective levels are dynamic: that is, they change in content and in relative importance over time and as context changes. For example, as a younger single employee I may not see my interests as being strongly affected by a company’s efforts

## Page 9

to change its benefits plans, including medical coverage. However, if I develop a chronic health problem, the company’s benefits plan may suddenly assume much greater salience among my various interests. This last example illustrates another factor in the complexity and dynamism of interests. Interests may be latent: that is, I may not realize that I have a certain stakeholder interest until it is evoked by circumstances—or by someone trying to mobilize my support for a certain course of action. Stakeholders The political perspective calls our attention to the concept of stakeholders. The term “stakeholders” identifies groups that have a shared “stake” (a set of collective interests) that is affected by what the organization is and how it does it. Each stakeholder group not only has common interests but shares an awareness of those interests and a willingness to act to further them. The stakeholder perspective identifies both internal stakeholders (those within the organization) and external stakeholders (including suppliers, customers, communities, and shareholders).

Internal stakeholders vary considerably both in the extent to which they themselves are organized and in the kind of ties they construct with external stakeholder groups. For example, in companies such as Uber or Airbnb, those selling their services (transporting others, renting their homes) are not employees of the firm and often have weak ties with one another. In contrast, some companies have employee unions, where workers are tightly connected with one another, and organize to advance their collective interests.29 These different ways of organizing affect how workers achieve alignment with one another, and with management.

Individuals and groups will support, be indifferent to, or try to block organizational actions based on how those actions affect their interests. To carry an action forward, its initiator needs to understand which stakeholders will be affected by it. IMPORTANT ISSUES RELATED TO THE POLITICAL PERSPECTIVE In this section, we address three important issues related to the political perspective: building a network, using power and influence, and the future of organizational power. Building a Network Your informal network is potentially your most important source of influence, both because of the resources you can mobilize by using it, and because it can provide you with direct knowledge of the interests of individuals and groups elsewhere in the organization. You can greatly enhance your capacity to take action if you put time, effort, and thought into constructing and maintaining an effective network.30 An effective network extends in three directions: upward, to those who are in higher positions of formal authority; horizontally, to those in adjacent units who are similar in formal position; and downward, to those who are working for you or who are engaged in tasks that have important consequences for your own work. The structural design of the organization provides you with a set of networks in each position you occupy, through its grouping and linking systems: you have a boss to whom you report, subordinates who report to you, and you participate in task forces and projects. You can decide, however, whether to reach beyond the links handed to you by the organization design, and

## Page 10

whether to maintain those networks when you move to a new position. We all know people who stay in touch with key individuals they have encountered on task forces, projects, and committee assignments, and who maintain a connection with their former bosses and co-workers when they move on to new positions. We also know people who take the time to seek out and build networks with people to whom they have no formal ties—with their boss’s administrative assistant, for example. As you build your networks, be mindful of the different kinds of networks that are important to have—for example, task networks, which relate to getting your work done, and developmental networks, which relate to your managing your career. Many individuals begin their careers searching for the perfect mentor. But, research suggests that it is better to pursue a strategy of building a network of developmental relationships. Moreover, rather than ask where you can find the “perfect” mentor, it may be more useful to consider how you might be the “perfect” protégé, by, for example, always delivering high performance and therefore making your mentor’s work life easier.31 In addition, it is important to remember that mentors are important for advice; but it is also important to have sponsors—those with power who can advocate for you.32

Much of the advice on constructing effective networks emphasizes the importance of reciprocity and trust.33 Reciprocity means the shared expectation that if you do something for me, I have an obligation to return that service by doing something of importance for you. In some cases, this reciprocity is direct: you do something for me, and I do something equivalent for you. In some contexts, however, the reciprocity is indirect, as in mentoring relationships: I play the role of mentor to you, and you in turn honor my efforts by being a mentor in your turn to someone more junior. As you and I establish a reputation for honoring social obligations, others will trust both of us to do the same with them. While people often think that they may not have anything to offer someone else, there are multiple “currencies” that people value, including: rewards-related currencies (salary, bonus, promotion, assignment to interesting projects); task-related currencies (assistance, information, cooperation, and resources relevant to assigned tasks); relationship-related currencies (acceptance, inclusion, support, understanding); and status-related currencies (recognition of importance, deference, “perks” that confer prestige). You can improve your networks by understanding which currencies have most value for which actors in the organization, and taking advantage of the fact that different currencies are valued differently by different individuals and by different groups and units in the organization. For example, status-related currencies may be very important to one unit: whose offices host the meeting? Who presents the agenda?  Another unit may place a higher value on taskrelated currencies, and be willing to exchange status-related currency for access to task-related information. Understanding these differences enables you to build effective exchange relationships with both groups and between them.

One important thing to remember about networks is that it is hard to build a network to serve a purpose. The more instrumental one appears, the more one appears to be “taking.” Networks must exist to serve purposes as they arise. So, it’s important to build ties before you need them, to make networking a way of life. Also, we tend to build ties with people who are located close to us geographically or who are similar to us. But, these ties are often less useful, because these people tend to have access to the same set of ideas and resources as we do. Instead, it’s helpful to build ties to people who are more distant. One way to do this is by engaging in shared activities around a common point of interest with people you don’t know. When you join with others in community service activities, sports teams, or interdepartmental initiatives, this allows for unscripted behavior, passion,

## Page 11

and opportunities for celebration and commiseration, all of which help to generate loyalty and form strong bonds. Using Power and Influence As emphasized throughout this note, all organizations are political. Many people in today’s organizations are uncomfortable talking about power, particularly their own.34 People want to think that the world is predictable and comprehensible, and that if they just do a good job, things will take care of themselves. And, in many books about leadership, famous leaders gloss over the exercise of power and influence that they used to succeed. Yet, those who are effective in organizations of all kinds engage in stakeholder mapping, under sources of resistance for each key stakeholder group, and tailor individual and collective influence tactics to particular stakeholders. Mapping Stakeholders

Stakeholder mapping is a key tactic for helping you to identify the interests of those who will be affected by your planned change and how salient those interests are for them (that is, which have the greatest priority for them).35 It also pushes you to identify more than one interest for each major stakeholder. This is a useful corrective to a common tendency to over-simplify the motivations of others, and to attribute to them a single-mindedness that we know would be impossible for us. It also alerts us to latent interests: that is, interests that stakeholders might have but of which they are not yet explicitly aware. For example, business unit managers may initially be excited by top managers’ introduction of an internal incubator to explore and accelerate new product development. However, if business unit managers begin to perceive that the incubator will not yield products applicable to their industry, they may begin to resist having their top engineers spending time working on incubator projects rather than business unit projects.

To do stakeholder mapping around a new initiative, you need to understand who is affected by what you want to do, what their interests are, and how much power they have to facilitate or to block what you propose to do. It can be useful to map stakeholders along two dimensions: agreement with the initiative and power of the stakeholder. Here are some key questions to consider:

-  Who are the key stakeholders who will have an interest in this initiative?
-  How powerful is each stakeholder?

Finding allies is a crucial element of a successful political strategy. A coalition is a set of allies who act together to support certain policies and activities. A long-lasting, coalition can form around common interests whereby a general coalition of allies provides reciprocal support for each other’s interests. One of the most common examples of a general coalition is the “dominant coalition” in an organization, which is composed of a set of upper-level executives. Each member can expect the support of the other members for his or her initiatives on the understanding that he or she will support those of the others. For example, a Chief Marketing Officer might support the Chief Human Resources Officer’s request to spend more money on training, so long as this does not result in the Marketing group having to cut its headcount. Generally, general coalitions are based on longstanding patterns of reciprocity—that is, of exchanges that over time maintain a roughly equal balance. The exchange of information and resources, mutual willingness to do favors for allies, and an agreement (tacit or explicit) not to take actions that seriously damage the interests of allies are key elements of successful coalitions.

A coalition can also be built to support a specific course of action: for example, to pressure a company to decrease its carbon footprint or to put pressure on a company to fire employees who have violated the company’s sexual harassment policy. One challenge in building a coalition around a specific issue is identifying potential allies whose interests will benefit from your proposed course of action, and then making them aware of your initiative and of how it will serve their interests.38 Stakeholder mapping can provide you with guidance in identifying potential allies and in finding out what interests are potentially compatible with your initiative. All too often, someone will initiate a course of action thinking that its virtues and advantages will be obvious to everyone. This is rarely the case in organizations: the advantages of a course of action need to be articulated, often in informal settings where frank discussions of interests and outcomes are possible. Finding allies in advance of formal decision points is an important success factor for managers in organizations. The Future of Organizational Power

Technological advances are affecting power dynamics inside organizations and in the environments they operate within, just as they are affecting organizational culture and design. For example, technologies such as social media—a group of Web-based technologies that allow users to

## Page 14

easily create, share and evaluate content—are having a transformative effect on society.39 As social media bring unprecedented levels of voice, information and ability to connect to everyday people, they are changing relationships between organizational actors and outside stakeholders. For example, studies have shown how some hoteliers, now that they are subject to TripAdvisor reviews, are changing their routines—adding, for example, daily meetings to discuss the latest reviews—in response to a world where they are under constant, highly public scrutiny by guests.40 Other studies show how professionals such as car salesmen can no longer use well-established tactics (e.g. test drives, withholding information, new car smell) to command a higher price from customers, because communication over the Web before the customer ever enters the shop puts them in a better bargaining position than in the past.41 To take another example, whereas doctors may once have been a patient’s sole source of medical information, now people can easily access a vast set of medical information on sites like Web MD and in free, online communities such as PatientsLikeMe, where patients share data with one another.42

As companies like PatientsLikeMe and TripAdvisor show, we are witnessing the rise of a class of firms that leverage social media to do traditional work—medical research, travel reviews—in new ways. These companies also need to navigate shifting power dynamics. For example, professionals inside these organizations need to manage their relationship with users who keep the business going through their participation, but are often unpaid. A study of PatientsLikeMe shows that professionals may struggle to at once achieve their goal (e.g. amassing a large, organized set of patient medical data) while on the other hand not alienating the customers (e.g. patients) that volunteer this data.43 Professionals at the firm decided that a given symptom was not associated with a disease so should not be used for other purposes such as keeping data organized, but patients became upset, feeling that their voice was not being heard. In such cases, managing conflicting interests through actions like highlighting, in the case of PatientsLikeMe, that the firm is “not-justfor-profit,” or explaining why certain decisions have been made, is important.

More broadly, across society, we are seeing changes to how people organize. Many movements—from #MeToo, Black Lives Matter, or Occupy Wall Street—have either started or grown rapidly due to the ease with which people can create and share information. In 2019, Google employees created a social media “blitz” on Instagram and Twitter to call for an end to forced arbitration in sexual harassment suits. Forced arbitration generally means that workers cannot take their employers to court over workplace issues like sexual harassment and assault and must settle their disputes behind closed doors. Facebook employees joined in this effort. Googlers created an Instagram and Twitter account to publish testimonials from employees, and then employees retweeted and reposted them. As former leaders replied and the hashtags caught on, the protest was picked up by mainstream media.

In this context, some have argued that power itself needs to be re-conceptualized. For example, while more conventional conceptualizations of power tend to treat it like currency (where it is a finite resource held by a few and closely guarded), we are seeing the rise of what some regard as “new power,” which is more like a current. This is to say, it is made by many; it is participatory and peer-driven; and the goal is not to hoard it, but to channel it.44

However, it is unclear whether the introduction of new technologies will facilitate a high degree of organizational or societal change. While many organizations use social media to give employees more voice, for example, it does not necessarily mean the employees have more ability to make decisions, though they may be better able to influence them.45 Moreover, many have criticized the ability of online movements inspired by “new power” to actually get things done, perhaps because

## Page 15

the ease with which they rise means they often lack the institution-building efforts needed to grow a robust organization.46 Finally, as critical theorists have pointed out, even the Internet itself, often touted for its democratizing effects, can be viewed as controlled by a handful of profit-seeking companies, sometimes with opaque practices related to issues like privacy.47 ANALYZING ORGANIZATIONS USING THE POLITICAL PERSPECTIVE When analyzing an organization from the political perspective, it is important to ask a series of questions:

-  What are your key objectives and planned initiative?
-  What are your sources of power (positional, personal, and network-based)?
-  Who are the stakeholders who will be affected by your initiative, what are their core interests, and how powerful is each stakeholder?
-  For each key stakeholder, what will be the key source of resistance?
-  What individual and collective power and influence tactics could you use to persuade each key stakeholder to join in your efforts? In conclusion, the ability to analyze an organization’s political system and to take effective political action—to get “buy-in,” find allies, build a network and negotiate—is essential to getting things done in organizations. The most effective political action, however, avoids the political label. People with the best political skills rarely get defined as political “operators.”  Instead, their colleagues praise them as having good people skills, an ability to work with the system, and a capacity to overcome obstacles and opposition. The fundamental insights for gaining such a reputation, are drawn from an understanding of the political perspective on organizations. Using the political perspective can help you learn to be more effective—not only for advancing your own interests, but those of your colleagues, the broader organization, and society at large.
