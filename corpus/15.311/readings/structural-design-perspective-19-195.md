A STRUCTURAL DESIGN PERSPECTIVE ON ORGANIZATIONS1

A structural design perspective focuses on organizations as systems deliberately constructed to achieve certain strategic goals. This perspective highlights that, by understanding basic principles of organization design—by aligning the organization’s design with its strategy, and by making sure that both strategy and design fit the environment in which the organization is operating—managers can enhance their organizations’ effectiveness.1 The structural design lens2 emphasizes the efficiency and effectiveness of the organization. Efficiency involves accomplishing strategic goals with the least possible expenditure of resources; effectiveness involves ensuring that goals are accomplished to the standards necessary for the organization to succeed. WHAT IS STRUCTURAL DESIGN? Structural design is the deliberate crafting of an organization in an attempt to make it best able to achieve its goals. This perspective on organizations is built on the assumption that an organization’s design should be set up in a way that allows the organization to best carry out the key activities required by its organizational strategy. Scholars working with this perspective often use metaphors of the organization as a mechanism or system, of “engineering” or “re-engineering” the organization, of “organizational architecture,” of the organization as a complex organism that can be “diagnosed” like a medical patient. Organizational design choices begin with determining what grouping—that is, the division of activities and individuals into work units—will best allow the organization to implement its strategy. Once activities are divided into “boxes” on the organization chart, the units must be linked according to the nature and level of interdependence in their tasks. This will ensure that information and other needed resources flow effectively and efficiently between the different groups. Finally, the design must use a variety of alignment mechanisms (direct and indirect compensation, training, development, and advancement, etc., discussed below) to ensure that people are motivated to carry out the tasks assigned to them by the grouping structures and the linking mechanisms. KEY BUILDING BLOCKS OF A STRUCTURAL PERSPECTIVE There are several key elements of organizational life that the structural design perspective draws our attention to: grouping, linking, and aligning.

Grouping: Division of Labor Grouping decisions dictate the basic framework within which all other organizational design decisions are made. Grouping gathers together some tasks, functions, or disciplines, and separates them from others. Grouping choices should follow directly from the strategy of the organization as a whole, and the associated strategy of the particular organizational unit. A fundamental assumption of the structural design approach is that coordination and communication are easier and denser within a unit than across units. This means that sharing information and building and adding to a common knowledge base are easier within than across units. Therefore, the most important areas of interdependence should be under a unified reporting structure. The organization’s overall strategy should guide the organizational design by identifying the most strategically important parameters of coordination, interdependence, and knowledge sharing. Grouping focuses on questions of how to cluster tasks and activities. Grouping can be seen as “drawing the boxes” of the organization chart. Historically, organizations have used three basic forms or “ideal types” of grouping: functional structure, divisional structure, or matrix structure. There is no universally ideal choice of grouping pattern. Each strategic grouping option comes with its own set of strengths and weaknesses. Functional Structure Grouping by function (or more broadly, by expertise) brings together individuals who share similar disciplines, skills, and work processes. Functional organizations are the oldest form of business enterprise and this form is still often the form first adopted in new organizations. In the functional organization, all the activities concerned with a particular function are grouped into separate divisions, as shown in Figure 1. Grouping by function has three major strengths. The first is that it allows the development of deep functional expertise and good skill development within each function. These organizations can be extremely innovative in specific technologies or functions. The second strength is economies of scale: that is, the functional organization makes it relatively easy to transfer resources across activities within functions. If sales of one product decline, for example, the manufacturing division can switch a production line or a factory to a product that is in greater demand; engineers can be switched across projects; marketing divisions can reallocate their sales and support efforts. Finally, a functional organization allows each group to create separate incentive and control systems suited to its needs and to reinforcing its strengths. However, these advantages come at the cost of integration across functions. This model often assumes a sequential interdependence across the functions, from upstream (R&D) to downstream (marketing). Functional organizations are often not very responsive to changes in markets or customers. Moreover, as their level of specialization increases, individuals tend to develop narrower perspectives, and have difficulty in solving problems that require joint efforts with other groups. It can be difficult in this kind of organization to assess costs clearly, especially on a product line basis, and to assign accountability for the overall performance of the organization. Finally, because career ladders are primarily within functions and the number of functions is limited, there is a tendency for the number of levels of management in each function to expand over time. Large functional

## Page 3

organizations often have very tall hierarchies that can inhibit speedy and effective information flows. Moreover, because there are few opportunities for managers to gain experience outside their functional areas as they move up the career ladder, the functional organization does not develop a large supply of general managers who can see the organization as a whole and make decisions that serve the organization’s strategy instead of following a specifically functional logic. A functional organization is frequently adopted by new organizations and maintained over time by organizations that have a single major business, or several businesses that share the same technologies and have very similar markets. Divisional Structure The divisional structure gathers together people who perform different activities and tasks and produce different outputs but who serve the same customers or market segments. The most common dimension for division is geography. Large multinational companies operating around the world, for example, historically favored a geographic organization, grouping by geographic region and country. But grouping by service or product line rather than by geographical location is also a variant of this form, as shown in Figure 2. The people within the group perform a variety of different tasks and activities, but they are all contributors to the same final output (a product or set of closely related products or services). For example, firms with a range of product lines and markets usually find the functional organization too inflexible, and instead adopt a product line structure. In this design, pioneered in the U.S. by DuPont and General Motors and in Japan by Matsushita, the functions are distributed across the business or product line they support.

The divisional structure has three major advantages. First is transparency of performance:  it makes the costs and profits of each business much clearer than does the functional structure. Second is the clear strategic focus it provides for the managers of the product division. Third is its capacity for developing deep customer knowledge and close customer relationships. It allows the organization to tailor its products and services to differentiated customer needs. But, it is not without disadvantages. Its three major weaknesses are the duplication of activities and resources, the erosion of deep technical expertise, and missed opportunities for synergies and learning. Making each business unit accountable for its own “P-and-L” (profit and loss statement) can make it difficult for units to share resources and can lead to duplication of activities (each unit wants its own accounting staff, its own training staff, and so on). And because functional specialists are spread across different groups, they can lose their professional focus and become less attuned to breakthrough innovations in their own fields (and this holds true across functions, from R&D to accounting). Distributing activities across business units can also lead to missed learning opportunities in core functions (for example, factories in one business unit may be unaware of potentially useful innovations in another business unit’s factories). It can also make new business creation difficult: business units may focus exclusively on expanding their existing business, instead of finding new opportunities; in others, units compete to “own” new business. In some cases, companies try to break down their “functional silos” by setting up divisions only to discover that they have created “business unit silos” that are just as internally competitive and resistant to developing potential cross-boundary synergies as the old structure had been.

Matrix Structure A matrix structure is an organizational form that picks two strategic grouping dimensions and gives them equal weight in the organization structure, so that both the functional and the divisional structures are implemented simultaneously. The manager of each operating unit reports to two “bosses,” one for each dimension. For example, in a matrix organization such as the one illustrated in Figure 3, the left side of the matrix contains the traditional functional departments (sales & marketing, engineering, operations), which are responsible for maintaining state-of-the-art expertise in each function. The top of the matrix is composed of product groups, with a product manager coordinating the functional activities involved in developing and producing the product and getting it to market. Thus, an engineering manager within an operating unit would report to two bosses: the head of engineering and the product line manager. Matrix structures vary in the number of people who report equally to two bosses. In some, the matrix penetrates deeply into the organization, so that every engineer, for example, would have both an engineering manager and a product manager; in others, only those in relatively high-level management positions have two bosses. Matrix organizations seem to provide a way of balancing two equally important grouping dimensions. However, from every perspective they are more complicated than single-dimension organizations: they require dual systems, roles, controls, and incentives that reflect both dimensions of the matrix. Along with this complexity can come confusion, higher costs, delays in reaching decisions, and a heightened potential for conflict. Often, organizations that adopt a matrix structure find that, despite the formal effort at balancing the two, one dimension tends to be more powerful than the other, negating the potential advantages of the matrix structure. Even when the balance is fairly even, employees often find it extremely difficult to have two “bosses,” and spend a great deal of time in meetings. Many organizations have found that a matrix organization is difficult to maintain. Linking: Coordination Mechanisms While grouping involves differentiating clusters of activities by drawing boundaries around them, linking involves integrating across clusters by creating connections between them. Linking entails designing formal and informal structures and processes to connect and coordinate organizational units and subunits whose tasks are interdependent, but that have been separated by grouping decisions. There are various mechanisms for linking people across organizations: formal reporting structures, special groups, special roles, and technology systems. Formal Reporting Structures: Hierarchy and “Dotted Line” Relationships The organization chart is not simply a “map” of strategic grouping; it is also a map of who must keep whom informed and who has responsibility for linking which activities. For example, in a divisional structure, functional managers report to their respective market-based managers who, in turn, report to the company president. Managers in different functions at the same level coordinate their group’s activities via their common boss, who channels information, controls the type and quantity of information that moves among groups, and adjudicates conflicts. Often, formal direct reporting structures include what is commonly called a “dotted line” hierarchical relationship, indicating that the lower-ranking person is formally responsible for

## Page 5

supplying all relevant information to the higher-ranked person, but that the latter has no formal authority over the former beyond the information flow. The formal hierarchy and reporting structures are the simplest and most pervasive of the formal linking mechanisms, but they are also quite limited. In particular, individual managers can quickly become overloaded as the number and complexity of coordination issues requiring their attention rises. As a result, key decisions may be delayed. There is also the risk of message distortion when orders and information pass up and down the organizational hierarchy. People at the top may be shielded from critical or negative information, while people at the bottom are sometimes excluded from information that could help them in their work. Formal reporting structures alone are therefore often inadequate when conditions change rapidly. Special Groups Organizations can also use special groups to ensure that different parts of an organization are linked. Project teams are temporary groups of members assigned to work with one another on particular projects. Membership in these groups, while temporary, is often a full-time assignment; members return to their original groups only after the completion of the assigned project. These teams are common in many software companies, but also in industries like management consulting and advertising. A particular form of project teams, called “X teams,” emphasize outreach to stakeholders both inside and outside their companies.2 These networked teams are characterized by external activity, extensive ties, expandable structures, flexible membership and internal mechanisms for execution. Their entrepreneurial focus helps them respond more nimbly than traditional teams to the rapidly changing characteristics of work, technology and customer demands. The task force is another example of a temporary cross-unit group. Many task forces are composed of people whose primary tasks lie elsewhere; membership in these groups is a part-time assignment. Task forces bring together representatives from various groupings in the organizations; they are problem-focused, and exist until the particular problem is solved. For example, we see companies pull together task forces to analyze problems related to customer satisfaction and to make recommendations for improvement. Some organizations use permanent cross-unit groups to bring together representatives of different work groups, with a formal mandate to pool their expertise and coordinate the efforts of their respective groups with respect to particular products, clients, markets, or problems. Membership in these groups is a part-time assignment. For example, many companies bring together groups of middle managers for this purpose, in hopes of helping to create networks outside of departmental silos, especially as companies grow. Special Roles Organizations sometimes create or use special roles to help coordinate work. For example, when the coordination requirements involving two or more groups expand, the organization frequently assigns responsibility for coordinating across the groups to specific individuals, who serve as liaisons.3 These people serve as conduits for information and expertise, and as contacts and

## Page 6

advisors on the work involving their groups. Their roles are primarily information-focused; they rarely have the authority to impose decisions on others, especially those in other groups. One example might be the engineering liaison in a manufacturing plant, who is a member of the engineering organization but who is physically located in the plant to link the two units. The liaison role is often not a full-time responsibility; instead, it is combined with other activities. Another kind of role is an integrator. Some situations may require a more general management perspective and swifter resolution of problems or issues involving several units than is offered by liaison roles. In such cases, organizations may create positions for integrators.  The integrator has some “carrots and sticks” with which to reinforce the role (such as having a role in the performance evaluation of other actors involved, a budget line to provide resources, etc.)  Product managers, brand managers, and account managers are all examples of formal integrator roles. In each case, the task of the integrator is not to do the work but to coordinate the activities and the decision processes of members from various groups. Technology Systems Advances in digital technologies, the Internet, and social media have rapidly increased the linking and coordinating alternatives open to organizations, and they are profoundly influencing the way companies organize and manage work. For example, in many organizations, it is now commonplace for employees scattered across the world to spend significant amounts of their day collaborating with one another using video conferencing technologies.4 Decision-making and decision support systems also increasingly extend outside the company, creating links with customers and suppliers. And in some organizations, managers are using social media technologies to engage the entire organization in collective conversations that lend employees’ higher levels of voice than have been possible in the past.5 As is evident from all of these examples, linking mechanisms connect people and units separated by strategic grouping and coordinate their activities as needed to achieve the organization’s strategic goals. The flow of information is a critically important element of their role, and the challenge for managers is to construct linking mechanisms that are both effective and efficient. The easiest linking mechanisms to design and maintain are those whose coordination benefits are readily recognized by the units involved. But most grouping structures also entail “difficult linkages” that are strategically important for achieving the organization’s strategic goals but whose coordination benefits are not easily recognized by the units involved.6 Designing the linkages is in such cases only the first step; making them work involves alignment, the final element of organization design. Aligning: Control Mechanisms Alignment involves ensuring that individual and organizational goals are aligned with one another. One of the reasons for the failure of organizational design efforts is a lack of organizational congruence. This includes organizational patterns that pull individuals into behaviors that undermine the organization’s strategy or that pull different groups in opposing directions. Consider, for example, a company in which the manufacturing division is rewarded on the basis of gross profit margins while the sales division is rewarded on the basis of volume. No amount of linking mechanisms can prevent

## Page 7

the two groups from working at cross purposes: Sales will do everything possible to reduce the unit price in order to sell as much as possible, which will hurt Manufacturing’s margin. Manufacturing may then respond by cutting corners in order to cut costs, thereby diminishing the product’s quality or features and reducing its appeal to customers. What the organization requires in this case is an alignment system that better supports organizational goals. There are various mechanisms for aligning individual and organizational goals: direct compensation, indirect compensation, recruitment, selection, and onboarding, work design, and training, development, and advancement. Direct Compensation Direct compensation is comprised of base pay, variable pay, and equity-based compensation. Base pay is the regular wages or salary that an employee earns. For salaried or exempt employees (non-hourly workers such as managers), base pay is the individual’s annual salary. For non-exempt, employees (hourly workers), base pay is an hourly wage that varies depending on the number of hours that an employee works. An employee’s base pay typically grows over time with cost-of-living increases (raises linked to the rate of inflation), merit-based raises (raises linked to job performance), and tenure (raises linked to the number of years an employee has been at the firm). Variable pay is compensation that changes based on performance (in contrast to a fixed base salary, which is paid regardless of performance).  An annual bonus may be set according to units sold, sales revenue, customer service scores, number of clients, or any job-related criteria. For example, hourly employees who are paid by the piece (piece-rate system) receive variable pay. However, variable pay more commonly refers to a combination of pay types used to motivate employees to focus on key areas of the business. Variable pay should reward performance that is: 1) well-aligned with the firm’s strategy, 2) easy to measure, 3) clear to the employee, 4) controllable by the employee, and 5) guided by the principle of equity. Individual incentives can be a powerful tool for changing behavior, but they are difficult to design and implement. For example, incentives should clearly link pay to easily measurable objectives, because “you get what you pay for.” If a team’s objective is customer satisfaction, that should be the measure of performance, rather than volume or duration of service calls, which may bear little relation to whether the customer’s needs were actually met. However, when a job requires multiple tasks, some are easier to measure than others, and when performance measures are incomplete, they drive behavior that is not perfectly aligned with organizational goals.7 Equity-based compensation is non-cash pay that represents ownership in the firm. This type of compensation can take many forms including stock options, restricted stock options, and performance shares. Equity-based compensation allows the employees of the firm to share in the profits via appreciation and can encourage retention, particularly if there are vesting requirements (employees cannot access options for a period of time). Equity-based compensation is often used by start-up companies that may lack the cash or want to invest cash flow into growth initiatives, yet attract high quality employees.

Indirect Compensation Indirect compensation is comprised of required benefits and discretionary benefits. In the U.S., required benefits include social security (including unemployment insurance, pension benefits, and Medicare health insurance), worker’s compensation (a mandatory insurance program for those injured on the job), family and medical leave (leave time for medical or family emergencies), and health benefits (firms of 50 or more employees must offer health insurance to employees or pay a tax penalty). Discretionary benefits include paid time off (vacation, sick time, personal leave), education benefits (opportunities for workers to pursue further education while they work), retirement plans (plans to pay workers after they retire), transportation (subsidized cost of commuting on public transportation or private bus routes), childcare or eldercare, opportunity for social impact, or other perks. Recruitment, Selection, and Onboarding Although direct and indirect forms of compensation are the two aspects of alignment that most managers recognize most readily, there are others that are equally important. Recruitment practices that provide applicants with a comprehensive picture of the organization, such as realistic job previews and referrals by current employees, help to align individual and organizational goals and reduce the likelihood of subsequent turnover. Selection methods that assess applicant fit with the job and organization, as well as use weighted selection criteria, also enable the hiring of individuals more likely to act in concert with organizational goals. Onboarding practices that provide new employees with connections to experienced organizational insiders as role models, mentors, or trainers, give them positive feedback as they adapt, and provide them with clear information also help to align individual and organizational goals and reduce the likelihood of turnover in the critical first year after organizational entry. Work Design

Designing work to foster employee engagement can also be an effective alignment mechanism. Scholars have highlighted seven key work design factors that facilitate employee engagement: 1) setting clear goals (allowing employees to know where their work is heading and why it matters); 2) providing autonomy (allowing employees to determine what to do and how to do it); 3) providing resources (giving employees access to necessary equipment, funding, materials, data, and personnel); 4) providing low to moderate time pressure, 5) getting employees the right sort of help, from the right people, at the right time; 6) providing a climate of psychological safety by conveying that employees will be commended rather than punished for making suggestions and pointing out mistakes; and 7) listening to employees, and allowing time for debate.8 Training, Development, and Advancement

Training, development, and advancement opportunities can also serve as alignment mechanisms. Organizations vary in how much they facilitate upward mobility and promotions. For organizations that consistently promote from within, entry-level hires may be willing to take initially

## Page 9

lower pay in return for the chance of early promotion to a better position. By offering training opportunities in areas related to key initiatives, organizations can help employees gain the competencies needed to reach organizational goals. Organizational redesign often requires providing employees with skills and expertise in new ways of doing things. For example, moving from a functional to a divisional structure often works best if the organization provides training in general management skills to high-potential functional managers. IMPORTANT ISSUES RELATED TO THE STRUCTURAL DESIGN PERSPECTIVE In this section, we address two important issues related to the structural design perspective: deliberately changing an organization’s structure and the future of structural design. Deliberately Changing an Organization’s Structure Organizational design changes are becoming a normal, on-going process in many enterprises. In fact, the ability to adapt organizational structure—to respond quickly and effectively to new conditions in the business environment and to a changing organizational strategy—may be among the most valuable organizational capabilities in today’s competitive environment. Stimuli for Organizational Redesign No matter how successful a company may be at any point in time, there are powerful forces at work, both within an organization and in its external environment, that make redesign inevitable. Sometimes redesign is a response to the growth, evolution, and maturation of an organization and of its products. As organizations grow, for example, they become more complex, and simply multiplying existing units does not suffice to cope with the broader range of customers, employees, and products. At other times, redesign is necessary because of internal problems, such as lack of coordination, excessive conflict, unclear roles, poor work flows, or a proliferation of ad hoc organizational units such as task forces, committees, and special project teams. However, the most frequent stimulus to organizational design change is that the current design no longer fits the pressures from the external business environment. The organization is, in this perspective, seen as a “throughput” system that takes inputs from the environment, adds value to them through various internal processes, and then distributes them to users outside the organization; accordingly, the environment is primarily seen as a source of inputs and a market for outputs. Shifts in the firm’s environment can make the established design inadequate. This might happen when a new competitor enters the space, an emerging technology disrupts the industry, or a new regulation is introduced. As the environment changes, the organization must adjust its design to “fit” new environmental pressures. To some extent, managers are making organizational design decisions all the time. Every time a specific job is designed, a procedure created, a process altered, or a task moved, the organization design is being changed. And yet we know that the very process of redesign, especially major redesign, involves inherent and significant costs.

Costs of Organizational Redesign One key cost associated with organizational redesign is stress and anxiety for employees. Frequent waves of restructuring can lead to enormous anxiety, a loss of continuity, the departure of key people, and the damaging of the core competencies that made the company successful. People may worry about losing their jobs, friends, status, or day-to-day routines. Anxiety often leads to a number of predictable reactions ranging from panic or withdrawal to outright resistance. Another cost of organizational redesign is the disruption of the normal flow of business. While organizational redesign may be deliberately aimed at breaking established patterns and habits, redesign efforts occupy the time and attention of managers, and tie up organizational resources. People throughout the organization tend to focus on the implications of the redesign for themselves and their part of the organization, sometimes at the cost of their immediate tasks. For example, established communications patterns are often severed, either temporarily or permanently, during redesign efforts, leaving customers and suppliers with no idea of whom to talk to in the company. A related key cost of organizational redesign is the disruption of informal systems and processes. No formal blueprint for organizational design can ever capture fully the processes and interactions that make the organization work. These emerge over time, as people adapt to the organization and to the demands of their jobs and their environments. Sometimes an organizational redesign is less effective initially than its designers expect, or it initially seems to work but then runs into unexpected snags and glitches. Often this is because the redesign has driven apart the formal and informal organization.9 Minimizing the Costs of Organizational Redesign A careful process of organization design is one way for leaders to develop the organizational capability for proactive change or rapid response, and can minimize the costs of change, although it can never eliminate them entirely. For example, managers, when designing changes to an organization, should keep likelihood of implementation in mind. And as with any kind of change, the process is more likely to be successful if the people responsible for making it work feel that they were part of shaping the change. This can be structured through the creation of various arenas in which people can form new networks, discuss problems, and form new patterns. Sometimes the worst problems of misalignment of the previous structure and the new design can be anticipated, and steps taken to counteract them: for example, by providing new training, rotating personnel into different positions, or providing extra linking mechanisms early in the adjustment process. The Future of Structural Design In the traditional, hierarchical organizational structures mentioned above, those at the top of the organization have the ability to set or change organizational strategy in a top-down manner. However, we are witnessing the rise of newer ways of organizing where strategic action emerges from participative, dynamic and ongoing processes happening lower down in the organization—or even outside of the organization. These newer ways of organizing are becoming more commonplace

## Page 11

for several reasons: technological advances; the growth in knowledge-based work; and a trend towards viewing work and organizations as places for creating personal meaning.10 This section describes some of the more common new forms of organizing. Less-Hierarchical Organizations In response to the drawbacks of more hierarchical ways of organizing (e.g. slower decisionmaking), many organizations are experimenting with new forms of management that put employees at the center. One such type of organizing is a “conversational firm,” which leverages the tools of social media to promote far more employee voice than in prior forms of organizing.11 Yet, while “conversational firms” subvert the traditional communication hierarchy, they do not necessarily have to subvert the traditional decision making hierarchy; there is an important distinction between voice rights and decision rights, and different organizational models can be identified by whether they delegate or hierarchically structure those different types of rights.12 In a conversational firm, for example, employees are given voice rights but not always decision rights; this form of organizing attacks the bureaucratic firm’s structure of communication while leaving the structure of authority largely intact.13 In other less-hierarchical forms of organizing, by contrast, top managers may attempt to give both voice rights and decision rights to employees. In such self-managing organizations, the hierarchical reporting relationship between managers and subordinates is eliminated, and authority is decentralized across the organization. A popular example is holocracy, a method of decentralized management and organizational governance in which authority and decision-making are distributed to a series of self-organizing teams rather than being controlled by managers at the top. In a holocracy, decision-making power is with fluid teams, or “circles,” and with roles rather than individuals. Zappos is a well-known example of a company that adopted holocracy. Here, circles meet to define and revise roles in regular “governance meetings.” In these meetings, any member of a circle can propose changes to the way the circle is organized, such as shifting a role’s accountabilities or proposing a new group policy. All proposed changes are discussed and consented to by the group. As a result of this revision process, formal roles frequently evolve as new issues arise.14 While holocracy is perhaps the best-known of the self-managing organizations, there are other forms of organizing that involve radical decentralization.15 For example, Valve, a computer game maker, allowed employees full flexibility and autonomy to choose the games on which they want to work. Rather than managers determining which games should be developed, employees’ decisions about what they wanted to work on dictated what games were made. At Morning Star, a tomato processing company, instead of managers directing the work, employees voluntarily entered into contracts with one another. These contracts (called “colleague letters of understanding”) outlined individual responsibilities, activities, goals and metrics for evaluating performance. They were renegotiated at least once a year and formed the basis for how work was coordinated. While examples like Zappos, Valve and Morning Star are recent, some organizations—such as W.L. Gore, Oticon and Johnsonville Sausage—have used employee-centric management systems for many years. The intended benefits of less hierarchical ways of organizing include faster decision-making, increased agility and adaptability in an increasingly fast-changing economy, and more empowered, motivated employees. However, no way of organizing is without its drawbacks. One of the challenges

## Page 12

of self-managing organizations is that they may increase sometimes corrosive peer pressure. Some research suggests that pressure from peers can actually be more stressful for employees than that from bosses.16 A second challenge is dealing with the ambiguity and constant state of flux that selfmanaged organizations are often in, as these can lead to inefficiencies. For example, exit interviews of a subset of Zappos employees indicated that “ambiguity and lack of clarity around progression, compensation and responsibilities” and getting “no definitive answers” to what they felt were basic organizational questions were what led them to leave the company.17 The social media company Medium had been using holocracy, but dropped it, with a senior manager noting “it was difficult to coordinate efforts at scale.”18 Crowd-Centric Ways of Organizing Increasingly, complex forms of work are taking place outside of the boundaries of conventional organizations entirely. We can see this with crowd-centric forms of organizing, where individuals outside the boundaries of a firm come together to accomplish work. One example of this form of organizing is online communities. Wikipedia, for example, has mobilized millions of individuals to produce an enormous, high quality, and constantly updated encyclopedia. Contributors are volunteers scattered around the world. We see similar feats of complex knowledge production happening in other communities, such as Linux. In online organizations, the division of labor is fluid, and integration mechanisms are community-based, with people bound together by a shared sense of purpose, and linked using emerging technologies as opposed to hierarchical relations.19 However, the fluidity with which online communities operate does not mean that these communities lack organization. For example, while online communities do not involve manager-subordinate relationships, coordination mechanisms such as peer code reviews keep work organized. Scholars have also shown how these communities still have status hierarchies, though they tend to be based on who has demonstrated knowledge and commitment, versus one’s rank.20 Another example of crowd-centric forms of organizing comes from companies like Uber, TaskRabbit, and Caviar, local gig platforms that mediate on-site, in-person services for a set price. Here, the platform is a technological application that connects customers and service providers in a “two-sided marketplace.” Employers fund the platform, govern its usage, and take a fee from each transaction. They often direct and control the behavior of workers and are powerful actors in the employment relation, yet they define themselves as neutral intermediaries not employers and thus bypass employment regulations—precisely because they have embedded so much of what we think of as the employment relation in technology instead of human managers.21 Crowd-centric organizing can also be seen in flash organizations, where crowds are structured like organizations to achieve complex and open-ended goals.22 In a flash organizations, the crowd’s division of labor is encoded into de-individualized roles, similar to how roles work in movie crews or disaster response teams, where roles help coordinate work between individuals who have not worked together before. The organization’s structure is then constantly reconfigured, enabling continuous adaptation of the work and division of labor. Flash organizations can be convened to do complex work such as product design, software development, and game production.

Hybrid Models of Organizing Increasingly, we are seeing ways of organizing work that involve a mix of actors outside firm boundaries as well as employees inside. For example, many once-“closed” organizations are “opening” their boundaries, using crowdsourcing for innovation and for accomplishing tasks. One way that companies are engaging the crowd in innovation is by creating contests, in which the focal organization identifies a specific problem, offers a cash prize, and announces an invitation to submit solutions. Online platforms such as Innocentive, TopCoder, and Kaggle provide crowd-contest services for brick-and-mortar organizations.23 Companies use contests for innovation when it is not obvious what technical approach will lead to the best solution for a problem. Another way that organizations are using the crowd is by using online freelancing platforms. Unlike gig platforms like Uber and TaskRabbit, online freelancing platforms enable hiring managers within firms to source work directly from freelancers on an on-demand basis, at a speed and scale that was unimaginable until recently.24 Microwork platforms like Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) and Crowdflower focus on the speed and low managerial overhead of the matching process, and are best suited for relatively simple, repetitive tasks. Online freelancing platforms such as Upwork and Freelancer focus on more specialized and knowledge intensive projects in categories such as software development, creative and design, and writing and translation. In a study of nine Fortune 500 firms and MNCs that source work through online freelancing platforms, researchers found that companies use platform sourcing for numerical flexibility (the ability to rapidly adjust workforce size or number of hours worked, in response to changes in product demand), functional flexibility (the ability to gain access to workers with new skills and expertise), and financial flexibility (the ability to adjust human resources costs in response to changes in the external price of labor).25 However, they also found that platform sourcing brings with it a set of challenges for employers, such as the need to align internal work with external work, minimize increased coordination costs, and overcome internal resistance. The Fortune 500 companies in their study that used platform sourcing successfully facilitated the adoption process by introducing a program management layer, creating space for experimentation, allocating sufficient financial resources, and involving top managers and stakeholders early on. Once platform sourcing had been adopted, managers facilitated work with external freelancers by having a strategy to decide what work to source online, creating a freelancer vetting program, creating a bench of high-value experts, fostering a sense of community with freelancers, and not hiring to replace in-house employees, but to complement them. While researchers are only beginning to understand the complexities of such ways of organizing, critical voices have begun to raise questions about how crowd-centric and hybrid modes of organizing may create undesirable social conditions including precarious jobs, insufficient incomes, powerful and unregulated corporations, and worsening inequality.26 A growing number of global workers are now doing gig work online—accepting project-driven tasks, with companies that assign, schedule, route, and bill work through websites or mobile apps. Researchers highlight a number of practices that employers and our wider society could use to improve working conditions for these workers. One book details how employers and society members could make important changes at the employer level, the ecosystem level, and the public policy level.27 At the employer

## Page 14

level, a good work code could push companies to make sure that their vendors provide good work conditions and practices to their workers. Companies could pledge, at a minimum to work with vendors who can verify that they provide a base pay for all work completed; pay workers within one week of completing the task; charge them no additional transfer fees to collect their paychecks; and have the same non-discrimination policies in place as the hiring company, to ensure that all workers producing value for a company are using fair business practices. At the ecosystem level, a third party registry could allow on-demand workers to build their work resumes and accrue reputations independent of the platforms. Such a registry, managed by workers’ representatives could allow workers to display validated feedback from previous employers and requestors. Platforms could be required to post this portable stack of recommendation letters as part of the worker’s platformspecific profile. At the level of public policy, some have advocated for universal healthcare, paid family leave, municipal working space, and continuing education. ANALYZING ORGANIZATIONS USING A STRUCTURAL DESIGN PERSPECTIVE When analyzing an organization from a structural design perspective, it is important to ask a series of questions:

-  What is the organization’s strategy, and how does any planned new initiative relate to it?
-  What is the formal grouping structure? Does it impede or facilitate the planned initiative?
-  How does linking currently occur? Will the initiative require new linking mechanisms?
-  What are the key elements of the current alignment system (direct compensation, indirect compensation, recruitment, selection, and onboarding, work design, and training and development)? Will the initiative require new alignment mechanisms?

In conclusion, a structural design perspective leads us to see organizations as fundamentally rational, in the sense that in a well-designed organization, each person and each unit in the organization can and should be oriented to accomplishing organizational goals. The major impediments to realizing this situation are that people do not adequately understand the goals or tasks (inadequate information), that they do not have the resources necessary to accomplish their assigned tasks, or that an inadequate organizational design is directing their efforts to individual or subunit goals that get in the way of the overall goals. While the look and feel of organizations will continue to change over time, especially in light of emerging technologies, grouping, linking, and aligning are key processes for ensuring that the organization builds an efficient and effective foundation for realizing its strategic goals.
