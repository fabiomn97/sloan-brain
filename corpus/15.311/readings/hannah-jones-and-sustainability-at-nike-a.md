HANNAH JONES AND SUSTAINABILITY AT NIKE (A)1

When Hannah Jones stepped into her new role as a Director in the Corporate Responsibility Group at Nike in 1998, Nike was under fire from activists who were calling for a global boycott. Recent articles had exposed sweatshop conditions in factories in Indonesia and other Asian countries that supplied Nike merchandise.

Nike had initially denied responsibility for the working conditions of its subcontractor factories saying, “We’re not here to eliminate poverty and famine or lead the war against violence and crime. Our critics say that the world is going to hell in a Nike sports bag. Then again, our critics, for the most part, aren’t athletes.”2

But Nike later reversed its position. The same year CEO Phil Knight hired Jones, he took to the public stage saying, “The Nike product has become synonymous with slave wages, forced overtime, and arbitrary abuse.”3

Jones had a background in activism and media. She had started her career at the BBC, encouraging BBC audiences to take action on social issues. After the BBC, she worked for an NGO on social-action campaigns addressing racism and AIDS/HIV. She moved into the private sector when she took a job consulting for Microsoft on community investments. She then joined Nike a few years later.4

“I always knew that I wanted to be an agent of social change and justice," Jones reflected, “and this broadened out to sustainability later on. It never occurred to me that I would move into the private sector. The epiphany moment came when I was working at the NGO on an AIDS campaign and rather naïvely sent out a mass letter to corporations looking for funding. One company, which I will not name, sent back a letter response basically saying thank-youvery-much and go away because ‘AIDS is not an issue to our consumers.’ This made me start wondering what you could do if you were in a corporation with the reach and potential influence that these companies have across the world.”5

Under Jones’s leadership, Nike would transition from taking a defensive position in response to public scrutiny about sustainability and labor conditions to becoming one of the leading companies in sustainable design and operations.

Nike’s Labor Crisis

Nike’s strategy of pursuing global sourcing opportunities to produce lower-cost products was financially rewarding, but it had created serious public relations problems for the company just before Jones joined the firm. Beginning in the early 1990s, activists engaged in public protests over working conditions and labor rights issues in Nike's overseas factories. Activists alleged that workers were systematically subjected to conditions including unjust and illegal pay practices, forced overtime, verbal and physical abuse, sexual harassment, unfair barriers to unionization, and excessive toxic chemical exposure.7 Rob Harrison, editor of Ethical Consumer, suggested that Nike was targeted by campaigners because it was the world’s best-selling brand and because it had initially denied responsibility for any malpractice in its subcontractor factories.8

Nike Code of Conduct and M-Audit

Even before Jones arrived in 1998, Nike leaders had realized that they needed a new strategy to deflect the growing criticism and improve their suppliers’ performance.9 In 1992, Nike had developed a “Code of Conduct” specifying standards for working conditions, wages, hours, and health and safety for all factories.

But Nike had little insight into what was actually happening inside the overseas factories that manufactured its products. Nike leadership initiated a basic audit to assess whether factories were adhering to its Code of Conduct. The audit highlighted problems, but it did not provide Nike with enough granular understanding of the root causes of the problems to equip the company to address them. As a result, Nike realized that it needed to develop a more multilayered monitoring approach.

Following audit visits, Nike and contract factories created action plans to bring noncompliant sites up to the standards laid out by local law and Nike’s Code of Conduct.

Noncompliant Factories

Nike found that persistently noncompliant factories typically fell into two categories: “flat line,” describing factories that failed to make material changes to their practices even after visits or monitoring, and “yo-yo,” describing factories that responded to audit feedback only while scrutinized and then returned to pre-audit practices. In both cases, factories remained relatively close to their pre-audit performance.11

In order to better understand and address flat-line and yo-yo factory challenges, Nike added another layer of monitoring—one that was closer to the problems that were happening inside the factories. Nike assigned field managers to the various regions to look at day-to-day compliance with labor laws and the Nike Code of Conduct.  The results of these audits by field managers were overwhelming, uncovering a plethora of issues including forced overtime, child labor, improper safety precautions, and significant environmental impact from hazardous materials.

Charting a Path Forward

Jones and her team had to figure out what Nike should do to address these issues. So far, their approach of monitoring and installing field managers on the ground had not yielded the results Nike had hoped—in terms of improving conditions in the factories and avoiding negative media attention. The company was continually engaged in “firefighting,” as Jones explained.12

One option was to double down on the investments Nike had already made in monitoring systems. Nike could be more consistent about ending relationships with repeatedly noncompliant factories, or create a more granular list of specifications to which factories needed to adhere to win their first (or repeat) contracts. Accountability could definitely be increased.

Yet Jones and her team wondered if they really understood the root causes of the noncompliance. For example, were factory managers trying to change but were unable to do so? Or did they really not care to follow the rules Nike was trying to set, perhaps because they saw their current operations as the best strategy for making a profit?

These questions led Jones to wonder about a different approach. Could Nike turn to NGOs to learn more about local conditions and capacities? And would NGOs (some of whom were the company’s “harshest critics,” as Jones put it)13 even be interested in helping Nike?

Jones’ team had other issues to consider as well. Nike, like many companies, had started producing an annual corporate responsibility report in the early 2000s. After a few years, Jones and her team invited a panel of external experts to review a draft report and share feedback on what goals and strategies the company might pursue next. The panel urged Nike to be even more transparent if they truly wanted approval from the media, NGOs, and activist groups. Jones wondered if Nike might publish the names, addresses and locations of all the Nike factories worldwide. That level of disclosure seemed risky and even counterproductive because keeping a company’s supply chain secret was understood as a way to protect competitive advantage.14 On the other hand, a move like this might help Nike pave the way to being seen as a leader in the sustainability space.

Jones and her team wanted to devise a plan to make Nike a more sustainable business, but given the complexity of Nike’s global web of relationships, this was a daunting task. At the same time, Jones knew she had to start somewhere.
