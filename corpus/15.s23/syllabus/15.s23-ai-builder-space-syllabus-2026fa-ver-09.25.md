COURSE DESCRIPTION

AI Builder Space is a hands-on, interactive MBA proseminar that gives you dedicated time, space, and support to develop practical AI fluency — and a portfolio of real work that demonstrates it. Designed as Sea complementary extension of the AI Foundations series, the course helps you understand and stay ahead of the current AI landscape by learning, building, and sharing.

Most class time is devoted to hands-on projects that you design and build, producing a personal portfolio of applied AI work to showcase to prospective employers. Along the way, you will collaborate with peers, industry practitioners, and faculty — learning from diverse perspectives, sharing insights on specific projects and AI tools, and producing more impactful AI innovation together.

Who this course is for Sloan students who:

- Want to understand, create, and lead AI initiatives in the context of modern management;

- Are excited to jump in and start building with AI — no technical background required;

- Have class assignments, personal projects, or business opportunities they want to tackle with AI; and

- Are curious about what is possible with today's frontier AI tools and want to help define what comes next.

Course philosophy

- Learning by building. The fastest path to AI fluency is creating something real — from reports and presentations to vibe coded prototypes to fully deployed agentic workflows.

- Your context is a superpower. Your expertise across professional and personal domains makes your AI applications more valuable, not less.

- Collaboration accelerates everything. Learning, building, sharing, and iterating together form a virtuous cycle across Sloan’s purpose-driven community.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

Strategic & conceptual

- Articulate the current landscape of AI capabilities, limitations, and business applications;

- Evaluate AI opportunities and risks within an organizational, industry, or product context; and

- Develop the case for an AI-powered product, initiative, internal tool, and/or personal workflow — the user, the problem it solves, and its value.

Technical & applied

- Apply prompt engineering, context engineering, vibe coding, and agentic workflow design to realworld problems;

- Use frontier AI tools and APIs (e.g., Claude Code, Cursor, Vercel, Supabase, StackAI, Lovable) to prototype functional solutions; and

- Evaluate and iterate on AI outputs with a product and user lens.

Communication & community

- Explain not just what you built but why and how — the problem, the user, the pipeline, the key decisions and tradeoffs, and the limitations — to both technical and non-technical audiences;

- Foster community across Sloan's AI and tech ecosystem, in the classroom (classes, Action Learning, SIP) and beyond it (clubs, hackathons, and more); and

- Share what you learn with other and future Sloan and MIT students, alumni, faculty, and staff — contributing your expertise and insight in real time across our community.

In practice, the three pillars run on a small set of interlocking structures: Squads — At the start of each sprint, you will be assigned to a squad of three or four projects. Your squad is your home base — your primary source of accountability, feedback, and community. Project teams within a squad may be no larger than three students, and at least one project per student must be completed individually over the duration of the semester, so that everyone builds direct, hands-on experience with the tools.

Sprints — Building is organized into timed sprints — two over the fall semester, each about three weeks of building with dedicated reflection at the end. Every sprint opens with a short planning document and closes with a check-in and share-out day.

Planning documents — Before each sprint, you will submit a brief, structured Product Requirements Document (PRD): what you will build, who it is for (a market, a team, or just yourself), and what “done” looks like. Whoever your user is, the same product discipline applies. Plans are short by design, and the teaching team & Alumni mentors review — helping you scope a project that is ambitious but achievable within the sprint. PRDs can be made with the assistance of Claude (PRD prompt provided).

Seeded projects — Not everyone arrives with an idea, and some builders want a project with real stakes. The course maintains a small, curated menu of project briefs sourced from Sloan courses and staff — for example, a simulation for teaching intercultural communication, or a live dashboard for a Sloan office. You can pick one up, adapt it, or set it aside and build your own. Seeded projects come with a real user, which makes them strong material for the course's product discipline — and for your build interview.

Building time — Sprints are largely self-directed: self-organized squad meetings, plus optional studentled and AI Club workshops on specific tools, offered on demand.

Check-ins & share-outs — Each session opens with a brief squad check-in — a couple of minutes per project on progress and blockers — to keep momentum and surface stalls early. At the close of each sprint, check-ins go deeper: you will show real progress and get feedback from your squad before a squad representative brings two or three “things everyone should see” to the full class, so the best builds and tools reach everyone.

Ideation sessions — Between sprints, a faculty-led session combines a short read on what is new and worth your attention, a guest practitioner, and structured ideation that feeds directly into your next planning document.

Paired build interviews — Near the end of the semester, you will pair up and interview each other about a tool you built — a short, recorded, podcast-style conversation of about 10–15 minutes per person, with each of you screen-sharing and walking through your work while the other asks about it: who it is for, why you built it this way, what the pipeline was, what broke, and what you would change. Because the raw material already exists in your sprint work and the demo happens live in conversation, preparation is light — no separate pitch deck or demo video is required.

REQUIREMENTS & GRADING

The course is graded P/D/F, and assessment is completion-based rather than quality-graded — deliberately, so that you can take real creative risks. To pass, you will:

- Submit a brief planning document at the start of each sprint [20% each, 40% total];

- Attend class sessions and participate in your squad's check-ins throughout each sprint [30%];

- Complete both Sprints building at least one individual/solo project and at least two total projects during the semester, ensuring direct, hands-on experience building with AI tools [5% each, 10% total]

- Record a paired build interview — on your own project and a partner's [15%];

- Ship a final portfolio web-doc to the class gallery [5%]; and

- Vote on class project superlatives [contributes to participation score].

Peer feedback from your squadmates is formative throughout the semester and is never used for grading.

VIBE CODING WORKSHOP

The teaching team will lead a series of vibe coding workshops designed to help students be prepared to meet the increasing need for MBA graduates to be fluent in AI with no technical background required. Through structured, recruiting-relevant, tangible projects, students will gain AI skills through practical workshop exercises such as building a personal AI toolkit and website, building with no-code AI platforms, and building a full-stack AI platform. Specific exercises will be shared at the beginning of the course based on the latest advice of the AI & ML club leadership and the teaching team.

THE FINAL SESSION & GALLERY

The semester culminates in the launch of the class gallery: a published page linking every student's portfolio. The gallery goes live before the final class and keeps working for you long after the semester ends — it is shareable by faculty, students, and Sloan's social channels (with student permission), and built to be put in front of employers. Because each linked portfolio embeds your build interview, the gallery carries both the work and the story behind it.

Once the gallery is live, you will vote on class superlatives — most useful, boldest, best demo — which are awarded during the final session.

COURSE POLICIES

Attendance & participation

This is an in-person, workshop-style course: most of its value comes from building alongside your squad, and the weekly check-in is the course's core accountability mechanism. You are expected to attend each session and participate in your squad's check-ins. If you must miss a session, notify the teaching team and your squad in advance and arrange to share your check-in update asynchronously. Because passing the course is completion-based, repeated unexcused absences will put your grade at risk.

Use of AI tools

Generative AI is not merely permitted in this course — it is the subject of it. You are expected to use frontier AI tools extensively in your projects, and to develop informed judgment about when and how to use them well. Three ground rules apply:

- Disclose. Your portfolio and build interview should make transparent which tools you used and how — the pipeline, the prompts and design decisions that mattered, and what the tools did versus what you did.

- Protect data. Do not enter confidential, proprietary, or personal data into AI tools without authorization. This includes material covered by an employer's confidentiality obligations and any data governed by MIT's information-protection policies. When in doubt, ask before you upload.

- Mind the boundaries. The norms of this course do not transfer automatically to the rest of your program. Every MIT course sets its own policy on AI use; follow the policy of the course you are in.

You are also responsible for complying with the terms of service and licensing requirements of the tools and models you use.

Academic integrity

MIT's academic integrity policies apply in full. The work you present as your own — your projects, your portfolio, and your build interview — must reflect your own building and thinking, with collaborators, sources, and tools credited honestly. When you adapt open-source code, a template, or a seeded project brief, say so. Misrepresenting someone else's work as your own build is a violation. For guidance, see integrity.mit.edu.

Accommodations

MIT is committed to the principle of equal access, and we want every student to be able to participate fully in this course. If you have a disability and need accommodations, please contact MIT Disability and Access Services (DAS) at das-student@mit.edu. If you already have an accommodation letter, share it with the teaching team early in the term so that we can put your accommodations in place from the start.

Questions & communication

Course announcements, planning-document templates, and schedule updates live on the course Canvas site 15.S23 SSIM: AI Builder Space Proseminar. For anything else, catch the teaching team after class, come to office hours or schedule a time by appointment, or email the teaching team; we aim to respond within two business days.
