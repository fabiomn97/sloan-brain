## Page 5

Decision-Making Under Uncertainty: Sequential Decisions

- Most real-world decisions are not one-off events, but part of an ongoing sequence of choices. o We need to balance short-term with long-term goals and decide strategically, not

myopically. o Decisions that we make today critically affect what types of decisions we can make

in the future (e.g., R&D, opening new store, airline routing…etc.)

- Decision-Trees are a technique for visualizing and optimizing such decisions. o Visualization. The tree clearly lay out all the possible decisions and uncertain

outcomes o Optimization. The tree can be analyzed to determine an optimal policy: it tells you

the best decision in any situation you encounter

## Page 14

Why is this challenging?

- You must commit to your decisions without knowing the future.

- You might make two types of mistakes: o You stopped on a $5 or $10, but could have gotten more if you continued o You continued on a $5 or $10 hoping to get more, but you ended up with

less because nothing better came along

- We can use decision trees to help us calculate the optimal strategy.

## Page 38

Observations about this optimal strategy

- At the beginning of the game, we will only settle for a $20.
- Starting at cup 4, we will also settle for a $10.
- Starting at cup 9, we will also settle for a $5.

- Our strategy highlights the inherent trade-off in this problem. o The more time we have left, the higher chance we can get that $20. So we’re

willing to wait for a higher payoff. o If we don’t have much time left, we’ll settle for whatever we can get like a

$5.

## Page 42

Two possible interpretations

- We need to make a single decision o Do R&D and launch the drug o Don’t do R&D and use old drug

If we do R&D, we will always launch the new drug

- We can make two sequential decisions o First, we decide if we want to do R&D or not. o If R&D was chosen, we first observe the efficacy of the drug, then decide

whether to launch the drug

If we do R&D, we may still go with the old drug

## Page 89

When is the Expected Payoff a good objective?

- If the financial consequences are small relative to the scale of the business, then expected payoff is a reasonable (and common) criterion since you have the “financial cushion” to absorb the losses and achieve the expected payoff over many investment decisions.

- However, if a single “bad” outcome can lead to ruin, you will want to consider the entire distribution and perhaps consider what decisions will minimize the probability of ruin or maximize the probability of achieving a minimum payoff. o Think of the asymmetries in risk tolerance between a large Pharma and a small

biotech

## Page 93

“Thinking in distributions” is the key to dealing with uncertainty

- The best we can do is work with a distribution for the uncertainty, either through historical data or modeling assumptions.

o Coop Paper demand → Average is 35, Stdev is 10. Stocks out 9% of the time o Guests who show up → Average is 75% with everyone showing up 2% of the time o Sanjay’s monthly labor costs → Uniform distribution between $5040 and $6860 o What will be under cup 2 → 50% $1, 30% $5, 15% $10, 5% $20. o Whether R&D will be successful → 70% successful, 30% unsuccessful

## Page 94

When data is available, use it. When data is unavailable, collect it.

- Historical data tells us what outcomes are likely vs. unlikely – sometimes that’s enough to make an informed decision (Lecture 1)

- But often-times, the right data doesn’t exist, especially for new decisions that depart from the status quo.

- In that case, try to collect the data through experimentation. (Lecture 2) o Use confidence intervals to ensure that your conclusions are based on strong

evidence supported by the Central Limit Theorem.

## Page 95

When you can’t collect data either, build a model

- Some decisions can’t be tested – experiments are too slow, costly, illegal…etc.

- Using domain knowledge and basic research, create a probabilistic model that maps uncertain inputs to uncertain outputs (Lecture 3).

- Running a Monte-Carlo simulation across tens of thousands of trials (scenarios) essentially generates synthetic data on possible outcomes.

- All models are wrong, so do sensitivity analysis on your key assumptions

## Page 96

Real decisions aren’t one-shot, they’re sequential

- In the real world, you often need to make a sequence of decisions, with some random outcomes revealed in between (Lecture 4).

- Decision trees lay out all the decision nodes, chance nodes, and outcome nodes in a format that is easy to communicate with other stakeholders.

- Solving the decision tree involving finding the path through the tree with the highest expected payoff.

- Sensitivity analysis ensures that the decisions are robust to changes in the problem parameters.
