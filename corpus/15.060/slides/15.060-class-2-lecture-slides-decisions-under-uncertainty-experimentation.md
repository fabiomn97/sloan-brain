## Page 8

When we need to go beyond historical data, we really only have 2 options:

1. Run an experiment – just collect the “right” data by trying out your decision for a small proportion of the population (Today’s Class)

2. Build a model – make informed assumptions on how the underlying data is generated. Use your existing data to “flesh out” an appropriate model (Wednesday’s Class)

Experimentation > Modeling

When experimentation is possible, it is usually preferable over building a model.  Why make assumptions that could be wrong when you can just collect the “right” data?

Refresher: Normal Distribution Properties

When data follows a normal distribution (i.e., a bell curve):

- It is symmetric with a single peak
- 68% of the data is within 1 standard deviation from the mean
- 95% of the data is within 2 standard deviations from the mean
- 99.7% of the data is within 3 standard deviations from the mean

## Page 70

Deciding On The Sample Size

How many visitors do you need in each group to arrive at a reliable result?

- The right answer depends on one factor that you don’t control … o The variance of the underlying metric (e.g., revenue per visitor)

- … and three factors that you do control

o The minimum increase in the metric that you want the test to detect

o The probability of detecting this increase if it exists (= called the power of the test). Higher is better.

o The probability of seeing a large effect just by chance even though there’s no difference between A

and B (= called significance). Lower is better.

## Page 78

Real-World Best Practices

-  Agree on a single decisive metric before launching the experiment, to determine if the experiment succeeds/fails

-  Multiple metrics are unlikely to all point in the same direction
- When this happens, it can be difficult to achieve consensus and move forward
- Also, without proper care this increases the chance of finding results that appear significant just by chance
- BUT, if key metrics move significantly in opposite directions, be careful

- Start with an A/A test
- Biases and errors tend to creep into experiment setups
- To ensure the test has been set up correctly, offer the same Control treatment to both groups for a brief period
- If the comparison metric is approximately equal for both groups, move forward with the A/B test; otherwise, “debug” the setup
- Another benefit of an A/A test: you can use the data to calculate variance/standard-deviation of the comparison metric to use in determining the sample size for the A/B test.

## Page 79

Real-World Best Practices

- Ramp gently
- Try the A/B with a small B first to make sure “nothing blows up” (particularly important for customer-facing experiments)
- If everything is OK, increase the size of the B group to the planned sample size

-  If the Test group performs better than the Control:

-  Try to triangulate test results with “outside” data to gain confidence
- Be very skeptical of big improvements (remember Twyman’s Law) and repeat the test immediately to see if the results hold up
- Periodically re-run the experiment. Novelty effects can be strong in certain cases - almost any change will have a positive, short-term impact but may not sustain over time.

- Analyze only the subjects that are actually exposed to the Control or one of the treatments; filter the rest out.

- If you are testing a change to the checkout page of your ecommerce site, only look at the users who made it to that page

## Page 80

A/B tests the gold standard for determining which managerial interventions actually work

- Due to randomization, group A users and group B users are identical in every way except which agent they are exposed to.

o Group A and B should have roughly equal men and women o Group A and B should have roughly equal teens and seniors o …etc.
- The only significant difference between group A and B is the AI assistant’s style – hence this is the only factor that could account for the observed outcome difference.
- Consider why randomization is so important here.

o Let’s say that we allowed users to choose which style agent they prefer (concise vs. chatty). If we

observed the same result, could we make the same conclusion? No!

For these reasons, A/B tests (otherwise known as randomized control trials), are the gold standard for making causal claims.
