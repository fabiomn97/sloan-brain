## Page 10

Four kinds of obstacles

- Can’t randomize — the treatment is not something you can assign

- Shouldn’t randomize — ethics and the law say we can’t

- Won’t randomize — the organization won’t for financial reasons

- Already didn’t randomize — we want to extract insight from past data

## Page 11

Can’t   ·   Shouldn’t   ·   Won’t   ·   Already didn’t

- Many business “treatments” aren’t randomly assignable at the customer level, especially in bricks-and-mortar e.g., a new logo, a new return policy, revised product packaging

- Test and Control units interfere with each other o If you discount some riders in a marketplace, that will attract other drivers away

from riders in the control group. o Other examples: Merchants that “copy” the treatment recommendations to the

control group, sales reps who share treatment coupons with control customers, shared accounts

## Page 12

Can’t   ·   Shouldn’t   ·   Won’t   ·   Already didn’t

- Withholding a treatment that works. If a new cancer drug extends survival, the placebo arm cannot ethically continue.*

- Differential treatment may cause legal exposure. Randomly assigning different prices or different credit terms can be discriminatory

- Customers compare notes. Brand damage from a potential customer backlash.

*Which is why trials carry stopping rules and a data safety monitoring board.

## Page 13

Can’t   ·   Shouldn’t   ·   Won’t   ·   Already didn’t

- Forgone revenue from the control group. Assigning 10% of customers to the control group in a campaign that is expected to be profitable may face opposition from sales and marketing

- Decision and outcome timelines may differ widely. Churn and lifetime value take a year to measure, while decisions are made weekly

## Page 14

Can’t   ·   Shouldn’t   ·   Won’t   ·   Already didn’t

- The data has already been collected. “Did last quarter’s campaign work?”

- No experiment is possible, even in principle. You cannot go back and re-run last year with a control group, since business conditions have changed

## Page 34

Confounding occurs when
- subjects self-select into groups or get assigned to groups based on non-random factors and
- these factors influence the outcome metric the subjects are compared on.

Think of a confounder as a common cause of both how subjects end up in groups and how the groups fare on the outcome of interest.

Linear Regression for Controlling Confounders

- Set up and solve a regression with

o Dependent variable = Outcome o Independent variables = Treatment, all confounders

- The coefficient of the Treatment variable is the causal effect of the Treatment on the Outcome, after controlling for all the confounders.

o Jargon: This is called the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) in the literature.

## Page 76

Linear Regression for Causal Inference – Pros and Cons

- Linear regression is a convenient shortcut for the manual comparison we did. Each coefficient measures the effect of that variable with all the other variables held fixed. o With route in the model, the airline coefficient answers: "On the same route, how

much more likely is a Quanta flight to be delayed?" o It handles many confounders, including numerical ones, in a single model.

- Regression doesn't create data you don't have. When groups are sparse, it fills the gaps by assuming each variable's effect simply adds up (a straight-line, no-interaction model). That assumption is what makes the shortcut work, and it isn't always true.

- And it can only control for confounders you include. It can't adjust for variables you never measured.

People who meditate regularly are X% less likely to get heart disease than those who don’t.

Possible confounding due to exercise and diet: people who exercise and follow healthier diets => more likely to be in the meditation group and less prone to heart disease.

Possible confounding due to expertise level: newer technicians => more likely to try the new devices and may take longer to do an ultrasound in any device.

A hospital looking to replace its ultrasound machines runs a test and discovers that the new device is taking X% longer to use than the old one.

Website visitors who clicked on product recommendations spent X% more per visitor than visitors who didn’t.

Possible confounding due to loyalty: Loyal customers may visit the site often, explore the site more and click on product recommendations, and spend more when they buy etc. Thus, loyalty can influence both their propensity to click on a recommendation and their spend.

## Page 101

Strictly speaking, this is not an example of Simpson's Paradox. While confounding inflated the true causal effect by 3.7x, it did not reverse it (i.e., recommendations did not lower the purchase rate).

Confounding may or may not lead to Simpson's Paradox. Just think of Simpson's Paradox as an extreme example of confounding at work.

## Page 103

Limitations of working with observational data

- When working with observational data, we can never be sure that we have captured all confounders. This is ultimately a leap of faith based on domain knowledge.

- The confounders we work with may be crude proxies for what we actually care about (e.g., loyalty status and traffic source are proxies for purchase intent)

- A visitor who arrives already intending to buy a specific item is both more likely to click a recommendation and more likely to purchase — and neither loyalty nor traffic fully capture that intent.
