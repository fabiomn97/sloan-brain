NETWORK EXTERNALITIES

Network externality The value of a product increases with the number of users.

Direct The more people use a platform, the more valuable it is to other users. E.g. WhatsApp, Instagram, Zoom, Nasdaq.

Indirect The more people use a product, the more investment there is in complementary goods. A two-sided market, where two groups meet through a platform, is an indirect externality. E.g. iOS and Android (apps), Uber (drivers), Spotify (artists), Visa (merchants).

- Generate barriers to entry, and so market power.

- Feedback loop: exponential growth, or exponential decline.

- Difficult to start, and difficult to maintain.

- Make demand more elastic ➜an aggressive pricing strategy becomes more profitable.

LEARNING CURVES

Learning (experience) curve As experience increases, unit cost goes down (or quality goes up). Usually stated as the % change in cost when cumulative experience doubles.

- Manufacturing. Ford’s Model T: cut the price, sell more, move down the curve. In a modern assembly plant, defect rates fall sharply with cumulative output.

- Google Search. “The source of Google’s competitive advantage is learning by doing” (Hal Varian, former Google chief economist). More searches ➜more data on what people click and skip ➜better ranking.

LECTURE 5 Electricity Markets Investment Case

THE LAB

The goal of the lab is to make an investment decision for a data center. Since electricity is the main variable input, we use supply, demand and equilibrium prices to predict electricity prices in New England.

TAKEAWAYS

1. The profitability of capital investments that rely on electricity as the main input depends on future electricity prices, which in turn depend on supply and demand in hourly electricity markets. We used the supply curve and demand to predict the future electricity cost of a potential data center investment.

2. Supply curves in electricity represent the marginal cost (MC) of each power plant—natural gas, nuclear, solar, wind, biofuels, etc. Technologies of power plants differ in both marginal cost and capacity factors. Solar and wind have zero marginal cost, but capacity varies by hour (solar disappears at night; wind varies by weather). Natural gas plants have higher marginal cost, tied to natural gas prices and therefore more volatile. In New England natural gas power plants tend to be marginal, so electricity prices closely follow natural gas prices.

3. Supply curves shift over time due to entry of new power plants (e.g., planned solar/wind farms) and changes in fuel prices. Additional renewable capacity shifts supply to the right. But because the natural gas section of the supply curve is flat, price changes can be small even when capacity expands.

4. New demand moves the price by the local slope of supply. The data center draws 500,000 GPUs × 1,500 W = 750 MW. By day that lands on the flat natural gas section and moves the price about 16 cents; at night the same 750 MW lands on a steep section and moves it $2.88.

5. Forecasting the electricity bill for the data center requires assumptions about:
- future demand growth,
- future entry and retirement of plants,
- completion/cancellation rates of planned projects, and
- future natural gas prices.

6. We learned that the projections can change significantly under different assumptions.

7. Lessons from the lab: the decision is not sensitive to the price of electricity: New England has enough generation to power the data center under many scenarios. The revenue side (rental price, utilisation, GPU life) moves the answer far more. There is a lot of uncertainty in every one of these numbers.

Remember, the point of the lab is not to compute a single “correct” number, but to become comfortable using supply-demand analysis to think through long-lived energy investments under uncertainty.

Market segmentation is just one instance of price discrimination. By charging different prices to segments of the market with different elasticities, you can increase profit. LECTURE 7 Consumer Self-Selection and Two-Part Tariffs

MARKET SEGMENTATION VS SELF-SELECTION

Market segmentation Firms identify and categorize consumers on specific characteristics (age, income, country) and charge different segments differently.

Consumer self-selection The firm does not know the consumer’s WTP. It offers a range of prices and products, and consumers choose the option that is best for them. E.g. coupons and rebates: only price-sensitive consumers bother to collect. Consumers think strategically, so they make the choices that maximize their own surplus. Firms must respond strategically: given that behaviour, which pricing policy best captures value?

LECTURE 8 Menus and Product Portfolios

KEY TERMS

Product portfolio Firms offer a menu of products that vary in quality or convenience, and consumers choose which version to buy. The goal is to structure the menu so that it charges higher prices to high-WTP consumers and lower prices to low-WTP consumers, extracting more surplus than a single price could. E.g. iPhone models, Netflix plans, Tesla ranges, a laptop with more memory or less.

Consumer self-selection Consumers are strategic: each chooses the product that gives them the most surplus, so the menu has to be designed around how they will react to it.

THE DESIGN PROBLEM

The premium model should go to the high-WTP consumer, the base to the low-WTP consumer. The base cannibalizes the premium one if it is too good and too cheap, so how bad should it be?

THE RECIPE

For any portfolio of products and prices:

- For each consumer type, compute the consumer surplus from each choice.

- Work out the profit from each type buying its preferred choice, multiply by the number of consumers of that type, and add up across types.

-  Check that consumers are buying the product you are targeting them with. If not, adjust the portfolio or the prices and redo the steps; if yes, see whether prices can be raised without breaking the targeting.

Work the recipe through the simplest case: two consumer types, H with high WTP and L with low, and two versions

of the product, costs ignored. Either type can buy either version.

QUALITY DISTORTION

The quality of the base model is itself a choice. Lowering it reduces what the low-WTP consumer will pay for it, but raises what the high-WTP consumer will pay to upgrade, and the second effect can dominate: in the lab, degrading the base model raised revenue. Product-line quality is a strategic tool, not just an engineering decision.

Business history has many examples: 19th-century railways removed the roof from second class so first class could charge more, HP shipped a printer with friction added to the same motor to make it slower, and Tesla limited battery range in software.
