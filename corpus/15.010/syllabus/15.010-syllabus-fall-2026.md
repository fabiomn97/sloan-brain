Overview

15.010 is Sloan’s core subject in microeconomics. Economics provides a framework to understand how businesses create value, capture value, as well as how they compete and cooperate with one another. The basic framework studies what happens if firms maximize profits: this framework both explains and informs the behavior of suppliers, consumers in business-to-business transactions, and competing firms. We begin with how firms set prices when they have market power. We then consider markets, including an introduction to game theory to understand the strategic interactions between and within firms.

Resources

The assigned readings in this course provide a balance of principles, tools, and applications; they are detailed in the syllabus below. They constitute valuable background material, especially for those who have less experience with economics courses.

A key feature of the course is the discussion of current events and real-world examples in class. On Canvas, you will find newspaper article readings assigned for each lecture, along with suggested discussion questions to facilitate active participation in class.

The main textbook for the course is Bernheim and Whinston’s Microeconomics, 2nd edition (referred to as BW below). We created an electronic version of Bernheim and Whinston with selected chapters. The title of the e-book is “Economic Analysis for Business Decisions.” The ISBN is 9781308864549, and it is available at https://create.mheducation.com/shop/.

We also have a digital course pack with selected readings found at Study.net. This provides additional textbook readings from Pindyck and Rubinfeld’s Microeconomics (referred to as PR below) along with other readings and a few cases.

Students may also refer to the (optional) 15.010 MIT MBA Economics Review Module, which is available online on MITx for background on the basic principles of microeconomics. Finally, we will provide (optional) practice problems after each lecture, which allow students to reinforce their learning of the class material.

Flipped classrooms: We developed a set of lectures that “flip the classroom”: a combination of pre-class videos and imbedded concept-check quizzes to introduce students to new content prior to a lecture session. During the lecture session, faculty will typically lead a discussion and/or an in-class lab to draw out the lessons. Students will complete these “labs” in small groups.

On the use of calculus: Economics uses calculus to develop and understand the underlying framework that forms the intellectual foundation of managerial economics. That said, the use of calculus in nearly all business settings is a non-starter. As a result, the course will discuss concepts using calculus, but in the end our goal is to develop fundamental economic principles, and your intuition, using real-world examples and current events. As a result, project and exam questions and solutions will not require the use of calculus.

Generative AI: This course permits the use of generative AI tools on assignments. Unless stated otherwise for a given task, the default is that AI use is allowed. Where an assignment restricts or prohibits it, that will be stated on the assignment itself.

POLICY ON PROJECTS AND EXAMINATIONS

1.Projects help you learn how to apply the material presented in lectures and recitations. You are encouraged to discuss course material, including projects, with other students in the class. Projects are graded, and your performance on them counts toward your final grade. Each project must be written in your own words and submitted on time. We strongly believe that working through the projects yourself is an essential part of the learning experience.

2.In fairness to students who complete assignments on time, late projects will not be accepted for credit. Please submit your projects on Canvas (i.e., please do not email it to your TA).

3.Examinations. We do not want exams to be a memorization exercise, so we allow students to bring an 8.5x11 inch sheet of paper with notes: one-sided for the midterm and both sides for the final exam. During exams, you may consult the proctor administering the exam if you need clarification of exam questions. No discussion or other form of communication with anyone else will be permitted after the exams have been distributed. If your calculator has memory to save information, you need to clear it before the exam. Students found to have cheated or engaged in any other unethical behavior will be given a grade of F on the examinations involved and will be turned over to the appropriate disciplinary committees within MIT for further action. If applicable, students in one section should not discuss an exam until students in all sections have completed the exam. Students arriving late will not be given extra time. Make-up examinations are only considered for hardship cases such as a family medical problem.

4.Re-grade Policy. Requests for re-grading an exam must be made within one week of the date upon which the graded examination is returned to the student. To request a re-grade, attach a written explanation of the potential problem with the grading and submit it to your TA. Re-grading may not be limited to the part of the exam that is discussed in the written explanation. Re-grades will be carried out by the 15.010 faculty and TA team.

5.Unless explicitly mentioned in the project, the use of AI is not permitted.

If you have questions about these policies, please raise them with the faculty members or TAs.
