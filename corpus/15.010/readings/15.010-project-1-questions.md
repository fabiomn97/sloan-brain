BACKGROUND

You are considering building a data center in New England. This project works up to that decision using

real-world data: you will build the region’s electricity supply curve, see what sets the price in a given hour, and then ask what a facility of this size would pay for power and whether the project is worth building. We have provided you with data on all power plants in New England.

A power plant, also known as a power station or generating station, is a facility that converts various forms of energy into electricity. US electricity is predominantly generated using six different technologies: nuclear power, coal generation, natural gas generation, solar, wind, and hydroelectric facilities. That is the national picture. New England has no coal plants, and the data below also covers oil, biofuels and waste, so eight fuel types appear in this assignment. Within each of them there are also differences in the details and the efficiency with which they convert energy into electricity. Use AI to familiarize yourself with how these technologies work (at a high level) and the differences within each broad category.

Power markets are quite complex, so we will make simplifying assumptions to make problems manageable. We will assume, on average, 12 hours of daylight and 12 hours of nighttime. Solar panels can generate

electricity at full capacity during the daytime. We will also ignore seasonal variations in electricity demand and assume that the demand is constant throughout the year. You can make other simplifying assumptions to solve these questions.

THE DATA

We acquired the following two datasets from Velocity Suite, a commercial data provider for electricity

markets, and have provided them to you.

DATASET 1

operating_plants_marginal_cost.csv

Marginal cost, capacity, and fuel type of all 1,164 power plants operating in New England in December 2025.

VARIABLE UNITS DESCRIPTION

plant_name — name of the plant

fuel_type — fuel input of the power plant. The values in the file are lower case: biofuels, natural gas, nuclear, oil, solar, waste, water, wind

capacity MW nameplate capacity: a 100 MW plant produces 100 MWh in an hour

capacity_factor percent the most a plant can produce relative to its capacity, on a 0–100 scale. It is 100 for gas, oil, biofuels, waste and nuclear, which can run at any time, and usually well below that for wind and water, which cannot. For solar this field is not a number: it holds the text Depends on Time of Day

fuel_cost_per_mwh $/MWh fuel cost to generate one megawatt-hour. Plants also have a small non-fuel variable cost, which we ignore throughout

DATASET 2

planned_plants_capacity.csv

The 336 power plants under construction in New England as of August 2026.

VARIABLE UNITS DESCRIPTION

plant_name — name of the plant

fuel_type — fuel input of the power plant, lower case as above. Every plant in this file is solar, water or wind

expected_online_year year the year the plant is expected to come online and start generating. Assume it is operational on the first day of that year

capacity MW nameplate capacity, as above

QUESTIONS

1. For each of the eight fuel types in the data, provide a few sentences on what sorts of costs would enter into an estimate of marginal cost. Is there anything unique about any of them?

2. For each of the same eight fuel types, provide a few sentences on the nature of capacity. For example, how is the capacity of solar changing over time? Is the same true for a natural gas power plant?

3. Construct a supply curve using the plants in New England during daytime hours and nighttime. How would solar supply change daytime and nighttime hours?

4. Suppose the hourly demand for electricity is perfectly inelastic and is equal to 17,000 MW during the daytime and 9,000 MW during the nighttime. What are the daytime and nighttime electricity prices in New England in 2025? What are the fuel types of marginal plants during daytime and nighttime?

5. Using the provided data, construct the supply curve in 2031 for New England during a daytime hour. What assumptions did you make to do so?

6. Suppose the daytime demand for electricity is perfectly inelastic and is equal to 20,000 MW. What is the change in daytime price from using the 2025 supply curve compared to the 2031 supply curve?

7. You are a developer planning a data center in New England to serve AI inference. The facility will house 500,000 NVIDIA H100 GPUs and comes online in 2027. Table 1 gives what is known about the project.

(a) How much electricity does this facility consume in an hour, and over a full year?

(b) Using the supply curve you build for 2027, what price will the data center pay for electricity in

a daytime hour, and in a nighttime hour? Does connecting this facility change the price that everyone else in New England pays? Figure 1 gives twenty years of New England demand if you want to project it forward.

(c) What is the data center’s annual electricity bill?

(d) Based on your analysis, would you recommend building this data center in New England? You

will have to take a stand on things Table 1 does not give you: how much of its capacity it actually sells, what it earns per GPU-hour, and how long it operates. Figure 2 gives the market price of renting one H100 for one hour, in dollars per GPU-hour, for every month from January 2025 to July 2026; use it to build your revenue assumption. State your assumptions and say why you chose them. Which of these assumptions matters most to your answer, and how would you go about pinning it down?

(e) Build an interactive dashboard for this decision. A user should be able to vary the assumptions

that matter — the number of GPUs, how much of its capacity it sells, the rental price per GPU-hour, the price of electricity, and how long the project lasts — and see whether the project should be built, along with the numbers behind that verdict. Use an AI coding assistant. Submit your dashboard as an HTML file with your slides.
