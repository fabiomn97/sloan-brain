3. COST CONCEPTS

a. Define: sunk cost; opportunity cost.

Sunk cost: a cost that must be incurred regardless of the decision being made. As a result, think of these costs as permanently lost and unrecoverable.

Opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative foregone in taking a decision. For example, an unused apartment or office space has an opportunity cost of potential rental income.

b. What is the difference between economic cost and accounting expense?

Opportunity cost (the value of the best alternative foregone) is the key distinction between economic cost and accounting expense.

There is a famous story of T. Boone Pickens purchasing gas stations at several times the price of their book value, which made others skeptical. But the actual value of the land was worth more: the opportunity cost of using the land for gas stations was that it could be converted into high-rise buildings. That opportunity cost is included in economic costs.

Opportunity costs drive business decision-making, and for that reason economic costs need to be considered to make the right business decisions.

i. What costs are fixed?

Fixed costs do not vary with output. Here rent (85,296) and leases (31,016) are identical in every quarter while quantity nearly doubles, from 45,613 to 88,646. On the evidence in the table, those are the fixed costs.

However, these do not have to be fixed costs in all circumstances. They are only likely to be fixed, and the reason is the contract behind them. Rent and leases are usually long-term commitments that cannot be cancelled without a penalty, so if the firm cut its output tomorrow it would still owe the same amount.

Change the contract and the answer changes. If the rent were renewed month to month, a firm that wanted to cut production could simply stop renewing; the cost would fall away with the output, and rent would be a variable cost. The six numbers in the table would look exactly the same.

So fixed versus variable is a question about the contract and the time horizon, not only about whether the number moves in the data.

ii. What costs are variable?

Variable costs vary with output. Here labor, energy, materials and transportation vary by quarter, and it is reasonable to assume that they vary with output.

The same caution applies in reverse: these could be fixed in other circumstances. If the workforce is unionized, for example, and workers cannot be laid off without a significant cost, then labor is mostly fixed in the short run. Output falls but the wage bill does not.

Again it is the contract and the time horizon that decide, not the column of numbers.
