BACKGROUND

You are an entrepreneur with an idea for a brand-new product. Before you launch it you need to decide what to charge, and to do that you need to know what demand looks like and what it costs you to make and sell the product. The problem is that the product does not exist yet, so there are no sales data to learn your demand curve from.

A large company would answer this question with market research: surveys, focus groups, conjoint studies and test markets. These are slow and expensive, often costing tens of thousands of dollars for a single study. As a startup with no money, you cannot afford them.

A new approach is to use large language models (LLMs) to simulate consumers. You give the model a person to simulate (their age, country, income, household and so on), describe your product, and ask what that person would pay for it. Repeat this for hundreds of different simulated people and you have a synthetic market survey, in minutes and for a few dollars.

This idea has moved quickly from research into practice. Researchers have found that LLMs reproduce many well-known patterns in human economic behavior (Horton, Filippas and Manning 2023), that simulated samples can match the survey responses of real demographic groups (Argyle et al. 2023), and that they can reproduce consumers’ stated purchase intent for real products (Maier et al. 2025). Willingness-to-pay estimates from LLMs are sometimes close to those from studies with real people, but they can also be far off, and occasionally even have the wrong sign (Brand, Israeli and Ngwe 2023). QUESTIONS

1. Your product. Suppose you are an entrepreneur and want to launch a new product. Which product would you launch? Choose a product that would have some market power, so that you will have some power to set your own price.

2. Market power. What are your sources of market power, and how long do you expect them to last? Why does market power matter for the pricing exercise in the rest of this project?

3. Two segments. Think of two segments of the market to which you might want to charge different prices. What are these two segments, and why do you expect their willingness to pay to differ? (For this project, do not worry about your ability to charge different prices to these segments. We will assume that you can.)

4. Simulate your customers. Go to 15010.ai/p2-instructions and follow the instructions there. You will describe your product and your two segments, and the platform will simulate 500 consumers, 250 from each segment, and ask each of them the most they would be willing to pay for your product. Download the resulting spreadsheet and submit it with your answers. (We assume that each consumer buys at most one unit. The time period this covers depends on the product: for coffee it could be one cup a day, and for a car one car every five years.)

A note of caution. Because this is a live demonstration of these tools, we cannot guarantee what your

demand curve will look like. It may come out unrealistic, for example with an unusual shape or with outlier values. In our own testing across different products it worked well, but if this happens to you, simply run it again with a new product. In that case, describe what you observed and explain why that demand curve is not usable.

7. Pricing. Now you will find the optimal prices. Use the linear approximation of each segment’s demand curve, which is given in the spreadsheet from Question 4, together with the marginal cost (MC) you estimated in Question 6. Assume you charge a single price per unit (no two-part tariffs, bundles or quantity discounts) and that direct competition will not be a problem for a while.

(a) Suppose you can charge each segment its own price. What is the profit-maximizing price in each
