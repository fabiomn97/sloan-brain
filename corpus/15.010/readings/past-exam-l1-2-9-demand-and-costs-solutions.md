1. 2025_midterm: Video game console demand

A recent review of video game console sales revealed that, despite the new generation of consoles being more expensive than their predecessors at launch, demand for them is higher than ever. In a blog post, a video game analyst concludes that “demand for video games is clearly upwards sloping; video games have become luxury goods.” Is the analyst correct in

concluding this from the data?

SOLUTION

False. The demand curve might have shifted given the release of the new consoles. WTP may be higher for the new consoles because of non-price factors such as higher quality, or because of a change in the composition of consumers purchasing them. If so, what the

analyst is observing is movement across different demand curves rather than along one demand curve. (Also, “luxury good” refers to how demand responds to income, not to an upward-sloping demand curve.) (Note: we will also accept Uncertain as the answer if followed with the above logic).

2. 2025_midterm: Startup acquisition sunk cost

Suppose you paid $1 million to acquire a new software startup. Soon after, a competitor with a superior product enters the market. At this point, should your willingness to pay for the competitor fall by $1 million compared with the case where you had not made the first

acquisition? Assume you do not face financing constraints.

SOLUTION

False. The $1 million is sunk (will not be recovered regardless of whether you acquire this competitor or not). Your willingness to pay to acquire this competitor should be the expected profit (net value) you think it can generate.

Following a major public bug in their cybersecurity software, PublicSlap®, the owning

company, decided to slash prices by 50%. An external analyst notes that the demand for PublicSlap® has barely changed and concludes that demand for cybersecurity software must be extremely inelastic. Is the analyst correct?

SOLUTION

False. The public bug likely affected the reputation and perception of quality of the software, lowering WTP, and contracting the demand. The reduction in prices might have

exactly offset this change, leading to the same demand level. The analyst is mixing demand shifts and movements along the demand curve.

4. 2023_midterm: Why randomized experiments?

Answer in 3–4 sentences.

Why are Randomized experiments considered a gold standard for demand estimation?

SOLUTION

The primary reason randomized experiments are considered the gold standard for demand estimation is their ability to establish causality. In observational data, prices are not set at random: firms change prices when demand shifts (seasons, promotions, competitors), so the price-quantity points mix movements along the demand curve with

shifts of it, and the relationship you see is not the demand curve. In a randomized experiment, subjects are randomly allocated to different prices, so price changes are unrelated to anything else that moves demand. This allows us to attribute the change in demand to the price change rather than to some other confounding variable.

5. 2023_midterm: Refinery oil in storage

True, False, or Uncertain? Explain in 2–3 sentences.

You’re an oil refinery (i.e., you turn oil into refined products such as gasoline) and have a large amount of oil storage on site. When you filled up your storage tanks you paid $80 for the oil.

When determining your profit maximizing price for your refined products, you should calculate

marginal costs based on an oil price of $80.

SOLUTION

FALSE: The relevant price of oil to use when determining your marginal costs is the

opportunity cost of your oil in storage. Because you can always sell that oil at the current price for oil, the relevant oil price, therefore, is the current price of oil. (Note: we would also accept uncertain since the current price of oil is not given and it may also be $80.)

6. 2023_midterm: Star Wars waffle makers

Suppose you have obtained an exclusive license from Disney to produce Star Wars-themed waffle makers. The licensing deal consists of an annual fee of $500,000 and a per-unit royalty fee of $15 paid to Disney for every waffle-maker sold. Now, Disney is raising the annual fee to $750,000 and your CFO comes to you and says, “Even with the new licensing deal, I can see that we remain

profitable. However, we should raise the price of the waffle makers to cover some of these additional costs.”

a) True, False, or Uncertain? Explain in 2–3 sentences.

Your CFO is correct.


Your CFO’s statement is false. The annual fee is a fixed cost and thus has no bearing on

the optimal price of the waffle makers, provided that you still want to remain in business. The optimal price is given by the markup formula (or, equivalently, MR=MC condition).

b) Answer in 3–4 sentences.

Would your response be different if instead Disney was raising the royalty rate from $15 to $20 per waffle maker sold?


Yes, the answer is different. The royalty is paid on every unit sold, so it is part of your

marginal cost: raising it from $15 to $20 raises MC by $5. By MR = MC (equivalently, the markup rule), a higher marginal cost raises the profit-maximizing price, so you should raise the price of the waffle makers. This is unlike the annual fee in part (a), which is fixed and does not affect the optimal price.

7. 2022_midterm: Short-answer concepts

Answer each in 2–4 sentences.

a) You are a venture capitalist and get a pitch from a company that is currently unprofitable. The CEO says you should invest in them because they have economies of scale. What data would

you ask to see to evaluate these claims, and how would you use the data?


Economies of scale exist when your average costs are falling with output. To verify this claim I would ask for total costs and output at several different output levels (for example over time as the company grew, or across its plants or markets), compute average cost = total cost / output at each level, and check whether average cost falls as output rises.

## Page 4

Current losses are not evidence against economies of scale if average cost is falling and would drop below the price at a larger scale.

b) Your team has spent $2m each month in developing a new drug. After 12 months, the lead scientist discovers from a new scientific result that the chance of a successful development

has fallen by 80%. During a meeting, the lead scientist argues that “we’ve put so much effort and $24m into this; it would be a shame to walk away now. We should continue the development project.” Evaluate your colleague’s reasoning.


Answer: the effort and $24m are sunk. They should not enter your decision to keep going. The decision should compare the remaining development costs with the expected payoff

from continuing, and that expected payoff has just fallen sharply because the chance of success fell by 80%. This makes continuing much less attractive.

Partial credit: if the effort and $24m mean that the next investment is poised to be successful, then it could make sense to keep going. (Note: this is not due to the shame of wasting the money, rather it is that the productivity of the next investment may be high.

That is you learned from spending the 24m.)

8. 2022_midterm: True/False/Uncertain

True, False, or Uncertain? Explain in 1–3 sentences.

a) Consider a low-budget hotel chain. As a consultant you see their data and find that when

price is higher than average, quantity is lower than average. This means that the data is suitable to estimate a demand curve.


False: price and quantity moving in opposite directions in the data does not mean the data trace out the demand curve. Prices may be high exactly when demand shifts (for example in different seasons), so the observed points mix movements along demand with

shifts of it. To estimate demand you need price changes that are unrelated to demand shocks, such as cost changes or a price experiment.

10. 2021_midterm: Short-answer concepts

Answer each in 1–3 sentences.

a) Define Opportunity Cost and give an example.


Opportunity Cost is the highest valued alternative use of an input; an example is “no such thing as a free lunch”: you could do something else with your time.

b) Define Economies of Scale and provide two (2) reasons why a firm might have economies of scale.


Economies of scale mean that average cost falls as output rises. Reasons include:

fixed costs spread over more units

specialization or different technology at larger scale bargaining power that lowers input prices learning and process improvements: a firm that produces more gets better at it and its unit costs fall (strictly, this depends on cumulative output, so the savings build

up over time)

Some students listed network externalities as a source of economies of scale. Remember that “economies” means cost reduction. Network externalities help increase revenue by bringing more people to the platform, rather than a source of lower costs. One argument for lower costs would be through lower customer-acquisition costs due to network

externalities.

14. 2018_midterm: Carbon tax and movie popcorn

Answer each with a brief but clear explanation.

a) “You are considering going to watch a movie on a Saturday night, and you love popcorn at the movies: your willingness to pay for watching a movie with a bag of popcorn is $18; and your

willingness to pay for a movie without popcorn is $5. The movie ticket costs $10 and the popcorn is also $10.”

Use notions of economic costs to make the following decisions:

Should you go to the movies?


NO because going with popcorn yields a payoff of 18-20 < 0 and going without yields 5-10<0.

b) If your friends force you to go to the movies, should you buy the popcorn once you are there?


YES because the marginal cost of the popcorn is $10 (the ticket price is sunk) and the marginal benefit of popcorn is “Movie with” – “Movie without” = 18 – 5 = $ 13.

Main lesson: When ticket cost is sunk, Willingness to pay for popcorn should be

compared to the cost of popcorn. Willingness to pay for popcorn can be calculated as “Willingness to pay for popcorn and movie” – Willingness to pay for the movie.

Not “WTP movie and popcorn” compared to cost of popcorn. Not “WTP movie and popcorn – cost of ticket” compared to cost of popcorn

15. 2018_midterm: R&D, elasticity, and resale

True, False, or Uncertain? Explain briefly.

a) “An industry with high fixed costs of research and development experiences significant economies of scale”

## Page 9

True: spreading out large fixed costs is a source of economies of scale.

Also acceptable is “uncertain:” whether the industry has economies of scale at all production levels will depend on the variable cost technology too.

The most common mistake was to consider the R&D investment cost as a sunk cost and not factor it in when determining economies of scale. It is true that, once sunk, R&D

expenditures do not affect supply decisions (which instead depend on marginal cost), but economies of scale = “Total Average Cost is decreasing,” which affects total profits.
